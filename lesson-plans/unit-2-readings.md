## hyer, "tonality" (pp. 726–52) · c. 1600 to 1910, the term from 1810

A revised version of Hyer's article for the second edition of *New Grove* (p. 726). Five sections: Usage, Rhetoric, Theory, Practice, Historiography.

**What the chapter claims**

- "Tonality" is a nineteenth-century term applied after the fact. Choron coined *tonalité* in 1810 for the arrangement of dominant and subdominant above and below the tonic, to separate *tonalité moderne* from *tonalité antique*. Fétis popularized it in the 1830s and 1840s (pp. 726, 728–30).
- The chapter's working sense is the arrangement of musical phenomena around a referential tonic in European music from about 1600 to about 1910, in two genera, major and minor (p. 728).
- The history of tonality is better told as specific harmonic practices than as immutable laws. From the start the term has been put to historiographical and ideological use (pp. 746–47, 750).
- A recurrent tension: whether tonality is a property of the music or of the listener's cognition, "a form of consciousness" in Norton's phrase (p. 727).

**The eight usages (pp. 726–28)**

1. Adjective: any systematic organization of pitch, Western or not. Church modes, slendro and pelog, maqām, raga, Rameau's tonic-dominant-subdominant constellation, Weber's paired scales, the 144 row transformations (Perle's "twelve-tone tonalities").
2. Noun: a self-contained "sistême musical" (Rousseau), often hypostatized into a Platonic form prior to any music. Sainsbury's 1825 translation of Choron first renders *tonalité* as "system of modes."
3. "Tonal" against "modal" (before 1600) and "atonal" (after 1910), implying discontinuity.
4. Premodern music called tonal on the grounds of (1), assuming continuity across 1600: *tonalité ancienne* and *moderne* differ in emphasis, not kind.
5. Phenomena arranged in relation to a referential tonic, which gives the music its "C-ness."
6. Psychophysical: phenomena perceived through the categories of tonal theory, 440 Hz heard as 2̂ in G major.
7. A synonym for "key." The concept reaches past a key's pitch-class content to the relations governing it: "keyness."
8. The arrangement around a tonic in European music from c. 1600 to c. 1910. This is the chapter's concern.

**Rhetoric (pp. 728–33)**

- Fétis: tonalité is "the collection of necessary relations, both successive and simultaneous, between the notes of the scale," figured as attraction. The "minor fifth" 4̂–7̂ is an "appellative consonance": 4̂ strives to 3̂, 7̂ to 1̂. The dominant seventh is only the usual support for that dyad, not an essential scale degree.
- Fétis dated *tonalité moderne* to Monteverdi: first *Stracciami pur il core* (1592), where the dominant seventh doesn't resolve over a change of bass, then *Cruda Amarilli* (1605), mm. 13–14, an unprepared V7 over G to C. Hyer calls the claim's validity "arguable" and the date and place "firm musicological lore" (p. 729).
- Most of this is Choron's (1810): Monteverdi invented the dominant seventh around 1590, first used it unprepared, and first treated the minor fifth as a consonance. The term also appears in Castil-Blaze (1821), Geslin (1826), and Jelensperger (1830) before Fétis (pp. 729–30).
- Tonal forces resist causal explanation and generate metaphor. Hyer's kinds, with his examples: gravitational (Rameau); animistic (the *note sensible*, Cowell's "musical homing instinct," Schoenberg's "instinctual lives of tones"); erotic (Rameau's tonic, "to whom all our wishes tend"); olfactory (d'Alembert: "the sourness of the dominant desires the sweetness of the tonic"); political (Schoenberg's tonic as sovereign and dominant as vassal, Momigny's tonic as queen, Riepel's 1755 farm with C major as master, G major overseer, A minor head maid, F major day laborer, E minor chamber maid, D minor errand girl) (pp. 730–32).
- Spatial metaphors: Marpurg was the first to call the tonic "home"; Helmholtz's *Schwerpunkt*; Tovey's vanishing point of linear perspective; Momigny's planetary orbits; Schoenberg's "remote regions"; the container ("in C major") with a diatonic inside and a chromatic outside (pp. 732–33).
- In actual music the tonic is a conclusion, not a center, and the dominant fundamental often lies a fourth below it (p. 732).
- The "laws" of tonality are social in basis. Hyer asserts a strong correlation between tonal theories and conservative political ideologies (p. 732).

**Theory (pp. 733–38)**

- Two traditions: function theories (Rameau, Riemann) and scale-degree theories (Weber, Schenker), with Fétis a hybrid. Both posit an ideational content, a tonic or a scale, and use a metalanguage of labels or Roman numerals.
- Rameau, *Génération harmonique* (1737): the "mode" is tonic, dominant, and subdominant, related by the imperfect and perfect cadences. The added sixth on the subdominant and the seventh on the dominant both resolve to the tonic's third, which decides the mode.
- Dahlhaus extends harmonic succession to intervals and so locates tonality's origins in Josquin and his contemporaries (p. 734).
- Weber numbers Stufen by pitch-class content. The recurrent problem is *Mehrdeutigkeit*: a C major triad can be I in C, IV in G, V in F, or VI in E minor (p. 735).
- Hyer proposes tonality as a *tertium quid* in which melody, harmony, and meter combine. Rameau knew the assertion of a tonic depended on the *mesure* (p. 735).
- Later: Hauptmann's F–a–C–e–G–b–D (1853), fifths interleaved with their thirds; Hostinský's Tonnetz (1879); Riemann's *Funktion* (1893) with Variant, Parallel, and Leittonwechsel; Schenker's mixture; Schoenberg's move from twelve major and twelve minor keys to twelve chromatic ones, "fully completed in the music of Wagner" (pp. 736–38).

**Practice (pp. 738–44)**

- Historians don't agree on how and when modal polyphony became Baroque tonality. Powers ("Is Mode Real?") argues the two coexist on separate epistemological planes, so no transition need be imagined. Hyer still registers a reduction from eight or more modes to two, a paired cantus durus and cantus mollis, completed in Mattheson's *Das neu-eröffnete Orchestre* (1713), which lists dur and moll on all twelve semitones (p. 738).
- Hybrids persist in Mattheson's contemporaries: Bach chorales give modal melodies dur or moll harmonizations (p. 738).
- The consensus: the major-minor ethos arrives with a simplified texture of harmonies over a basso continuo. Chords become discrete entities, and the *trias harmonica* determines medial cadences, taking precedence over the placement of semitones in the modal octave (pp. 738–39).
- The *clausula formalis* is reinterpreted as V–I: the bass G is the one note consonant with both D and the *subsemitonium* B. In German Baroque music this cadence becomes "a conventional gesture used to punctuate mosaic-like successions of phrases and ritornelli" (pp. 739–40).
- Rameau's theory mirrors French Baroque practice. Gravitational momentum is local, any triad without a dissonance is heard as a tonic, and modulations are additive chains of dominants (p. 740).
- Classical harmony: large-scale progressions toward "a distant but forehearable harmonic goal"; the dominant as dialectical opposite of the tonic (after Fichte and Hegel), with the reprise as synthesis; Subotnik's "musical equivalent of reason"; Schoenberg's monotonality; Schenker's tonicization, with his graph of Haydn's Hob. XVI:44 reading III at m. 13 as I in B♭ from m. 13 to m. 20 (pp. 740–41).
- Romantic and late Romantic: *Ganymed*, D. 544 (A♭ through C♭ to F); *Tristan* Act 3 (F minor to B major); *Götterdämmerung* (Bailey's associative tonality); Kurth's alteration style and "repression of the tonic"; Schoenberg's "floating tonality"; *Parsifal* Act 2, mm. 993–1001, with Adorno (pp. 741–44). These belong to unit 4 and to MUS 602.

**Historiography (pp. 745–50)**

- The standard story is evolutionary: tonality moves toward completion or a tragic demise, told as organic growth and decay.
- Hyer's three objections: it ignores mediation, since composers act knowingly to change or keep practice; it compresses contemporaneous diversity into one mainstream (Tovey's "the mainstream of music"); it privileges later practices as more advanced, Chopin progressive and Rakhmaninov regressive (pp. 745–46).
- Tonality never dissolved. To insist it did confuses a historical phenomenon with a cognitive one: tonal music is still what most people hear most of the time (p. 746).
- Before 1910 the concept had a modest provenance. Liszt was perhaps the first composer, besides Fétis, to write with awareness of it; almost all tonal music of the three previous centuries was written without reference to it (p. 746).
- Choron's cycle of stages: formation, development, progress toward perfection, permanence, decline. Fétis's four orders: *unitonique* (plainchant), *transitonique* (from Monteverdi, c. 1600), *pluritonique* (Mozart, Rossini), *omnitonique* (Berlioz, Wagner), the last heard as decadent (pp. 747–48).
- Fétis tied pitch repertoires to race through "cerebral conformation." Hyer places this within Orientalism (Said) and calls it part of tonality's genealogical heritage (pp. 748–49).
- Conservative ideologies use tonality to regulate practice and naturalize Western music; radical ones (Schoenberg) use evolution to make atonality its legitimate heir. Hyer's close: describe tonal music as contingent harmonic practices, not immutable laws (pp. 749–50).

**What unit 2 takes from it**

- Usage (3) against usage (4) frames Weeks 7 and 8. Burnett's chapter argues a continuity of system (eleven pitch classes, the missing pitch) across the modal-tonal boundary while dating key-centered tonality to the 1670s and 1680s.
- The Rhetoric section gives students categories for the metaphors in Burnett and Dreyfus.
- The *clausula formalis* paragraph (pp. 739–40) is the one place the chapter touches ritornello practice.

## burnett and nitzberg, chapter 4, "tonality and systems in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries" (pp. 89–153) · 1610 to the 1720s

The analytical spine of the unit. Seven sections: the introduction (Frescobaldi, Monteverdi), Marini, Legrenzi, Stradella's arias, Stradella's concerti grossi, Corelli, and the concerto from Torelli through Vivaldi to Bach.

**What the chapter claims**

- Key-centered tonality emerges in north Italian instrumental music (Rome, Venice, Bologna) in the 1670s and 1680s, under the influence of opera and cantata: a tonic triad prolonged over a whole movement by functionally significant auxiliary cadences (p. 89).
- Before that, instrumental composers reached areas outside the tonic only by shifting eleven-pitch-class systems. Cadences stayed on hexachordal pitches and returned straight to the tonic. Large-scale modulation needs a complete progression to stabilize the new key, which isn't fully established until ritornello form in the late 1680s (pp. 89–90).
- Modes are linear constructs. Harmonized, they let any central-hexachord degree carry a major or a minor triad, which yields cross relations and localized fifths motion and blocks tonal voice leading. Tonality needs a major dominant with the leading tone, itself prepared as the penultimate goal (p. 97).
- "Modality was never capable of sustaining or supporting a middleground" (p. 97). If modality can't rationalize the horizontal and the vertical, Schenkerian analysis of pre-tonal music "may exemplify musical-theoretical sophistry" (p. 98 n. 9).
- Corelli is "the first major composer to create a hierarchy of function between the fifths of the central hexachord of a key and the background tonic" (p. 132). His plan remains in effect, the chapter claims, until the late nineteenth century (p. 133).

**The system in tonal practice: what changes from chapter 3**

- System and key merge. A tonal signature governs two keys, a major tonic and its relative, in place of the eight or twelve modes of a modal gamut (pp. 117, 123).
- Every system is major. A minor key follows the hexachord of its relative major, "there being no such thing as an A minor hexachord" (pp. 117–18). Rameau's supplement to chapter 25 of the *Traité* (1722) begins all minor octaves on *la* (p. 117 n. 21).
- The Roman numeral under each hexachord pitch now names a fixed function. The 2♯ system G–D–A–e–b–f♯ reads IV I V ii vi iii in D major and VI III VII iv i v in B minor (pp. 118, 127).
- Degree inflection survives only as the raising of a minor hexachord triad to major for an applied dominant (II♯, III♯). It strengthens the goal where modal inflection weakened the tonic (pp. 123, 125).
- The missing pitch changes function. In modality, spelled as a flat, it's *fa* of the subdominant hexachord and moves the system a fifth down. In tonality, spelled as the minor third above the system root, it implies the parallel minor and becomes *do* of a new system three signatures flatward; spelled as an augmented second, it moves the system three signatures sharpward (pp. 118–19, 128). "Minor thirds are system roots; augmented seconds form tritones with system roots" (p. 128).
- Only one matrix exists at a time, and every harmonic event falls within the tritone axes of the tonic system. In an A-major movement C♯ minor belongs to the 6♯ system on F♯, not to a separate 4♯ system (p. 147).
- In minor, the leading tone of the dominant is the missing pitch of the relative major's system, so each tonicization of V shifts the system three signatures up. The shift is usually corrected at once (pp. 127, 141).
- The tonic is the missing pitch of the minor mediant's system, so the return to I after iii corrects the system with no preparatory dominant. Baroque concertos put a climactic ritornello in iii and revert to the tonic in the next measure (pp. 131, 148–49, 153).

**Before Corelli (pp. 89–122)**

- Frescobaldi, *Toccata ottava*: F mode in cantus durus, so naturalis. The passing E♭ in m. 1 turns F into V/IV and shifts to mollis; G♯ in m. 6 restores naturalis, uncontested to the end. The bass follows the fifths of the naturalis hexachord (A6–D–G–C from m. 2) toward D minor, and only the last measure redirects to F (pp. 90–91).
- Monteverdi, "Laetatus sum" (Vespers, 1610): transposed hypodorian on G in cantus mollis, made G minor by the written F♯s. The walking bass g–B♭ (via F)–D–G returns like a ritornello but is never transposed, and the relative major is heard only inside the tonic. The 116-measure movement is sectional, with no background prolongation (pp. 93–94). The chant enters in m. 3, in transposed B♭ hypodorian, and the continuo bass governs it: counterpoint "instrumentally conceived" (p. 93 n. 5).
- Marini, "La Foscarina" (1617), mm. 50–80: an unprepared third motion to A at m. 63, C♮/C♯ cross relations at mm. 67–69, and A revealed as V/V of G hypodorian only when the bass rises A–B♮–C♯ to D at m. 79. Heard in the 1♭ system, the internal areas balance symmetrically around the final: B♭ F C g d a (pp. 95–97).
- Legrenzi, Op. 2 (1655): sonatas in the eight church modes, an ordering Burnett calls unique. The phrygian and plagal settings lack a dominant. "La Spilimberga" (no. 2, G hypodorian): C minor passages at mm. 10–21 and 28–35 take almost half the movement; the bass C–C♯–D at mm. 26–27 becomes motivic; the closing G major triad sounds like V of C minor (pp. 102–6).
- Stradella, "T'intendo, si t'intendo" (*Moro per amore*, c. 1680): embryonic ritornello form. The F-major ritornello returns in D minor (mm. 15–19), twice in G minor (mm. 27–29, 30–34), and closes in F (mm. 45–49). The voice enters as a *devise*; motive a stays with the orchestra and b with the voice, a division "standard in concertos after 1700." The plan F–C–d–g–C–F stays on the 1♭ hexachord, eleven pitch classes, A♭/G♯ missing (pp. 108–13).
- Stradella's are the only concerto grosso scorings before Corelli (nine works, after Jander); the *Sinfonia in re maggiore* is the earliest known instrumental concerto grosso (p. 113).
- Stradella, sinfonia to *Ah! Troppo è ver* (Rome, c. 1670s), first movement in D, 2♯: principal ritornello a (mm. 1–5, to V) and b (cadential, to a half cadence), stated in D, G, and D; a chromatic secondary ritornello in E minor and A minor, read as Lucifer. F♮ in the A-minor statement (mm. 37–38) shifts to 1♭; G♯ is delayed to mm. 59–63 and restores 2♯. G major is heard as IV, displaced by V at m. 45 before the tonic at m. 46 (pp. 114–19). Stradella's concept of key likely rests on Bononcini (1673), who reduces the twelve modes to four (p. 115 n. 19).
- Lucifer's aria "Ah! Troppo è ver," E minor: ritornello a (concerto grosso, cadential) and b (concertino, scalar); G major occupies the middle of the aria; a secondary segment c sits inside the III area; the closing ritornello reverses the segment order (pp. 120–22).

**Marini, Op. 22 no. 1 (pp. 98–102)**

- The seminar's "Sonata à basso è violino ò cornetto." D dorian (first church key), cantus durus, naturalis system; violin, string bass obbligato, continuo. Three *parti*.
- Prima parte. Motives a (tonally stable) and b (sequential) are both stated unaccompanied in the bass. The opening, mm. 1–5, is i–V6–i–VI–V to a phrygian half cadence; the B♭–A upper neighbor to the dominant answers the C♯–D lower neighbor to the tonic.
- Fortspinnung: 5–7 fifths sequences from motive b at mm. 17–19 and 24–28. The background is D–A (m. 12)–E (m. 17)–A–D, fifths up the hexachord away from the tonic. Formal cadence on A at m. 22; two b-motive cycles at mm. 24–26 and 26–30, with B♭ again at m. 25.
- Naturalis throughout: eleven pitch classes, no E♭ or D♯. Hexachord F–C–g–d–a–e, B♭ as the allowable flat.
- The movement ends on a D major triad Burnett calls "bifocal": the tonic's root, and V of the Seconda parte in G hypodorian.
- Seconda parte: G hypodorian with B♭s under a cantus durus signature, still naturalis, no E♭ or D♯. The B♭–A relation is worked out to the dominant cadence at mm. 50–52; B♮ displaces B♭ at m. 53 and the mode becomes G mixolydian.
- Terza parte: localized fifths G, C, F, B♭ and back; cadence on D at m. 77 as V/G, authentic cadence on G at m. 81, duple meter from m. 87, D displacing G as tonic from m. 94. Major throughout, no system change. These measure numbers follow the Hortus Musicus edition (p. 102); check them against the Tufvesson copy.
- Burnett's frame: "the last gasps of modality and the birth of a new compositional ideal" (p. 94).

**Corelli, Op. 3 no. 2 (pp. 122–30)**

- The sonata's hexachord: G–D–A–e–b–[f♯], IV I V ii vi [iii]. Qualities are fixed; thirds are raised only for applied dominants to V and vi.
- Corelli never moves to the subdominant as a first goal. Stradella often did, which Burnett reads as residual modal thinking, "nascent tonality" (p. 123).
- Grave. A four-measure phrase from I to a half cadence on A, over a walking bass with fourth-species suspensions in the violins; the same on the dominant to a half cadence on E; then B minor at m. 12, the furthest point and the only minor auxiliary cadence. B minor becomes an upper neighbor to V in m. 13, which resolves to I in the same measure; coda. iii is omitted: a cadence on F♯ minor would need E♯ and a 5♯ system (pp. 124–25).
- Allegro (fugal). Bass D (m. 10)–e (m. 11)–E major (m. 12, II♯ as V/V) to a formal cadence on A at the downbeat of m. 13; cadence on vi at m. 17; b reinterpreted as V7/II♯ at m. 22, fifths B–E–A–D reaching the tonic in the middle of m. 26; four-measure coda (pp. 125–27).
- Adagio in B minor, 3/2, in the style of an aria. A seven-measure phrase to an authentic cadence; A (m. 8)–D (m. 9)–G (m. 10), cadence in D at m. 13. The minor dominant is tonicized through C♯, which brings E♯ at m. 19: first cancelled by D♮ in violin 1, then effective at the end of m. 19 in the bass (5♯). D♮ in violin 1 on the last beat of m. 22 (III6) restores 2♯. Back to III, then i, a seven-measure coda, and a phrygian cadence after the final tonic cadence that outlines the hexachord's boundaries, G and F♯. The only system shift in the sonata (pp. 127–28).
- Allegro, binary dance. I to V via II♯ at the double bar; then vi; B minor–E minor (turned to II♯)–A–D on downbeats; three-measure coda (p. 130).
- The minor mode is read as unstable: a rotation beginning on *la* that seeks its relative major. The major mode "vibrates ... in harmony with the overtone series," and the minor third wants "to close in on itself into a unison" (p. 129).
- Op. 5 no. 1 (1700), second movement: 62 measures in the tonic, then vi, then iii (F♯ minor, 5♯), then back by inverted fifths. From 1700 composers tonicize the last hexachordal fifth as a climax. The movement ends unprepared in the parallel minor via a g♯°7 chord; F♮ shifts to 1♭, which lasts into the next movement until G♯ in its second measure (pp. 130–31).
- The Corelli model (pp. 132–33). Major: I, V, vi, sometimes iii with a system shift, then back through IV or ii to I and a coda. Minor: i, III, then iv or VI, V, i, coda; the minor moves toward the major side of the hexachord, inverting the major's direction. A movement may end in a complementary system to prepare the next movement's key.

**Torelli (pp. 133–41)**

- Torelli, "following in the footsteps of Stradella," transposes the opening theme to hexachordal degrees, so Corelli's auxiliary cadences become complete periods: a systematic ritornello form (p. 133). Elementary in Op. 6 (1698), standardized in Op. 8, published posthumously by his brother Felice in 1709 (p. 134).
- Solo episodes have virtuosic figuration and move to the next area, usually IV, vi, or III. The order of solo and ripieno isn't standardized (Op. 8 no. 2 begins with the soloists). Middle movements stay in canzona form (p. 134).
- Two ritornello types. The fugal type can't be segmented and must return whole (Op. 8 no. 3). The segmented type, interlocking motives each with its own harmonic function, is the one Vivaldi and Bach use (pp. 134, 138).
- Op. 8 no. 3 in E, first movement: an 18-measure fugal ritornello with a tonic counter-exposition; a modal signature with every D sharped. Subdominant bias: ritornellos in A (IV) and c♯ (vi), none in V. The central episode prolongs V; G♮ in an A7 tonicizing D shifts 4♯ to 1♯, and 1♯ holds through the closing ritornello until A♯, leading tone to V, six measures from the end (p. 136).
- A subject on IV answered on V/IV is "almost cliché," returning to I with no preparatory dominant (p. 138).
- Op. 8 no. 8 in C minor, last movement: a 17-measure ritornello. Vordersatz mm. 1–10 (fugal subject and answer, closed by an authentic cadence), Fortspinnung mm. 11–14 (a fifths cycle modulating to the dominant), Epilog mm. 15–17 (cadence in G minor). Burnett adopts Fischer's terms from here on (p. 138 n. 25). Ritornellos only in i and III; the order is i–III–iv–v–i. The dominant is prolonged by its own dominant from m. 37 and takes a seventh at m. 43, which Burnett likens to a retransition (pp. 138–40).
- In the 3♭ system each F♯ implies the parallel major's system and is corrected at once by E♭; only G♭ would sustain the complementary system (p. 141).
- Vivaldi's Op. 3 circulated in manuscript around 1700, Burnett says, and may have influenced Torelli's Op. 8. Bach reshaped the subject of Op. 8 no. 8's last movement into BWV 537 (p. 138).

**Vivaldi, Op. 9 no. 2 in A, first movement (pp. 141–51)**

- The opening ritornello is in unison strings. Vordersatz mm. 1–6 to a half cadence, three rhythmic patterns; Fortspinnung a three-measure diatonic sequence on the neighbor figure, from m. 7; then the *pianoidée* (Krüger's term, 1932), a segment that turns to the parallel minor at a lower dynamic with reduced scoring (p. 145).
- The *pianoidée* brings C♮, the missing pitch of 3♯, shifting to the "0" system; B♭ at m. 11 is that system's allowable flat. D♯ at m. 30 restores 3♯ (pp. 145–47).
- Figure 4.1, the 3♯ matrix: the A–D♯ tritone is the system's axis. C♮ leads to the C system (divider F♯). B♯ leads to the F♯ system (6♯), whose missing pitch is A (p. 147).
- Dyad conflict: D♯ at m. 7, the onset of the Fortspinnung, against D♮ at m. 8. System conflict: C♮ against D♯. D♯ governs the dominant (the second ritornello, m. 29) and the C♯-minor area. Hexachordal opposites pair up under one implied signature: E with c♯, A with f♯, D with b (pp. 147–48).
- B♯ against A♮ governs the second episode and the third ritornello, in iii; A♮ wins out at m. 60 and returns the system to 3♯ (p. 148).
- iii before vi is unusual. The norm is iii as the penultimate ritornello, the furthest hexachordal pitch, corrected by the tonic return. Here ritornello 4 in F♯ minor (from m. 76) and ritornello 5 in A are Vordersatz only, joined by a 5–6 exchange. The third ritornello modulates from c♯ to b (pp. 148–49).
- The PCA in a tonal movement: pc 1 enters only at m. 43, and A–A♯–B–B♯–C♯ follow quickly, C♯ becoming the third ritornello's area. D♮ (pc 5) at m. 60; D♯ at m. 64, E, E♯ at m. 66, F♯ minor at m. 67 (pc 9) as the fourth ritornello; G♮ (pc 10) with a trill at m. 86; G♯ at m. 88 and again at the penultimate cadence, m. 102 (pp. 149–50).
- Ritornello 6 resolves D♯ to D♮, mm. 7–8 returning as mm. 105–6. The *pianoidée* comes back with C♮ and no correcting D♯, so the movement ends outside the tonic system. The second movement restores 3♯ with D♯ at m. 8; the finale settles everything (pp. 150–51).
- "Resolution" has two senses in the theory: voice-leading resolution, and the return of an inflected pitch to its diatonic form (p. 151).
- Brandenburg 1, first movement (p. 150 n. 30): the opening ritornello is diatonic except for B♮. By the closing ritornello the movement is in a 4♭ system that only B♮ can correct, so the final ritornello restores 1♭ and resolves B♮ back to B♭.

**Bach (pp. 151–53)**

- At Weimar (1708–17) Bach transcribed Italian concertos for Prince Johann Ernst, who collected Vivaldi's Op. 3 in manuscript before publication (p. 151).
- Bach follows Vivaldi's harmonic plan and segmentation but is more polyphonic. Solo and ripieno intertwine so that the ritornello-episode boundary blurs and segment boundaries are hard to fix. He disguises the Fortspinnung's sequential redundancy with rhythmic variation and asymmetrical phrasing (p. 152 and n. 32).
- Brandenburg 2, first movement, 1♭ system, hexachord B♭ F C g d a. The first 24 measures move I to V inside a solo episode with fragmentary ritornello; the second ritornello, in V, at m. 25; D minor at m. 31; a tonic ritornello at m. 45 divides the movement and is reinterpreted as V/IV; IV reached at m. 55; the ritornellos then climb the hexachord B♭–F–c–g–d–a with the dominant kept minor; ritornello 8 prolongs A minor through E major, whose G♯ shifts to 2♯; the last ritornello returns straight to F, and F♮ corrects the system (pp. 152–53).
- A penultimate ritornello in iii followed directly by the tonic, with no retransition, is common Baroque procedure. The early symphonists adopt the reintroduction of the missing pitch before the tonic return (p. 153), which is where chapter 5 and unit 3 begin.
- Burnett notes that Dreyfus analyzes the same movement without system shifts (p. 152 n. 33).

## dreyfus, "j. s. bach's concerto ritornellos and the question of invention" (pp. 327–58) · 1711 to 1735

*Musical Quarterly* 71, no. 3 (1985), the Bach and Schütz anniversary issue.

**What the article argues**

- Invention in Bach's concertos resides in the opening ritornello, understood as a set of harmonic functions, not a melodic idea. The foils are Wolff in *New Grove* (invention as "the critical moment," the rest craft), Marshall ("a fully formed melodic idea"), and Mattheson's "Thema, Modus, Tactus" (1739), which divides theme from harmonic system (pp. 327–29, 357).
- Dreyfus accepts invention as a rational ground of composition and sets out to specify it for the concertos, while denying that inductive rules guarantee its discovery (p. 328).
- Bach elaborated Vivaldi's ritornello procedures into a rigorous system, as his fugues make a genre of an informal set of opening moves. "Influence" trivializes the relation (p. 330).
- The sense of a concerto allegro doesn't lie in the number of ritornellos, the order of keys, or the overall balance; those "facts" of ritornello form are "anachronistically imposed by formal models derived from later eighteenth-century music." It proceeds from the relations among ritornellos and from their absence during solo episodes. Neither tutti-solo contrast nor virtuosity can compete in depth or logic (p. 334).
- Bach's ritornello movements matter "more ... as paradigms than as syntagms" (p. 334).
- The invention of a work may be an "ideal ritornello" that precedes it and isn't its opening gesture (p. 346). Composition, and analysis, can't rely on a verifiable starting point (p. 357).

**Scope and premises**

- Excluded: fugal-ritornello movements, middle slow movements, and ritornellos with unison themes (BWV 1052), none of which segment on the model of Vivaldi's Op. 3 nos. 8 and 10 (p. 328 n. 6).
- Against motivic labeling (Eller, Klein, Breig, Drummond): alphabetic symbols ignore function and turn differences in meaning into "a string of empty symbols" (p. 329).
- Bach's concerto ritornellos are "nearly without exception, tonally closed." The few that aren't presume a closed ritornello that matters later (p. 329).
- Period witnesses: Quantz (concerto themes more harmonic than melodic); Scheibe (the soloist enters only after the first cadence); Walther's *Praecepta* (1708), which defines the ritornello as a repetition, or "a *clausula*" (pp. 329–30).

**The three functions (pp. 330–33)**

- Wilhelm Fischer's Vordersatz, Fortspinnung, Epilog (1915), kept in German because English equivalents suggest sonata-form terms. Fischer stressed surface melodic process, to link it to Haydn and Mozart; Dreyfus turns the terms into harmonic functions "clothed in thematic identities" (p. 330).
- [V] defines the tonic by reference to its dominant, with evident root-position triads moving I to V. *Whether* it does so outranks *how*.
- [F] is premised on the absence of a defined tonic or of an authentic cadence resolving it. Conventional sequences (10-7-10-7, 5-6-5-6, 10-10-10) or freer counterpoint delay strong tonal articulation.
- [E] is the *clausula*: the formal tonic cadence closing on 1̂ in the upper voice. It begins where the Fortspinnung "transgresses, as it were, on foreign territory," for instance on arriving at the dominant.
- Segments are identified by function, not contour, and adjoining segments share notes and chords. BWV 1042, first movement: the Vordersatz reaches the dominant only at the end of its second phrase and is indivisible; the Fortspinnung is a 10-7-10-7 progression and then falling sixth chords; the Epilog has two subsections, deceptive then authentic (pp. 331–33).
- Brandenburg 1, third movement: Vordersatz mm. 1–4 to the dominant; Fortspinnung by two sets of tenths to the dominant at m. 12; Epilog in two harmonically equivalent closed segments, mm. 12–15 and 15–17 (p. 333 n. 10).

**Markedness (pp. 333–36)**

- Table I: Tonic Definition (I→V) is present in [V], absent in [F] and [E]. Tonic Resolution (V→I) is present in [E], absent in [V] and [F]. For TD, [V] and [F] are the marked and unmarked pair; for TR, [E] and [F]. The absence of TD in [E] and of TR in [V] isn't significant. The terms come from linguistics, via Holenstein on Jakobson.
- "The notion of 'invention' in the ritornello therefore directly presupposes the logic of tonality": the ritornello contains "the essential properties of the harmonic system in a tonal microcosm" (pp. 333–34).
- Ritornellos are marked, solo episodes unmarked. Episodes are defined by the absence of complete segments, not by contrasting themes. BWV 1042, mm. 70–78: Vordersatz fragments appear twice without the move to the dominant; the complete Vordersatz arrives in the subdominant, where Bach "covers his tracks" with a change of register and dynamics midway (pp. 334–36).

**The operations**

- SEGMENT: a ritornello displays its subdivisions, and a partial ritornello has logical consequences. The Echo's R3, [F* E], resolves the preceding material and gives that "solo section" Vordersatz-like pretensions (p. 354).
- DECORATE: surface change that keeps the syntax intact, tested by substituting the ideal form (pp. 339, 354). Two forms are grammatically identical if a competent listener hears no change of meaning; two segments are syntactically equivalent if they fill the same marked function differently, as [V1] and [V2] do (p. 336 n. 12).
- ORCHESTRATE, a kind of DECORATE: piano and forte mask the ritornello-episode polarity. In BWV 35/1, R8, [F1] (mm. 103–5) is marked piano with solo counterpoint, and the forte at m. 105 is "a playful deception" (p. 339). The tutti-solo distinction and the group-versus-solo concerto distinction don't govern the ritornello principle (p. 339 n. 14).
- ARRAY: segments transposed into significant keys. The order is "far from haphazard and reflects the usual hierarchy of scale steps," but Dreyfus stresses "the essential lack of an assigned harmonic plan prefiguring the tonal ARRAY" (p. 355).
- MODESWITCH: translation into the opposite mode, which checks for voice-leading errors and semantic shifts and rejects segments it renders non-occurrent (pp. 341–43, 355).
- RESEARCH: discovery of links between segments already ARRAYed and MODESWITCHed (p. 355).

**The analyses**

- BWV 1059/1, as BWV 35/1, D minor. R1, mm. 1–9: [V1 V2 F1 F2 E]. [V1] juxtaposes V and I in m. 2 and opens on 1̂; [V2] couldn't begin the piece and ends on V; [F1] is a 5-6-5-6 sequence to III in m. 6; [F2] rises in suspensions to the end of m. 8, where a diminished seventh substitutes for V6/5 on c♯; [E] closes (p. 336). Table II: R2 I (mm. 12–13); R3 III (22–23, 28–29); R4 V (33–36, 41–47); R5 IV (60–66); R6 VI (80–81); R7 to R10 all I (88–92, 98–105, 110–16, 122–31) (p. 338).
- MODESWITCH in BWV 35/1. [V1] translates. [V2] needs b♮ for b♭ in its penultimate chord, since the minor half cadence on a descending tetrachord has no exact major equivalent, and it produces an improperly prepared diminished fifth between bass and tenor, which R3 avoids by reducing the inner parts. [F1] in major can't resolve e to f and loses its direction to the relative major. So the major ritornellos, R3 and R6, have no F-segments; R6 and the episode at mm. 82–88 break off after [V1] and work motives where the Fortspinnung would be (pp. 341–43).
- Vivaldi, Op. 3 no. 10 (BWV 1065), first movement. The ritornello of Example 6 occurs nowhere intact in the movement. Table IIIa: R1 I (5–8, [V]); R2 I (12–15, 20–28); R3 III (37–40, [V]); R4 I (68–72, [F]); R5 I (97–103). The invention is an ideal ritornello, [V]–[F]–[E1–2] (pp. 343–46).
- Brandenburg 2, BWV 1047/1. R1 (mm. 1–8) is [Va Vb E] with no Fortspinnung; [Va] lacks a confirming dominant and punctuates the soloists' entrances. R7 (mm. 75–83), [Va F E], is closer to the convention but has a defective Vordersatz. Table IVa: R2 V (23–28); R3 VI (31–39); R4 I (46–49); R5 IV (56–59); R6 V minor (68–71); R7 II (75–83); R8 III (94–102); R9 I (103–6, 115–18). Every segment translates between modes except [F], non-occurrent in major, so every ritornello with a Fortspinnung is in minor. No grammatical ritornello is possible in either mode: the ideal ritornello is non-occurrent in both, and Bach "thinks the musically unthinkable" (pp. 346–51).
- "Echo," from *Clavierübung* II (BWV 831), B minor, given complete. Eight ritornellos (Table V), including two coupled pairs in different keys, R4–R5 and R6–R7, which look like modulating ritornellos. The Fortspinnung is non-occurrent in major. The solo episodes (mm. 5–12, 22–25, 37–44, 54–62) neither reproduce ritornello functions nor repeat themselves. Dreyfus calls it "a finer *Bach* concerto" than the first movement of the Italian Concerto, which Scheibe singled out in *Critischer Musikus* (pp. 351–56).

**The close (pp. 357–58)**

- Against organicist metaphors (Kerman, Narmour) and narrative ones (invent first, then work out). Metaphors "are rather like filters"; the task is to consider what each excludes, not to retreat into a language that feigns objectivity. At the limit *inventio*, *dispositio*, and *elaboratio* stop mattering and "the metaphor of 'music as rhetoric' itself begins to teeter."

## where the readings collide

**Burnett against Dreyfus: what organizes a ritornello movement**

- Burnett reads a movement as a harmonic plan along the reordered hexachord. Ritornellos stabilize its degrees, and system shifts mark its furthest points (pp. 123, 133–34, 148–49). He explains Vivaldi's iii-before-vi order by the D♯ and the PCA.
- Dreyfus holds that key order and ritornello count are later formal models imposed on the music (p. 334) and that no assigned harmonic plan prefigures the ARRAY (p. 355).
- The two agree that the order of keys follows the hierarchy of scale steps (Dreyfus p. 355). They disagree on whether that order is where the movement's sense lies.
- Paper 2's second directed question is Burnett's and its third is Dreyfus's. Ask students which reading their analysis supports, and on what evidence from the solos.

**Burnett's account of Dreyfus**

- Burnett (p. 140): a structural ritornello statement, which Dreyfus "calls 'marked,'" contains enough of one or more segments to stabilize a harmonic area. In Dreyfus, marked and unmarked name the binary features of Table I and the opposition of ritornello to solo episode (pp. 333–36). Stabilizing a harmonic area is Burnett's gloss.
- Burnett (pp. 140–41 and n. 27): Dreyfus "insists" that a statement made only of the Fortspinnung can't stabilize a non-tonic area, and Handel's "Alexander's Feast" concerto refutes it. Dreyfus defines [F] by the absence of tonic definition and tonic resolution (pp. 331, 334) and has no sentence stating the claim Burnett attributes to him. His own tables admit partial ritornellos built on [F]: R4 of BWV 1065 is [F] alone, in the tonic (p. 346), and the Echo's R3 is [F* E], in V (p. 354).
- Students who cite Dreyfus through Burnett will misstate him. Send them to pp. 330–36.

**Two vocabularies for one segmentation**

- Burnett adopts Fischer's terms from p. 138. Before that he labels motives a, b, c (Marini, Stradella), the practice Dreyfus rejects (p. 329).
- Burnett uses "Fortspinnung" in two senses: a general late-Baroque spinning-out technique, already in Marini (p. 98), and Fischer's segment. Dreyfus uses only the second.
- Burnett's Vordersatz can close with an authentic cadence (Torelli Op. 8 no. 8, p. 138) or end on a half cadence (Vivaldi Op. 9 no. 2, p. 145). Dreyfus defines it by tonic definition alone, with tonic resolution not significant. The two are compatible.
- Burnett allows an Epilog that confirms the dominant (p. 138 n. 26) and ritornellos that modulate (p. 140). Dreyfus holds Bach's ritornellos tonally closed and explains the Echo's apparently modulating ritornellos as two segments ARRAYed in different keys (pp. 329, 355). The difference may be one of repertoire, Torelli against Bach, which is what the fourth directed question asks about.
- Burnett adds a fourth segment, the *pianoidée*, for some Vivaldi (p. 145). Dreyfus's matrix has three.
- Dreyfus excludes unison-theme ritornellos as unsegmentable on the Op. 3 model (p. 328 n. 6). Burnett segments the unison ritornello of Op. 9 no. 2 (p. 145).

**Burnett's narrative against Hyer's historiography**

- Burnett's book calls its own organization "basically teleologic" (p. xix). Chapter 4 speaks of "stagnant tonality" (pp. 92, 95), "the last gasps of modality," and an art of composition that "needed to" change "in order for the modern developmental process to become an integral part of a burgeoning tonality" (p. 94); of Legrenzi "at the gateway of the late Baroque" (p. 106); of Marini's attempts taking on significance "in light of later compositional practice" (p. 102); of Corelli's plan lasting into the late nineteenth century (p. 133).
- Hyer's three objections apply point for point: mediation, the single mainstream, the privileging of later practice (pp. 745–46). Burnett concedes the first: "it is unreasonable to criticize Monteverdi or Frescobaldi for not being Vivaldi" (p. 94).
- Burnett grounds Stradella's concept of key in Bononcini (p. 115 n. 19) and Corelli's minor in Rameau (p. 117 n. 21). Hyer: almost all tonal music before Liszt was written without the concept of tonality (p. 746). Chapter 3 claimed the hexachordal systems as constructs available to sixteenth-century musicians; for the tonal systems of chapter 4, ask students whether the claim still holds or the systems have become an analytical model.

**Metaphor in Burnett and Dreyfus, read through Hyer**

- Burnett: tonal "gravitational pull" (p. 94); a major mode that "vibrates ... in harmony with the overtone series" and a minor third "wanting to close in on itself into a unison" (p. 129); Stradella's keys as "good" and "evil" (p. 116); a pitch that "wins out" (p. 148). Hyer's Rhetoric section (pp. 728–33) sorts these as gravitational and animistic; the moral reading of keys is a further kind.
- Dreyfus's model is itself a metaphor, language: grammar, syntax, semantics, marked and unmarked. He says so and treats metaphors as filters (p. 357). Hyer doesn't list the linguistic metaphor.
- Burnett's Roman numerals under hexachord pitches belong to Hyer's scale-degree tradition. One hexachord pitch reading differently in D major and B minor (Burnett pp. 118, 127) is Weber's *Mehrdeutigkeit* (Hyer p. 735). Burnett's hexachord in fifths and Hauptmann's F–a–C–e–G–b–D (Hyer p. 736) both lay the diatonic out on a line of fifths.

**Where tonality begins**

- Hyer: Fétis's 1600 Monteverdi origin is lore (p. 729); Dahlhaus places the origin with Josquin (p. 734); Besseler with fauxbourdon in the 1430s (p. 747); Powers denies there's a transition to locate (p. 738). The reduction to two modes is complete in Mattheson, 1713 (p. 738).
- Burnett: key-centered tonality in the 1670s and 1680s, codified by Corelli; Monteverdi's 1610 "Laetatus sum" still sectional (pp. 89, 93–94, 122–23).
- The unit 1 collision between Burnett and Powers continues. Hyer cites a different Powers essay, "Is Mode Real?" (1992), for the separate-planes argument.

**The system rule changes between units**

- Unit 1, chapter 3: the missing pitch spelled as a flat moves the system down a fifth, spelled as a sharp up a fifth.
- Unit 2, chapter 4 (pp. 118, 128): in tonal music the minor-third spelling moves it three signatures flatward, the augmented-second spelling three sharpward.
- Chapter 4 replaces the names naturalis, mollis, and durus with signature counts (2♯ system, 1♭ system, "0" system), while still equating cantus durus with naturalis for Frescobaldi and Marini (pp. 91, 98).
- Students who carry the unit 1 rule into Paper 2 will misplace every shift. Put both rules on the board in Week 7.
