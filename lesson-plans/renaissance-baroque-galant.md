# renaissance, baroque, galant: a style comparison, c. 1550–1770

This instructor-facing file is an introductory comparison of the three style labels and the ground between them, by strand. `unit-1-modal-theory-1000-1600.md` covers modal theory to 1600; the `unit-N-readings.md` files cover each assigned reading.

Project sources are cited by author and page. The Christensen chapters (*The Cambridge History of Western Music Theory*, 2002) are cited by chapter author: Wason (ch. 2), Barnett (13), Schubert (16), A. Cohen (17), Bent (18), Caplin (21), Hyer (23), Lester (24), McCreless (27), Burnham (28). Burnett and Nitzberg (2007) are cited by chapter author, Burnett for chapters 3–5 and Nitzberg for chapter 2. Powers is "Tonal Types and Modal Categories in Renaissance Polyphony" (1981); Dreyfus is "J. S. Bach's Concerto Ritornellos and the Question of Invention" (1985).

Outside scholarship is cited by author and page with a tag for how it was read. [read] is the source itself. [via review] is a review, abstract, catalog record, or another scholar's citation of the source. [lead] is a reference-work claim not yet checked against its source. Full citations for outside sources are at the end. Quotations from outside sources were read through a summarizing fetch tool; check them against the source before they're used in student-facing material.

## the labels

### "baroque"

The earliest musical use found is pejorative. An anonymous letter in the *Mercure de France* of May 1734 called Rameau's *Hippolyte et Aricie* baroque (Martín Sáez 20–21 [read]); Wilfing-Albrecht gives the 1734 attack on Rameau, by comparison with Lully, without naming the opera [read]. Rousseau's *Dictionnaire de musique* (1768) defines baroque music as harmony confused and overloaded with modulations and dissonances, and Koch's *Musikalisches Lexikon* (1802, 214) repeats the definition (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]; Rousseau and Koch not read). In both it's a term of criticism. Martín Sáez stresses that it isn't yet a period or style label (25, 27 [read]).

The period term is borrowed from art history. Burckhardt's *Der Cicerone* (1855) used it for an epoch of decay; Wölfflin's *Renaissance und Barock* (1888) made it an independent stylistic epoch (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]). Curt Sachs, "Barockmusik" (1919), is usually credited with bringing it into musicology, but Wellesz used it in "Renaissance und Barock" (1909/10), and Wellesz dated the beginning to 1520 or to 1576 (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]). Robert Haas (1928) set the start at about 1600, after the deaths of Palestrina and Lasso in 1594 (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]). Adler avoided the word; Riemann's alternative was the Generalbasszeitalter, the thoroughbass age (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]; A. Cohen 542 n. 31).

Bukofzer (1947) divided the era into early (1580–1630), middle (1630–80), and late (1680–1730) phases. One review gives these as phases for Italy (Feraru [via review]); Wikipedia gives the same dates, citing Palisca's Grove article [lead]. Martín Sáez argues that Bukofzer popularized the label by adopting Wölfflin's comparative method, and that the book asserts its opposition to the Renaissance and its doctrine of affections without explaining them (30–34 [read]).

Palisca defended "Baroque" for c. 1540–1730 while conceding it names no single idiom (Martín Sáez 35 [via review]). Wikipedia gives the same article's span as 1600–1750 [lead]. The Grove article wasn't read. The start dates proposed across these sources are 1520, c. 1540, 1576, 1580, and 1594/1600.

The Christensen authors use the word with reservations. Wason calls it the name the period has "invidiously" come to have (53); A. Cohen calls the music "typically, if problematically" labeled Baroque (538). Caplin, Hyer, Bent, Lester, McCreless, and Burnett use it without comment, and Burnett has Monteverdi leading into the early Baroque (80) and Legrenzi at the start of the late Baroque (106).

### "galant"

Galanterie was in use in France from the 1630s as an aesthetic, a social model, and an ethic (Aspden 10, after Viala [read]). Mattheson's early use is social. In his *Das neu-eröffnete Orchestre* (1713) the galant homme is the fashionable amateur listener, and the word means up to date, not a compositional style (Perutková 128–29 [via review]). That Mattheson first wrote of a "galant style" in *Das forschende Orchestre* (1721) is a Wikipedia claim [lead]; Perutková says the 1721 text doesn't define the style in detail (129 [via review]). Heinichen's *Der General-Bass in der Composition* (1728) promotes the new galant style through the concept of taste, and his 1711 treatise doesn't (Utrecht dissertation abstract, author and year not shown [via review]).

Eighteenth-century writers define the galant against an older style. Scheibe calls the old style strict, bound, and learned; C. P. E. Bach distinguishes the learned style from the galant; Koch equates the free, unbound style with the galant (Perutková 131–33 [via review]). Ratner reports "free style" used as a synonym for galant against the strict style (Pedrero-Encabo 3, citing Ratner 23 [via review]). Sheldon (1975) holds that musical definitions of the galant appear only in eighteenth-century Germany, and treats the galant more as a theoretical antithesis to the rule of tradition than as a style (Aspden 10, citing Sheldon 239, a page before the article's first page [via review]). Marpurg and others opposed "effeminate" galant song to the "manly" character of counterpoint (Aspden 14 [read]).

Modern scholarship uses "galant" in place of other labels. Heartz prefers it to "rococo" and "Enlightenment" and resists "classical" as reductive (Lockwood [via review]); Gjerdingen uses it in place of Vorklassik, Rokoko, and empfindsamer Stil (Neuwirth 402 [via review]).

In the project sources the term appears in Caplin (657–58), McCreless (868, 870), and Burnett. Caplin pairs "the galant and Classical styles" by formal articulation, melodic prominence, and balanced phrasing (657). Burnett dates "the emerging style galant" to the 1730s and 1740s, derives it from the even phrasing of the dance, and finds it in comic opera (Pergolesi, *La serva padrona*, 1733) and the Italian keyboard sonata (160); its traits are dance rhythms, symmetrical phrase groups, and an overall diatonic harmonic framework (164).

### "empfindsam"

The word wasn't a musical term in C. P. E. Bach's lifetime. Lessing suggested it to Bode as a German rendering of Sterne's "sentimental," and Bode's translation popularized it (Marggraf 329, who gives 1768 in the text and 1769 in a footnote [read]). Bach uses "Empfindung" and never "empfindsam" in surviving documents, and at the time the word described persons and literature. Musical use is retrospective; an early case is Reichardt's memoir in the *AmZ* (1814), recalling 1774 (Marggraf [read]). Schulenberg gives Lessing's suggestion little weight and notes "Empfindungen" in the title of Bach's late fantasia W. 80 (Supplement 1.5 [read]).

Schulenberg reports the alternatives: Darrell Berg's "mannerist" in place of Empfindsamkeit, and Eggebrecht and Hoffmann-Erbrecht's tie to Sturm und Drang (introduction, 5 [read]). He calls Telemann and Hasse, Bach's older contemporaries, "more galant" (1), and criticizes the evolutionary model that made Bach a transitional figure between Baroque and Classical (4, 7). Wikipedia frames Empfindsamkeit as a North German dialect of the galant, citing Heartz and Brown's Grove article and Eugene Wolf in the *Harvard Dictionary* [lead].

In the project sources "empfindsam" appears only in McCreless (870, 872).

### "renaissance," "rococo," "pre-classical"

Neither the project sources nor the outside search covered the history of "Renaissance" as a music-historical label. The pairing with "Baroque" comes from art history (Wölfflin, 1888) and enters musicology with Wellesz (1909/10) (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]).

Bücken's *Die Musik des Rokokos und der Klassik* (1927) institutionalized "Rokoko" as a Handbuch period (Wilfing-Albrecht [read]); Sheldon discusses Bücken's two-phase model of the galant (2024 *Zbornik radova* article [via review]). Kirkendale's *Fugue and Fugato in Rococo and Classical Chamber Music* (1979) keeps "Rococo" in a study of fugue in the period (title only, not read). Heartz rejects "rococo"; Gjerdingen avoids Rokoko and Vorklassik.

## 1. texture and the bass

Late-sixteenth-century counterpoint is built on a two-voice frame. Zarlino's chord tables take the tenor–soprano interval as given, and the remaining voices are chosen from the tables (Schubert 525). For Zarlino, C–E–A is a modification of C–E–G, not an inversion of A–C–E (Lester 754). How late the tenor–soprano framework continues to apply is debated (Schubert 519 n. 55, after Dahlhaus).

Continuo theory codifies a practice older than itself, in existence since at least the late fifteenth century (A. Cohen 540, quoting Ashworth and O'Dette). Viadana's *Cento concerti ecclesiastici* (1602) has the first independent continuo part; the partitura and the basso seguente (the term first in Banchieri, 1607) are its precursors (A. Cohen 540 and n. 26). Agazzari (1607) classes instruments as foundation or ornament (A. Cohen 541) and gives practical reasons for figured bass: recitative needs only a bass, and imitative music suits the new style poorly (Barnett 441). Hyer: with continuo, Western music is heard as successions of harmonies rather than collections of intervals (739). In late Baroque music the walking bass outlines triads and marks cadences and often behaves as a contrapuntal voice (Burnett 112 n. 16).

By the early eighteenth century thorough bass is taught as composition. Heinichen (1728) equates playing a thorough bass with improvising or composing on a given bass (A. Cohen 543). Mattheson (1739) says composition can't exist without it; Rameau (1760) equates the principles of composition and accompaniment; C. P. E. Bach explains the free fantasia through it (A. Cohen 542). Wason places the emergence of a transposable major–minor key system most clearly in Baroque keyboard practice (53).

The project sources say little about galant texture as such. Caplin names melodic prominence (657); Burnett names an overall diatonic harmonic framework (164). Gjerdingen's schemata are melody–bass pairs, and the partimento that taught them is a bass to be realized (see the galant schemata section). The Alberti bass and the galant keyboard idiom aren't in the project sources.

Grey areas:

- The bass as foundation is older than continuo. Sancta María (1565) starts from the soprano–bass interval and likens the bass to a building's foundation (Schubert 525).
- Triadic theory is older than continuo theory and comes from other traditions: Harnisch (1608) and Lippius (c. 1610) (Lester 755). Thomas Campion's 5/3 chains (c. 1618) descend from fifteenth-century contrapunctus simplex as much as they show triadic thinking (Schubert 505, 527).
- Continuo is at odds with inversion in theory and promotes it in practice (Lester 755).
- Burnett has continuo-derived counterpoint replacing voice-derived counterpoint by mid-century, with Monteverdi's "Laetatus sum" (1610) already there (93 n. 5). Schubert puts a seventeenth-century trio on a dance bass in the same framework as Burgundian and Spanish improvisation (504).

## 2. pitch organization

Renaissance modal theory classifies polyphony by final, ambitus, and species, in eight or twelve modes. Modal analysis of polyphony is itself a Renaissance innovation, and Zarlino's twelve modes are the doctrine most widely disseminated in the seventeenth century (Barnett 415). Cerreto (1601) still argues for eight; Bononcini (1673) for twelve (Barnett 414 and n. 23).

In practice the seventeenth century organizes pitch by church keys, the psalm-tone tonalities, and these are the basis of the early-eighteenth-century notion of key (Barnett 419; Table 13.3, 420–24). French and English practical writers reach two kinds of key by the 1660s. Millet (1666) says there are only two cadences, of the minor third (b mol) and of the major third (b quarre) (Barnett 434–35), and Thomas Campion's "key of G with B flat" and "B sharpe" anticipate the later English sharp and flat keys (Barnett 439). The English, outside the Catholic psalmodic tradition, reach a two-key system by mid-century (Barnett 441). In France Cohen ties the change to Lully's dominance in the 1660s (Barnett 430 n. 73). Mattheson's twenty-four keys (1713) come in three groups of eight, with the church keys as the core (Barnett 426–27). Heinichen draws the circle of keys in 1711 (Barnett 444–46). Rameau (1722, 1737) is the first theorist to recognize that the separately developed components of harmonic tonality interact (Lester 753); for him a key is a focus that emerges from the progression (Lester 762).

Burnett's modal-system model applies from c. 1542 to the 1670s (47). He dates key-centered tonality to north Italian instrumental music of the 1670s and 1680s, under the influence of opera and cantata (89); large-scale modulation needs ritornello form, from the late 1680s (90). Corelli is the first major composer to set a hierarchy between the hexachord fifths and the tonic (132). Through the eighteenth century, modulation stays within the roots of the tonic hexachord, and Vogler (1776) allows six principal keys in any key (Nitzberg 37).

Grey areas:

- Barnett and Powers deny that modes evolved into keys. For Barnett, terminology tracks the theorist's purpose, not stages of style (408, 427, 435, 450). For Powers, interest in modality increased through the sixteenth century, so the question of evolution is a "non-question," while particular tonal types are ancestral to particular keys (467). Hyer reports a gradual reduction from eight or more modes to two, completed in Mattheson 1713 (738), and also criticizes evolutionary histories (745–46). Burnett calls his own book basically teleological (xix).
- Church keys are neither modes nor keys. So-called incomplete signatures (c with two flats, A with two sharps, d with none, g with one) are church keys or their transpositions (Barnett 428–29).
- The tonal answer was the seventeenth century's "modal" fugue (Barnett 419).
- Fétis's dating of modern tonality to Monteverdi (*Stracciami*, 1592; *Cruda Amarilli*) is musicological lore of arguable validity (Hyer 729). Other proposed starting points: Josquin (Dahlhaus, Hyer 734) and fauxbourdon (Besseler, Hyer 747).
- German theory keeps modal terms through the seventeenth century (Barnett 435) and into the eighteenth (Lester 758). Fux (1725) rejects the twenty-four keys and fought Mattheson over them in 1717–18 (Bent 570, 577). Martini (1774–75) puts mode first (Bent 579). Albrechtsberger (1790) replaces Fux's six modal cantus firmi with two, in C major and E minor (Bent 583).
- The translations of Fux change his tonal language. Mizler's German (1742) equates D lasolre with D minor (Bent 576); the English (1768) speaks only of sharp and flat keys (Bent 578).
- Bach's chorales give modal melodies major or minor harmonizations (Hyer 738).

## 3. counterpoint and dissonance

Zarlino's Part III (1558) is the definitive contemporary account of prima prattica technique, though his conservative rules don't mirror practice without distortion (Wason 52). Voice-leading rules were learned young, for improvisation; the composer's task was elaborating a soggetto (Schubert 503). Vicentino (1555) and Zarlino (1558) already discuss evading the cadence (Lester 757), and Banchieri (1614) teaches a "moderna pratica" on Lassus and Rore, with evaded cadences, scherzi, and word painting (Schubert 518).

The seconda prattica reoriented pedagogy (Wason 53). The Artusi–Monteverdi exchange, from Carter's chronology [read], with Fuchs [read]:

| 16 Nov. 1598 | Monteverdi madrigals performed in Ferrara; Artusi probably present |
| 20 Nov. 1600 | Artusi, *L'Artusi overo Delle imperfettioni della moderna musica*, quoting Monteverdi's unpublished madrigals without naming him |
| 1 Mar. 1603 | Monteverdi, Fourth Book of madrigals |
| 25 Mar. 1603 | Artusi, *Seconda parte dell'Artusi*, naming Monteverdi |
| 30 July 1605 | Monteverdi, Fifth Book, ending with a statement on the seconda pratica |
| 1605 or 1606 | Artusi as Braccino da Todi, the lost first *Discorso musicale* (Fuchs 455: 1605; Carter: "1606?") |
| 21 July 1607 | Giulio Cesare Monteverdi, "Dichiaratione," in the *Scherzi musicali* |
| 1608 | Braccino, *Discorso secondo musicale* |

Artusi's objections include the cadences in C rather than G in *Cruda Amarilli* (Burnett 41–42). Fétis locates Monteverdi's unprepared dominant seventh at *Cruda Amarilli*, mm. 13–14 (Hyer 729); Choron has Monteverdi inventing the dominant seventh around 1590 and first introducing it without preparation, naming no piece (Hyer 729 n.).

Bernhard (c. 1660) makes style the basis of classification. Stylus gravis is the prima prattica; stylus luxurians communis and theatralis are the seconda prattica for church and theatre, with the freest dissonance reserved for the theatre (McCreless 862). Each luxurians figure is given an origin in the stylus gravis (McCreless 864). In the later *Ausführlicher Bericht* he retreated from the style classes as more applicable to Italian than German music (McCreless 862 n. 22). Mirka traces the eighteenth-century pair of strict and free (galant) styles through Bernhard to Doni's stile antico and moderno (1635) and the prima and seconda pratica (Mirka 3 [read]).

Fux's *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725) has an Italian late Baroque opera composer lead the modern composer through the species, with Palestrina as the model; it contains no Palestrina, and Fux recommends his own masses (Bent 555, 572). Its rules aren't new. The four rules of progression are in Diruta (1609), Bismantova (1677), and Berardi (1689), and proto-species are in Diruta, Banchieri (1613, Bent's date for the *Cartella musicale*; Schubert and Barnett give 1614), Zacconi (1622), and Bononcini (1673) (Bent 561–66). The last section treats present-day styles: the a cappella, the mixed, the recitative (Bent 574–75; translated in Wollenberg 1992). Its translations are Mizler (1742), Manfredi (1761), English (1768), and Denis (1773–75) (Christensen bibliography).

Its afterlife reaches the galant and Classical generation. Durante recommended it to Piccinni, and a translation by Carro, master of music at the Naples court, was reportedly the sole elementary book of composition at the Royal Conservatory (Bent 576). Haydn owned and annotated it in the early 1750s. Mozart's lessons to Attwood (1785–86) took over half their exercises from it but began with chords, figured bass, and free composition, and used Rameau's fundamental bass (Bent 580). Wagenseil, Fux's pupil, taught it to Schenk, who taught Beethoven (Bent 581; Burnett 167). Albrechtsberger requires harmonic analysis of the cantus firmus first; Kirnberger (1782) calls the method excessively strict (Bent 582).

Grey areas:

- The old style becomes one register among several. Bernhard's classes and Fux's a cappella, mixed, and recitative sections name styles that coexist by function, in church, chamber, and theatre.
- The *Gradus* is a Renaissance style codified by a late Baroque opera composer, and its rules are a digest of seventeenth-century Italian teaching (Bent 561–66). It's also the training text of Haydn, Mozart, and the Naples conservatory.
- Bent divides eighteenth-century theory into a Catholic Italian line (Palestrina, Fux) and a Protestant Berlin line based on J. S. Bach, who rejected species counterpoint according to C. P. E. Bach (1775) (581). Wason divides it into French fundamental bass, Italian species counterpoint, and German thorough bass, blending by the century's end (59).
- In the galant and after, the old style returns as a topic. Ratner's topics include the learned style (Ratner, Part I [via review, catalog contents]), and Mirka defines topics as styles and genres taken out of their proper context and used in another (2 [read]). Reading the learned style as stile antico quoted inside galant music is inference from that definition.
- "Stile antico" as a term appears once in the project sources (Lester 773).

## 4. meter and phrase

The mensural system turns into the modern one during the seventeenth century. The tactus is at the semibreve, and seventeenth-century writing on meter mixes conservative and progressive views (Caplin 659). The measure derives from the tactus (hence *Takt*), and Mattheson holds the conservative view that all meters are fundamentally two-part (Caplin 660). Eighteenth-century rhythmic theory begins in the 1680s and 1690s (Caplin 658).

Caplin ties theory to style. The motoric pulses, regular accents, and dance rhythms of high Baroque style turn theorists to classifying meters; the formal articulation, melodic prominence, and balanced phrasing of the galant and Classical styles turn them to phrase groups and cadential goals (657). Rhythmic motives expressing the single affect of a movement are a hallmark of Baroque style (663–64). In Burnett, Fortspinnung can appear anywhere given the irregular phrasing of Baroque music (98), and Bach disguises sequences by asymmetrical phrasing (152 n. 32).

Under the galant, measure groups become "rhythmical" (Caplin 658). Riepel initiated phrase-structural analysis and set the four-measure phrase as a norm, at least for the minuet; Kirnberger says the best melodies have four-measure phrases (Caplin 671). Koch has basic, extended, and compound phrases (Caplin 672–75). In Burnett, Sammartini's Sinfonia in C (c. 1720s) moves in even four-measure units unlike Vivaldi's or Bach's asymmetrical units (160). For Burnett, galant phrasing repeats the first measure of each two-measure unit at the same pitch (160). Wagenseil's WV 393 (1760) is almost consistently in four-measure hypermeasures with extensions (170). Burnett gives two eighteenth-century types of opening statement: galant periodic phrasing, and the motivic segmentation of the late Baroque concerto (Vordersatz, Fortspinnung, Epilog) (185–86).

Grey areas:

- Caplin finds strong conceptual inertia (657): mensural vestiges into the eighteenth century (658), and Kirnberger still ties meters to genre and tempo, with 2/2 for church pieces and fugues and 3/4 at the tempo of a minuet (669).
- Burnett makes symmetrical, dance-derived phrasing the defining galant trait (160, 164, 185). Caplin says Koch's categories aren't rooted in phrase symmetry, and that symmetry as the Classical norm is a Romantic distortion (674–75).
- Dance phrasing is older than the galant. Seventeenth-century dances have clear-cut phrasing and no thematic expansion (Burnett 92), and Corelli's binary finales set the harmonic plan in symmetrical dance phrasing (Burnett 130).
- Phrase theory's starting point differs by author. Caplin credits Riepel (671); McCreless credits Mattheson's grammatical terminology (870); Wason has Mattheson and Riepel earlier, Koch the most systematic (57).

## 5. form

Before the 1670s, Burnett finds mosaic designs of short contrasting sections in the canzona manner, variations, sectional toccatas, and ricercars (89). Frescobaldi's 1637 preface lets toccata sections be played separately (92). The dance, non-sectional or binary, is the one form with inherent tonal coherence (91). The aria forms of 1670s and 1680s opera (binary, strophic, ostinato, da capo) lead toward tonal hierarchy, rather than the ensemble sonata (107). Corelli's binary movements move I to V at the double bar (130). Torelli makes ritornello form systematic, and his Op. 8 (1709) has Fischer's Vordersatz, Fortspinnung, and Epilog (133–38). Burnett dates sonata form from the late 1730s; before it the tonic return is unarticulated (117 n. 20).

For the galant symphony, Burnett has Sammartini's Symphony No. 1 in C (c. 1720s) close to the ritornello form of the concerto (158) and No. 3 in D (c. 1730s) binary, in the new galant aesthetic (164). Wagenseil's sonata form keeps ties to binary dance forms, with the dominant reduced to a large-scale half cadence at the double bar (167), and his and Monn's works are still slightly expanded binaries (180). Eighteenth-century composers and theorists never labeled sonata form, which Burnett calls a procedure rather than a form (181). Koch's exposition is a single period that establishes the tonic and cadences on the fifth (Burnett 170–71; Burnham 882); Koch treats the minuet exhaustively, and his sonata form is really the first allegro of the symphony (Burnham 882).

Grey areas:

- Sammartini's first symphonies have a ritornello-based movement (1720s) and a galant binary one (1730s) a decade apart (Burnett 158, 164).
- Burnett reads the early symphony out of the ritornello concerto: the penultimate ritornello in iii becomes Wagenseil's development climax in iii, and the gesture recurs in late Haydn (175–76). Dreyfus: the number and key order of ritornellos are facts imposed by formal models from later eighteenth-century music (334), and Bach's ritornello movements have no narrative themes given sequential development (331).
- "Development" is a later label for the second half of Sammartini's binary design (Burnett 166). "Variations" as a procedure is named only in the eighteenth century, Koch (1787) among the first (A. Cohen 548).
- Late-seventeenth-century sonata and dance collections order their movements by the church keys (Barnett 426).

## 6. rhetoric and affect

The German musica poetica tradition begins inside the Renaissance. Wason (52–53) and A. Cohen (535) date it to Listenius's *Rudimenta musicae* (1533); McCreless dates it to Listenius's *Musica* (1537) (853). Dressler (1563) first parallels exordium, medium, and finis (McCreless 853). Burmeister (1606) names twenty-seven figures and presumes a diatonic, homophonic, chorale-like norm; Lippius models the five parts of rhetoric and names no figures (McCreless 854–56). The figures are a descriptive taxonomy (McCreless 859).

Bernhard (c. 1660) recasts the figures by style (McCreless 862–64). Printz's figures are melodic Manieren with Italian names, and they merge with the diminutions of galant vocal and instrumental music (McCreless 868). Mattheson mentions figures in passing, many of them out of date, and turns to inventio (loci topici, after Heinichen 1711) and dispositio, melody before harmony and counterpoint (McCreless 869). His dispositio analysis of a Marcello aria doesn't work but announces rhetoric as a metaphor for form (McCreless 869). The text-expressive goal is incompatible with the galant and empfindsam styles and with independent instrumental music; Scheibe (*Der critische Musikus*) moves the figures into the Enlightenment, and Spiess (1745) is the last believer in musica poetica (McCreless 870). Forkel (1788) is the last Figurenlehre theorist (McCreless 872–73).

The project sources give affect as a Baroque trait in Caplin's single affect per movement (663–64) and in Burnett's single affect of the operatic text in the 1670s and 1680s (112 n. 15). Martín Sáez criticizes Bukofzer's reliance on a doctrine of affections (30–34 [read]). The Affektenlehre and key characteristics (Mattheson 1713, Schubart) aren't in the project sources.

Grey areas:

- Rhetoric as a theory of composition is German. Italian, French, and British theorists' devotion to rhetoric is superficial, and they develop no Figurenlehre (McCreless 852). Forchert argues that the central Protestant composition pedagogy (Calvisius, Crüger, Herbst) doesn't revolve solely around the figures (McCreless 858 n. 18).
- The tradition changes from Burmeister to Bernhard in fifty years and isn't a fixed doctrine (McCreless 853, 867).
- The galant breaks with text expression. The figures become melodic ornament (McCreless 868), and Scheibe's figures fit the declamatory German music of the empfindsam style and of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven (McCreless 872).
- Renaissance counterpoint is taught in a culture of memorized formulas: Tinctoris's successions, Montaños's commonplaces, Banchieri's cadences (Schubert 528). Galant schemata are memorized by rote in every key (Gjerdingen 26, 32 [read]). The comparison is inference.

## 7. pedagogy

Renaissance students learned improvised counterpoint (contrapunto alla mente) once they were skilled contrapuntists (A. Cohen 537). The principles of soggetto elaboration are consistent for nearly two hundred years (Schubert 504), and counterpoint treatises give little guidance on melody (Schubert 528).

The first figured-bass books are practical in the least imaginative sense (Wason 54). The Rule of the Octave has precursors in Bianciardi (1607), Penna (1672), Locke's *Melothesia* (1673), an anonymous late-seventeenth-century Italian manuscript, and Bruschi (1711), and triadic harmony and tonal function both develop within these scale harmonizations (Barnett 442–44). The guitarist François Campion canonized the rule in 1716 (Lester 756–57). Niedt's *Handleitung* (1700–17) varies thorough basses into dance movements (A. Cohen 545–46).

Wason gives three eighteenth-century models: Rameau's fundamental bass, Fux's species, and a less systematic German model of chorale harmonization and figured-bass diminution (56). C. P. E. Bach reports that his father's pupils skipped the species and began with four-part thorough bass and chorales (Wason 56). Heinichen's *General-Bass in der Composition* (1728) is a complete composition text of 960 pages (Wason 57). Christensen calls Rameau's "L'Art de la basse fondamentale" (c. 1738–45) arguably the first harmony textbook in the modern sense (quoted in Wason 55 and n. 30). The fundamental bass spread without attribution, in Heinichen (1728) and Lampe (1737); C. P. E. Bach decried Rameau but opened his 1762 manual with 5/3, 6/3, and 6/4, an order absent from pre-Rameau manuals (Lester 772–73). Mattheson claims to be the first theorist to give melody its due (McCreless 869). Kirnberger's *Kunst des reinen Satzes* (1771–79) synthesizes the traditions, and Koch (1782–93) gives the most systematic method of melody (Wason 57). By the century's end the three national traditions had begun to blend (Wason 59).

The project sources give partimento one paragraph. It's intermediate between thorough bass and preluding, a series of unfigured basses leading to the improvisation of whole pieces, fugal ones included, cultivated well into the nineteenth century, particularly in Italy (A. Cohen 548).

Grey areas:

- Each pedagogical tradition is national and confessional as much as chronological (Wason 59; Bent 581).
- In the galant decades four teaching traditions are in use: Fux (Haydn, the Naples conservatory), thorough bass and chorale (Bach's pupils), partimento (the Neapolitan conservatories), and phrase manuals (Riepel, Koch). Mozart's teaching of Attwood combines Fux, figured bass, and Rameau's fundamental bass (Bent 580).

## galant schemata and partimento

The project sources have no schema theory. This section is from outside scholarship.

Gjerdingen (2007) defines galant music by its court setting: courtiers modulated all their social behaviors, and galant music follows a parallel code (OUP abstract of the introduction; Neuwirth 402; Adams 101 [via review]). Adams reports Gjerdingen's view that J. S. Bach wrote galant music when it suited him and his patrons (101 [via review]). Two secondary sources quote Gjerdingen on a repertory of "stock musical phrases" as a hallmark of the style (Gjerdingen 6, cited by Gotham and Shaffer and by Pedrero-Encabo 4 [via review]).

From chapter 2, "The Romanesca" (25–43) [read]:

- Patrons learned the schemata passively by listening; composers learned them actively from maestros (25).
- A partimento is an instructional bass for a virtual ensemble, realized at the keyboard (25).
- Students memorized the currently fashionable schemata by rote and realized them in every key, meter, tempo, and style (26, 32).
- The Neapolitan conservatories taught with partimenti; Gjerdingen cites Cimarosa's notebook from Santa Maria di Loreto (30). A Neapolitan solfeggio is a two-voice piece teaching melody within the schemata of its companion partimento (40).
- The Romanesca was noticed and named in the sixteenth century and was a common ground bass in the seventeenth, as in Pachelbel's Canon (27–28). The leaping-bass variant is typical of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, the stepwise variant of the later eighteenth, and a galant hybrid has a 6/3 at the fourth stage (28–33).

Fonte, Monte, and Ponte are Riepel's names; Indugio, Quiescenza, and Passo Indietro are Gjerdingen's; Meyer, Cudworth, and Fenaroli are named after people (Neuwirth 402–3 [via review]).

The schema prototypes below are in scale degrees, from Gotham's *Open Music Theory* digest [via review]. Check them against Gjerdingen's "Schema Prototypes" appendix (453–64) before teaching.

| schema | position | melody | bass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Romanesca | opening | 1–5–1–1 | 1–7–6–3 |
| Do-Re-Mi | opening | 1–2–3 | 1–7–1 |
| Meyer | opening | 1–7–4–3 | 1–2–7–1 |
| Prinner | continuation | 6–5–4–3 | 4–3–2–1 |
| Fonte | continuation | 5–4–4–3 | ♯1–2–7–1 |
| Monte | continuation | 1–♭7–6–2–1–7 | 3–4–♯4–5 |
| Ponte | continuation | 5–7–2 | 5 pedal |
| Fenaroli | pre-cadential | 4–3–7–1 | 7–1–2–3 |
| Indugio | pre-cadential | 2–4–6–1–7 | 4–4–4–♯4–5 |
| Quiescenza | post-cadential | ♭7–6–7–1 | 1 pedal |

The digest also lists the Sol-Fa-Mi, Aprile, Jupiter, and Pastorella openings, the Passo Indietro, Comma, and converging cadence, and the cadenza semplice, composta, and doppia. The Fonte and Monte rows compress two-stage sequences, the Fonte descending from a minor stage to a major one and the Monte ascending.

Neuwirth's criticisms: the terminology is heterogeneous and many names are arbitrary (403); schema identification has no explicit criteria, and a Prinner is sometimes labeled on a bare 6–5–4–3 soprano (406); Prinner and stepwise Romanesca can be told apart only by formal function (406); the method neglects a schema's role in the movement, such as the Fonte in sonata form, and he proposes Ulrich Kaiser's "Modell" as a complement (407–8) [via review]. Neuwirth also notes that the analyses of Beethoven and Chopin show schemata outliving the century (408). Adams finds the relation between galant surface and contrapuntal training an unanswered question (104 [via review]). Holtmeier's review in *Eighteenth-Century Music* (2011) wasn't read.

From Byros's review of Sanguinetti (2012) [via review]: partimento is an instructional bass, figured or unfigured, tied to the four Neapolitan conservatories: Santa Maria di Loreto, the Pietà dei Turchini, Sant'Onofrio, and the Poveri di Gesù Cristo (para. 3). Its teachers run from Pasquini and Alessandro Scarlatti to Tritto and Valente, with Fenaroli's *Regole* (1775) and the pupils of Durante and of Leo (paras. 7, 13). Partimenti absorbed outside styles, and the practice reached the rest of Europe, as in Choron's 1809 collection, with Haydn and Beethoven among its subscribers (para. 15).

Grey areas:

- A schema Gjerdingen treats as galant has documented sixteenth- and seventeenth-century ancestry, so the Romanesca crosses all three labels (inference from Gjerdingen 27–33).
- Partimento teaching, from late-seventeenth-century Roman and Neapolitan masters to Fenaroli and Choron, continues across the textbook division of Baroque, galant, and Classical (inference from Byros).

## where the galant begins

Heartz (2003) places the galant's origins in the 1720s, in Neapolitan opera (Vinci, Pergolesi, Hasse) and Venetian instrumental music (Vivaldi), and organizes the book by city, Naples to Madrid (Lockwood [via review]). WorldCat's summary has Dresden under Hasse as the foremost centre of Italian opera in Germany [via review]. Heartz downplays Bach and Handel to read the era on its own terms; Lockwood answers that both knew the galant trends and the contrasts aren't black and white [via review]. Heartz credits Corelli with a role in developing and spreading the galant (101), and says the term minuet approaches synonymy with galant (23) (both via Pedrero-Encabo [via review]). Perutková calls the book mainly a summary (127 n. 5 [via review]).

Burnett dates the style galant to the 1730s and 1740s (160) and sonata form from the late 1730s (117 n. 20). Wikipedia, citing Bukofzer (17ff.), has the Italians turning to the galant around 1730 while J. S. Bach kept to the Baroque until 1750 [lead]; Wikipedia, citing Schulenberg's *Music of the Baroque* (2001), describes a gradual simplification of texture leading to the galant [lead].

Scheibe's critique of J. S. Bach is in *Der critische Musikus*, no. 6, which Hauge cites from the collected edition (Hamburg, 1739) [read]; the year 1737 is from a weak web source. Scheibe called Bach's style bombastic and confused and said an excess of art darkened his music; Scheibe, with Mattheson and Telemann, wrote for the amateur public, the homme galant, and Hauge calls his aesthetic and Bach's largely incompatible [read]. Buelow calls Scheibe's words mild aesthetic disagreement, taken as an insult by Bach's circle; the exchange runs through Birnbaum (1738), Scheibe's *Beantwortung* (1738), Birnbaum again (1739), and Schröter in Mizler's *Musikalische Bibliothek* (1752) (Buelow abstract [via review]). Perutková adds that Scheibe held the new style to be in many ways better than the old (129 [via review]).

## disagreements

1. Did modes evolve into keys? No: Barnett (408, 427, 435, 450), Powers (467). A reduction from eight modes to two, completed 1713: Hyer (738). Teleologically yes: Burnett (xix, 94).
2. When does key-centered tonality begin? Josquin (Dahlhaus, via Hyer 734); fauxbourdon (Besseler, via Hyer 747); Monteverdi c. 1600 (Fétis, via Hyer 729); England by mid-century and France in the 1660s (Barnett 430, 435, 441); north Italian instrumental music of the 1670s–80s, with Corelli (Burnett 47, 89, 132); Mattheson 1713 (Hyer 738); Rameau as the first theorist of the interacting components (Lester 753).
3. Bononcini (1673): twelve modes against eight (Barnett 414 n. 23, 415); the twelve reducible to four, two major and two minor (Burnett 115 n. 19, after Stein). Check *Musico prattico* before teaching either.
4. Fux and inversion: Fux explains the inversional relation of 5/3 and 6/3 (Lester 755); Fux doesn't fully acknowledge inversion, and a 6/3 is a triad abandoned (Bent 570).
5. Continuity or rupture in seventeenth-century practice: the same framework for nearly two hundred years (Schubert 504); a radical reorientation of pedagogy (Wason 53); continuo-derived counterpoint by mid-century (Burnett 93 n. 5).
6. Triads before or after continuo: from continuo on, music heard as harmonies (Hyer 739); triadic recognition precedes continuo and comes from other traditions (Lester 755; Schubert 505, 525, 527).
7. Rule of the Octave: precursors from 1607 to 1711 (Barnett 442–44); canonized by François Campion, 1716 (Lester 756). Lester's Campion is François (d. 1748); Schubert's and Barnett's is Thomas (c. 1618).
8. Phrase theory's starting point: Riepel (Caplin 671); Mattheson (McCreless 870); Mattheson and Riepel, with Koch most systematic (Wason 57).
9. Symmetry as the galant norm: yes (Burnett 160, 164, 185); a Romantic distortion of Koch (Caplin 674–75).
10. Ritornello and later form: the early symphony read out of the ritornello concerto (Burnett 153, 158, 175–76); ritornello "facts" imposed from later models (Dreyfus 334).
11. Divisions of eighteenth-century theory: three national traditions blending by the century's end (Wason 59); two lines, Catholic Italian and Protestant Berlin (Bent 581).
12. Listenius and musica poetica: *Rudimenta musicae*, 1533 (Wason 52–53; A. Cohen 535); *Musica*, 1537 (McCreless 853).
13. Thomas Campion's date: c. 1618 and c. 1610 (Schubert 505, 527); c. 1613 and c. 1618 (Barnett 436, 438). Each source is internally inconsistent.
14. Who brought "Baroque" into musicology: Sachs 1919 (the usual credit, and Palisca's); Wellesz 1909/10 (Wilfing-Albrecht; Martín Sáez 30 n. 45, whose earlier author the fetch tool rendered as "Dent").
15. Palisca's span for "Baroque": c. 1540–1730 (Martín Sáez 35) against 1600–1750 (Wikipedia). Resolve in the Grove article.
16. Bukofzer's phases: 1580–1630, 1630–80, 1680–1730 (Feraru; Wikipedia's prose). Wikipedia's phase table gives 1580–1650, 1630–1700, 1680–1750 without citation.
17. Braccino's first *Discorso*: 1605 (Fuchs 455); "1606?" (Carter).
18. Galant as a style: a style with origins in 1720s Naples and Venice (Heartz); a theoretical antithesis to tradition more than a style (Sheldon, via Aspden 10).
19. C. P. E. Bach: empfindsam (the modern label); mannerist (Berg); Sturm und Drang (Eggebrecht, Hoffmann-Erbrecht); none of these terms musical in his lifetime (Marggraf; Schulenberg).
