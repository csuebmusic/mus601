## lester, "rameau and eighteenth-century harmonic theory" (pp. 753–77) · 1555 to 1817

Chapter 24 of the Cambridge History, directly after Hyer's "Tonality" (unit 2). Sections: seventeenth-century antecedents; Rameau; Rameau's legacy.

**what the chapter claims**

- Each component of harmonic tonality arose before tonal syntax did, in a separate tradition with its own pedagogy: tertian harmony, voice leading, the bass as foundation, root generation, cadential direction, diatonic and chromatic interaction, harmony against meter (p. 753).
- Rameau was the first to recognize that these components interact to create tonality. "A major part" of his theorizing, in treatises from 1722 to 1761, recombines long-standing concepts into one directional perspective (p. 753).
- Rameau was a major composer, and his theory answers to music as he understood it compositionally. When speculation and practice conflicted he invoked "license" (pp. 753, 772).
- Later eighteenth-century theory bequeathed a preoccupation with verticalities over linear factors and no framework for relating local harmonic structure to phrasing and form (p. 774).

**the antecedents (pp. 754–59)**

- Zarlino (1558) defines a harmony's quality by the third above the bass, but as a rule of thumb for avoiding improper sonorities. For him C–E–A modifies C–E–G; it isn't an inversion of A–C–E. The tenor, not the bass, governs his structure (p. 754).
- Avianius (1581) treats single intervals as parts of chords. Burmeister (1599) and Magirus (1611) treat 5/3 and 6/3 as the only consonant harmonies. Iberian guitar *alfabeto* codifies the same chords (pp. 754–55).
- Harnisch (1608) treats 6/3 and 6/4 as rearrangements of 5/3. Lippius (c. 1610) names all three *trias harmonica*, on the model of the Trinity. Fux (1725) explains the 5/3–6/3 inversion at the start of three-part species (p. 755).
- Thorough bass conceives chords from the bass, not a root, but keyboard practice made players aware that one right-hand chord fits several basses. Keller (1705) gives C–E–G over C, E6, G6/4, and also over A7, which isn't inversionally related (pp. 755–56).
- Praetorius (1619): a 5/3 over a bass B contains a diminished fifth, so the figure over B is 6/3. Generalized, the figure over any bass solmized *mi* is 6/3, including the leading tone, the mediant of a major key, and secondary leading tones. Penna (1672) and Heinichen (1711, 1728) give rules for unfigured basses; Heinichen separates pattern rules from scale-step rules (p. 756).
- Campion's Rule of the Octave (1716) is a normative harmonization of ascending and descending major and melodic-minor scales, Ex. 24.1 (pp. 756–57). The rules codify norms but aren't a theory of harmony (p. 757).
- Vicentino (1555) and Zarlino (1558) recognize that some voice combinations create so strong an expectation of a cadential goal that its absence registers: "evading the cadence." In a 7–6 suspension before a cadential octave, the sixth resolves the seventh but drives on toward the octave rather than ending the motion (pp. 757–58).
- Keys come to be defined by the quality of the triad on the final, not only by final and octave species. Lippius first argues that the essential difference between modes is that triad. German traditions defend the modes well into the eighteenth century (p. 758).
- Descartes (c. 1618) derives consonances from a generating string: the fifth is direct, the fourth its "shadow." The same reasoning makes the major third primary and can't generate a minor third as the lowest interval of a triad, a problem never resolved. Mersenne on unity generating diversity; Sauveur's overtone series in the early eighteenth century (pp. 758–59).

**rameau's system (pp. 759–63)**

- Four premises, from the *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722) and revised over forty years. The root-position triad and the seventh chord are the source of all harmonies. The root (*son fondamental*) generates them. Progression is a succession of roots, the *basse fondamentale*, with voice leading as the connection of chord tones. The fundamental bass is directed motion that yields key, because its motions match cadences (pp. 759–60).
- The premises explain why the fifth in a 6/5 chord is dissonant (it's the seventh above the root) and why the "irregular fourth" of V4/3, held as a common tone, behaves as it does (it's the dominant's fundamental) (p. 760).
- Book I derives all chords from divisions of the monochord. It can't generate the minor third, justify octave identity, or derive the diminished seventh (pp. 760–61).
- In Book II the fundamental bass isn't meant to be played. Dissonance, above all the seventh, is the motor of progression, on an Enlightenment model of material causation. The first notated example in the *Traité* is the *cadence parfaite*, written on separate staves per voice (p. 761; Plate 24.1, p. 762).
- The *dominante-tonique* has two dissonances: the seventh ("minor dissonance," descends) and the leading tone ("major dissonance," ascends). The tonic has none. The dominant seventh defines the following chord as tonic, so a key is a focus that emerges from the progression, not a given pitch field (pp. 761–62).
- The *cadence irrégulière* is IV with added sixth to I, a rising fifth in the fundamental bass, Plate 24.2 (pp. 762–63).
- All music is a series of interconnected cadences, most evaded: one or both chords inverted, a seventh or added sixth on the goal chord, or a minor third in the dominant seventh, which makes it a plain "dominant" rather than a *dominante-tonique* (p. 763).

**supposition, irregular motion, double emploi (pp. 764–68)**

- Every dissonance should behave like a seventh. A ninth chord on G is a seventh chord on B with G "supposed" below. A 4–3 suspension A–D–E is a seventh chord on E with A supposed, so the resolving fourth is a concealed seventh resolving by falling fifth, Ex. 24.2. Supposition became a contested point in later controversies over the theory (pp. 764–65).
- Root motion by third yields successive consonant triads, each of which would then be a tonic. Only in later writings does Rameau separate a *ton régnant* from local tonicizations (p. 765).
- Root motion by step is explained by an interpolated fundamental bass: in Ex. 24.3 an A is inserted between C and D so that both moves are falling fifths. For IV to V, the subdominant's added sixth is reread as a supertonic seventh, *double emploi*, which gives I–ii6/5–V7 with no stepwise root motion (pp. 765–66). The term dates from the *Nouveau système* (1726) (p. 768).

**rameau's analyses (pp. 766–68)**

- His own vocal fugue (*Traité*, Book III, chapter 44); a monologue from Lully's *Armide*, twice, with different explanations (1726, 1754) (p. 766).
- Ex. 24.3 (*Traité*, Book III, chapter 41) sets a bass to a sarabande-like treble. All but three root motions are cadential fourths and fifths; the three are the *double emploi* progressions in mm. 2 and 5 and the stepwise motion into m. 7 (p. 767). The analysis decides where cadences conclude (mm. 4, 8) and where they're voiced to keep the phrase moving. In the appoggiatura dominant of m. 6, the seventh of the D7, held over as an inner-voice C, motivates the progression (pp. 766–68).

**after the *traité* (pp. 768–72)**

- The *sous-dominante* and *double emploi* (1726) become central in the *Génération harmonique* (1737): the tonic flanked by an upper and lower fifth, the "triple proportion" F:C:G = 1:3:9. Three functions, though Rameau never uses the word: tonic triad; dominant, descends by fifth, has a seventh; subdominant, ascends by fifth, has an added sixth. The added sixth is the dominant's added third placed below the subdominant (p. 768; Fig. 24.1, p. 769).
- The model changes from mechanical dissonance-resolution to the tonic's gravitational pull, on Newton (p. 768 n. 13).
- In the *Démonstration du principe de l'harmonie* (1750), Lester's Plate 24.3 (Rameau's Plate C), the major scale is derived from harmony: two tetrachords C–F and G–C, each harmonized in triple proportion, F–C–G for the lower and C–G–D for the upper. Lester calls it Rameau's reformulation of the Rule of the Octave, from a thorough-bass scale to the harmonic basis of a melodic scale. The scale arises from harmony, the position Rameau defends against Rousseau (p. 769; plate, p. 770).
- Sauveur's demonstration that the harmonic division of a string is audible in its resonance (the *corps sonore*) moved Rameau from a Cartesian deductive system (1722 preface) to a Newtonian empirical one: music as "a physico-mathematical science" (1737) (pp. 769–70).
- The *corps sonore* can't generate the minor triad. Rameau proposed an undertone series and dropped it when d'Alembert showed it illusory. Nor can it explain the subdominant, which isn't in the tonic's overtone series; in the triple proportion the subdominant, not the tonic, is the lowest term (p. 771).
- The philosophes: Voltaire, Rousseau, Diderot, d'Alembert's précis of 1752. Rameau ended his life perceived as defensive and, late, mystical (p. 771). Tartini (1754) proposed the third sound (Lester prints *terzo tuono*) as an alternative source and also failed to derive both triads (pp. 771–72). Serre (1753) and Roussier (1765) attack the practitioner-theorist (p. 772).

**legacy (pp. 772–74)**

- The fundamental bass spread without attribution: Heinichen (1728), Lampe (1737), C. P. E. Bach, who denounced Rameau but opened his 1762 thorough-bass manual with 5/3, 6/3, 6/4 (p. 772).
- Kirnberger separates "essential" sevenths, unprepared, from "accidental" dissonances, prepared. Marpurg (1755) on harmonic versus passing and changing notes. General terms for nonharmonic tones appear only in the nineteenth century (p. 773).
- Kirnberger and Nichelmann analyze Bach with the fundamental bass. Mozart taught harmony and composition with it to two pupils in the mid-1780s; there's no note, and the chapter bibliography lists the Ployer and Attwood study editions (pp. 773, 776). Koch's "species counterpoint" is a graded series of chord vocabularies. Albrechtsberger, Beethoven's teacher in the early 1790s, recasts Fux in 1790 so that the student first finds the harmonies under the cantus firmus (pp. 773–74).
- Symbols: Trydell's "harmonical figures" (1766), Vogler's few Roman numerals soon after, Weber's (1817), hence *Stufentheorie*. Tonic-dominant-subdominant thinking in Schröter, Sorge, Daube; function theory only with Riemann. Austrian theorists keep the fundamental bass into the nineteenth century, and Lester traces *Stufentheorie*, the fundamental bass, and function theory all to Rameau (p. 774).

**what unit 3 takes from it**

- The vocabulary the Classical composers were taught: root progression, cadence as the model of all motion, the *dominante-tonique*, the subdominant as the dominant's mirror. Lester reports, without a note, that Mozart taught with it (p. 773).
- Rameau's key as a focus that emerges from cadential progression. Set it against Rosen's polarized keys and Hepokoski's tonic projected outward ("Beyond," p. 118) when students describe how the second key is reached.
- Lester's closing gap, no theory relating harmony to phrase and form, is what Hepokoski's Sonata Theory addresses: the EEC and ESC define formal events by cadence.
- The triple proportion and the *Démonstration* fundamental basses (F, C, G; C, G, D) are adjacent fifths, the arrangement Burnett's hexachords use. The unit 2 synthesis records the same line-of-fifths layout in Hauptmann.
- Sechter's 1820 analysis of the "Jupiter" finale (Brown n. 3) is a Rameau-lineage analysis of the Paper 3 work, from the Austrian fundamental-bass tradition Lester names (p. 774).

## schmid, "mozart and haydn," translated by ernest sanders (pp. 145–61) · 1732 to 1809

*Musical Quarterly*, April 1956, the bicentenary year. The essay has no footnotes, and two plates are between pp. 156 and 157: Barbara Krafft's 1819 Mozart portrait and a page of Leopold Mozart's letter of February 14, 1785.

**what the essay claims**

- "Viennese Classicism" is an inadequate label for two composers whose lives enclose each other: Haydn's seventy-seven years contain Mozart's whole career, and his best-known works postdate Mozart's death (pp. 145, 149).
- The two aren't rooted in the same traditions. Haydn had direct contact with the Viennese Baroque (Fux, Caldara, his predecessor Werner); Mozart received the Baroque through his father and, later, through van Swieten (pp. 145–46).
- Influence ran from Haydn to Mozart until the early 1780s and then in both directions. Mozart's influences on Haydn "are less notable than those reflecting the reverse situation," and Haydn's late style is "essentially autogenous" (pp. 154, 159, 161).

**backgrounds (pp. 146–50)**

- Haydn's folk music comes from Rohrau's Leitha district: German, Croatian, Hungarian, and Gypsy. Mozart's comes only as stylized student songs, guild songs, and dances in Leopold's notebooks, from the milieu of Rathgeber's *Tafelkonfekt* (pp. 146–48).
- Haydn puts folk material in his most ambitious works, the London symphonies; Mozart keeps it for cassations and divertimentos (p. 148).
- Character is read from portraits and birthplaces: Haydn's "peasant stock," slow maturity, likened to Bruckner, his mature works coming in his forties; Mozart's refinement and early genius (pp. 148–49). Mozart's minuets are courtly; Haydn's trios are near the Ländler (pp. 149–50).

**contacts, documented and proposed (pp. 150–59)**

- Haydn's symphony "No. 13 in the Breitkopf Gesamtausgabe," dated 1763 in the manuscript, uses the four-note motive of the "Jupiter" finale in its finale, with varied counterpoints and a four-part stretto like that of Mozart's F-major Mass Credo (1774). The motive appears in the slow movement of Mozart's first symphony (London, early 1765). Schmid proposes that Mozart saw Haydn's symphony in Paris in 1764, where both composers were first printed that year, or through Michael Haydn, appointed conductor of the Salzburg court orchestra in 1762 (pp. 150–51).
- Mozart's later uses of the motive: Sanctus of the C-major Mass (1776), B-flat Symphony (1779), E-flat Violin Sonata (1785), the "Jupiter" (p. 151).
- Haydn's Capriccio on *Acht Sauschneider müssen sein* (autograph 1765) and Mozart's *Galimathias musicum* (1766) set the same tune (pp. 151–52).
- Leopold's letter to his wife from Verona, August 1771, asks that Nannerl find a Haydn trio in F and a C-major trio labeled "Wagenseil" for a friend in Milan (p. 152).
- Op. 20 (1772), "probably under the influence of" Werner, reverts to Baroque procedure: three fugal finales, the first violin's leadership reduced. Gassmann's fugal quartet finales follow a year later. Mozart's six Viennese quartets of 1773 adopt the contrapuntal texture and the minuet as third movement; the first one's Andante uses the theme Haydn uses in the fugal finale of Op. 20 no. 5, in the same key (pp. 152–53).
- Haydn's symphonies of the 1770s move from severe counterpoint to integrated part-writing and thematic development. Mozart jotted the themes of Haydn's nos. 47, 62, and 75 on the back of an autograph from the early 1780s. Pohl hears no. 62's Andante in the letter duet of *Figaro*; Geiringer hears Mozart's influence in no. 75 (pp. 153–54).
- Opera: Haydn's *L'incontro improvviso* and *Die Entführung*; Haydn's letter to a Prague music-lover after *Don Giovanni* (October 1787), in which he cedes opera buffa to Mozart (p. 155).
- Meeting places: the houses of Kees, where Haydn's symphonies of the 1780s were rehearsed; Greiner; Storace, where Kelly heard a quartet of Haydn, Dittersdorf, Mozart, and Vanhal (pp. 156–57). Schmid notes "some doubt" whether Mozart visited the Genzingers, then lists their house with the others (p. 157).
- The "Haydn" quartets (1782–85), modeled on Op. 33; Haydn's remark to Leopold in February 1785; "I have learned from Haydn how to write quartets"; the rebuke to Koželuch (pp. 158–59).
- Shared exposure to Baroque music through van Swieten: Mozart's C-minor Mass and Haydn's St. Cecilia Mass (p. 159).
- In 1788 Mozart wrote his last three symphonies while Haydn, "having just finished his Paris symphonies," was working on the "Oxford" (p. 159).

**after 1791 (pp. 159–61)**

- Mozart's farewell to Haydn in December 1790; Haydn's letters to Genzinger and Puchberg on Mozart's death; advice to Broderip to buy the manuscripts (pp. 159–60).
- Mozartean traits in late Haydn: the bass motive of the *Nelson Mass* Qui tollis after the *Requiem* Tuba mirum; wind writing and instrumental individualization (p. 161).

**the essay as a source**

- No quotation or claim is sourced. Each needs tracing to an edition before a student cites it: the Beethoven deathbed remark (p. 146); "Haydn always wears peasant stockings" (p. 149); Leopold's letter of August 1771 (p. 152); Haydn's 1805 remark on Werner (p. 152); Haydn on associating with emperors (p. 154); the Prague letter (p. 155); Haydn's letters to Genzinger of 1789 and from London (p. 157); Kelly's quartet (p. 157); Leopold's letter of February 1785 and Haydn's remark in it (p. 158); "I have learned from Haydn" and the Koželuch rebuke (pp. 158–59); "I fear, my father" (p. 160); the Puchberg letter and Broderip (p. 160); Carpani; "violin quartets and the Requiem"; Niemtschek's dedication (p. 161).
- The one documentary pointer is Larsen's published facsimile of the Kees catalogue of Haydn symphonies (p. 156).
- The character portraits from physiognomy and birthplace are the essay's method in its first half, and the essay is itself a document of 1950s reception.
- The influence claims cite proximity and plausibility: a print in Paris, a conduit in Michael Haydn, a tune in common. Schmid labels several as assumptions (pp. 151–52) or hedges them: "although there is no proof, it may well be" (p. 155), "it may be assumed" (p. 156).

**what unit 3 takes from it**

- A chronology of contact for Weeks 10–12, from Haydn's early symphonies through the Paris set to 1788.
- A test case in evaluating a secondary source: what kind of evidence each claim has, and what a student would have to verify before citing it. Brown answers several of the same questions with different evidence (see "where the readings collide").
- Schmid names three Paris symphonies (*La Reine*, *L'Ours*, *La Poule*) and says nothing on 84 or 90.

## brown, "eighteenth-century traditions and mozart's 'jupiter' symphony k.551" (pp. 157–95) · 1697 to 1791

*Journal of Musicology*, Spring 2003, published after Brown's death; a revision of a paper for the Hofstra Mozart conference of February 1991 (p. 157). The JSTOR cover title reads "Traditional"; the article's own title page reads "Traditions."

**what the article argues**

- Analyses of K.551 treat it in isolation. Brown places it in its musical context: Viennese court traditions back to Charles VI, composers alive in the 1780s, and the circumstances of 1788 (pp. 158–59).
- The first movement belongs to the Viennese C-major trumpet overture and symphony. The slow movement is a French keyboard sarabande, the minuet is of the serious type with a mixed trio, and the finale is a contrapuntal acclamation, an Alleluia or Gloria followed by fanfare. Together they meet "every Viennese celebratory requirement" (abstract, pp. 194–95).
- The symphony's departures from celebratory affect (Sturm und Drang, a buffa quotation, a duel) are ordered under "a consistent hierarchy of affect" (p. 170). Sulzer's and Koch's *Charakter* is the eighteenth-century category that fits (pp. 192–94).
- The symphony suits a victory celebration after the Turkish War; it may have gone unheard in Mozart's Vienna because the occasion came only with the peace of August 1791 (pp. 190–92).

**the viennese background (pp. 158–63)**

- Revisions to the biography: Mozart had cash-flow problems rather than poverty, partly from the war's general lull; the G-minor Symphony as personal anguish has been reconsidered; the trilogy was meant for a subscription series in autumn or winter 1788 (pp. 158–59).
- Scholarship has linked Mozart to North Germany (Handel, the Bachs) and to an anti-Mozart court. Against this: the court theaters staged his operas; he succeeded Gluck as Kammermusicus (November 1788); Salieri respected him and lent him old scores from the court library (Hüttenbrenner) (pp. 159–60).
- Documented study: Froberger's hexachord Fantasia arranged in 1782; copies of Reutter's church music, one page also with sketches for the "Jupiter" finale (Tyson) (pp. 160–61).
- Mozart's composition students in 1785–87 included Attwood, Ployer, and Freystädtler. Mann ties the teaching to Fux's *Gradus* and Haydn's "Elementarbuch"; Brown calls it "Fuxian strict as well as free composition" (p. 161).
- Kilian Rheinhardt's *Rubriche generali*, the ceremonial calendar of Charles VI's chapel (pp. 161–62). The court opera overtures (Fux, Caldara, Reutter, Bonno, Wagenseil) are scored for one or two choirs of trumpets with timpani (p. 162).
- The court trumpet corps numbered sixteen trumpeters and two timpanists in 1721 and was gone by 1776. In the Classical orchestra only fanfare formulas remain, and they survive as a series of works with martial themes, of which the "Jupiter" is "a direct outgrowth" (p. 162).
- Trumpet sinfonias are almost all in C; the Italian opera sinfonia prefers D. Mozart's opera sinfonias: D major or minor eleven times, C five, G and E-flat once each. Among them only the *Clemenza di Tito* overture returns to the trumpeting style (pp. 162–63).
- Of Mozart's seven C-major concert symphonies, five predate Vienna; among them only K.338's first movement trumpets. Of the last six, the first movements of K.425 and K.551 are saturated with fanfare rhythm (p. 163).

**first movement (pp. 163–70)**

- Mozart places fanfares after the opening flourish as refrain closings to P, T, and K: Pᵏ, Tᵏ, Kᵏ (p. 163). The same material closing each section occurs "in no other example known to me" (p. 163 n. 27).
- Refrains in the old trumpet sinfonias are cyclic binders between movements (Ariosti's sinfonia to *Le profezie d'Eliseo*, 1705, with improvised trumpets, Fig. 2) and, more often, first-movement ritornellos. Fanfare refrains after an opening statement are rare: in Vienna only Caldara's Introduzione to *Enone* (1734), Fig. 3; outside the trumpet repertoire Kraus's C-minor Symphony, which "dates in part from" Kraus's Vienna stay of April to November 1783 (pp. 163, 166 and n. 30).
- Three departures from the imperial affect. The first is the switch to a C-minor Sturm und Drang within S, Ex. 1, mm. 81–86 (models: K.338 mm. 13–14; Haydn 48 mm. 12–14 and 51–63). These episodes are parenthetical: removing them would leave a monolithic movement, "at the cost of high drama" (p. 166).
- K, mm. 101–10, quotes Mozart's insertion aria "Un bacio di mano," K.541, for Anfossi's *Le gelosie fortunate*: "You are a bit innocent, my dear Pompeo; go study the ways of the world" (Zaslaw's translation, n. 32). A private allusion; Brown speculates that Pompeo is Joseph II (p. 168).
- The development combines Pᵏ with a descending chromatic bass, mm. 171–80, Ex. 4a, unique in eighteenth-century symphonies; its one Mozart analogue is the duel in the *Don Giovanni* Introduzione, Ex. 4b. Brown asks whether it alludes to battle music in the dynastic operas (p. 170).

**second movement (pp. 170–75)**

- A French sarabande with second-beat emphasis, Ex. 5. Mozart uses the pattern in the slow movement of K.453 but not in his earlier symphonic slow movements; it's "virtually unknown" in the Viennese symphony after 1750, absent in Gassmann, Ordonez, Vanhal, and Dittersdorf; vestiges in a few Wagenseil symphonies; among pre-1750 C-major court trumpet sinfonias only Johann Georg Rheinhardt's overture to *La più bella* (1715) has one (pp. 170–73).
- The tradition is keyboard, as the 32nd-note swirls that fill the silences of mm. 2 and 4 suggest. Models available in Vienna: Bach's English Suite BWV 806 (added motion in mm. 10, 12, 20, 24), "perhaps the closest parallel"; Fux; and Muffat's *Componimenti musicali* (c. 1739), suite 3, "more convincing as a model" (pp. 173–74; Ex. 6, p. 175).
- Non-sarabande passages: the transition and development with dissonance and hemiola; a galant secondary theme (Sadie: Gluck's "Che puro ciel"); recapitulation roulades that make keyboard demands of the bass parts. The affect stays high-style throughout: Mattheson's *grandezza*, Sulzer's "noblest triple gestures," both quoted from Allanbrook (p. 174 and nn. 41–42).

**minuet (pp. 174–77)**

- Reichardt's two types, serious and gay. The minuet is serious: downbeat start, descending chromatic material, fanfare, canonic episodes (pp. 174–76; Fig. 4, p. 177).
- The trio opens with a consequent before its antecedent, a buffa twist of syntax that replays the circular strophic form of the song "Sei du mein Trost," K.391. After the double bar a long-note theme anticipates the finale's opening. The trio mixes gay and serious, where the norm puts serious in the minuet and gay in the trio (p. 176).

**finale (pp. 176–90)**

- Contrapuntal finales have a theatrical and symphonic history: Fux's trumpet overtures (1709, 1716, 1722, 1731) "contain finale fugues or have strong fugal elements." Table 1 lists contrapuntal symphony movements before 1788 by Ordonez, Gassmann, Vanhal, Schacht, Reicha, and others (pp. 176–78).
- Richter: nine of his 83 probably authentic symphonies have movements titled fugue or fugato; five of the seven available combine sonata functions, binary repeats, and fugal texture (Table 2, p. 179). Kirkendale: Richter alone, like Mozart, adds further entries after the opening fugato. No direct parallel beyond the sonata-fugue synthesis (pp. 177–79).
- Ordonez's Symphony D5, finale of a seven-movement "Sinfonia solenne" (probably 1760s): long-note antecedent, sonata orientation, and a structural climax at the recapitulation, Ex. 7. Mozart puts his peroration in the coda (pp. 178–79).
- Gassmann's fugue ends with the subject in octaves, the standard Viennese close and the opposite of Mozart's (p. 179). Vanhal's G-major finale lacks Gassmann's austerity and reshapes its subject midway, Ex. 8 (pp. 179–80).
- Joseph Haydn's contrapuntal finales: nos. 14, 3, 40, 70 (Sonnleithner's "framed" fugue), 5, 52. No. 13's finale opens with the same pitches as the "Jupiter" theme, Ex. 9, hints at Fux's third species at the outset, and otherwise largely avoids counterpoint; its source distribution and treatment make it an unlikely model (p. 180).
- Michael Haydn as the likelier source. The fugal finale of P.43/MH 287 (c. 1780–81) survives for 45 measures in Mozart's hand and opens with the "Jupiter" pitches reordered. Two motives of P.19/MH 384 (September 1784, Artaria) are "remarkably similar" to Mozart's: one of the motifs of the coda's quintuple fugue (m. 76) and the closing theme at m. 115 "owe something" to it, Ex. 10. Mozart's knowledge of P.31/MH 478 (finished February 19, 1788) "is more open to question," with no Viennese distribution in the sources; it shares dotted fanfares, a diminished-seventh Sturm und Drang moment, and the combination of sonata and counterpoint (Table 3, p. 185) (pp. 180–85).
- The C–D–F–E theme. Hyatt King traces it to Palestrina, Klenz through Mozart, Wollenberg to the hymn "Lucis creator" and Fux. Zaslaw and Klenz tie it to the Credo of K.192 as a statement of faith; Brown rejects that, since K.192 was known only in Salzburg and no Credo chant begins with these pitches (pp. 185–86).
- Brown's candidates are the Gloria of Mass IV in the *Liber usualis*, used by Michael Haydn in the *Missa Sancti Aloysii*, and a family of Alleluia chants with Viennese connections, used by Michael Haydn in the gradual "Qui sedes" (September 1, 1787). Mozart's letter of August 2, 1788, asks Nannerl to borrow Michael Haydn's two Tutti-masses and the gradual, eight days before K.551 entered his catalogue. Brown leans to the Alleluia, "marginally" (pp. 186–87).
- In the "Jupiter," unlike Mozart's other uses, the theme is "not hidden or of secondary importance" but "the foundation upon which an entire movement stands" (p. 187).
- The finale is acclamation (Pa) answered by fanfare (Pᵏ), each functional unit ending in a fanfare, Fig. 5 and Ex. 14, after imperial processions in which vocal acclamations were followed by a trumpet *Tusch*. Pᵏ is treated in canon and stretto, Ex. 15, mm. 332–44. First-movement allusions recur diminished: *minore* Sturm und Drang at mm. 127 and 335 in the text, the duel at m. 233 (pp. 187–90).

**occasion and character (pp. 190–94)**

- Summer 1788: Vienna emptied by the season and the war; the *Wiener-Zeitung* and its war supplements. K.543 is the most normal of the three; K.550's sighs and pathopoeia fit a public mood if the war continued, and its clarinet revision suggests a contemporary performance; K.551 is for "solemn celebration," with its battle music and Sturm und Drang "perhaps a recollection of the high costs" (p. 190).
- The "Jupiter" recalls ceremonies after the earlier Turkish campaign (1697–1707); trumpets, counterpoint, and solemnity emblematize the house of Austria (pp. 190–91).
- Sulzer (1771, in Robert T. Landon's translation, n. 69): a composition must have a fixed character; the composer may emphasize a figure outside it. Koch: retaining a *Charakter* is "one of the most important requirements." Mozart arranges characteristic styles hierarchically under one ceremonial statement (pp. 192–94).

## hepokoski, "back and forth from egmont: beethoven, mozart, and the nonresolving recapitulation" (pp. 127–54) · 1773 to 1810, with later examples

*19th-Century Music* 25, nos. 2–3. It's a companion to "Beyond the Sonata Principle" (p. 127 n. 1), and both preview Hepokoski and Darcy's *Elements of Sonata Theory*, cited as forthcoming.

**what the essay argues**

- A nonresolving recapitulation is a rhetorical recapitulation, parallel in layout to the exposition, crafted to "fail" in its generic mission of tonal closure. It's rare around 1800, easy to identify, and hard to interpret (p. 128).
- Rhetorical and tonal structures don't coincide in such pieces. Closure deferred to a coda is closure outside sonata space, in a zone not historically made for it (p. 134).
- A sonata form is a set of norms that a piece invites listeners to measure it against. Form resides in that dialogue, not on the page or the acoustic surface; a piece can be "both a sonata and not a sonata" (p. 135).
- The eighteenth-century sonata can be read as a metaphor for a successfully carried-out human action, with the ESC as the tonic's full presence; a nonresolving recapitulation stages a disruption of it, open to a private reading or an Adornian social-critique reading of the form's insufficiency (pp. 143–44).
- Expositions that leave the generic course predict nonresolving recapitulations, though a corrective recapitulation can rehabilitate them (p. 149).

**the terms (pp. 128–29)**

- The rhetorical recapitulation is a matter of thematic and modular layout; tonal resolution is a separate matter (p. 128).
- A rotation is an ordered array of thematic modules recycled, usually altered, later in the work; developments may be fully or partly rotational (p. 128 n. 2).
- The EEC, the point of essential expositional closure, is the first satisfactory perfect authentic cadence in the subordinate key that proceeds onward to differing material. What "satisfactory" means is deferred to *Elements* (p. 128).
- The ESC, the point of essential structural closure, is the corresponding PAC in the tonic in the recapitulation. It marks a resolving recapitulation (p. 128). It can coincide with the completion of the Urlinie's 3̂–2̂–1̂ (p. 140).
- P, TR, S, C are primary theme, transition, secondary theme, closing theme. The MC, the medial caesura, is the mid-expositional cadential break of a two-part exposition (p. 128 and n. 3).
- Fig. 1a–b (p. 129), from *Elements*, lays out the exposition and the whole structure, with the "essential sonata trajectory" to the ESC.
- A sonata deformation is a work that contravenes an essential generic marker yet asks to be heard through sonata norms (p. 136).
- First- and second-level defaults are the most standard choice and the next most standard when the first is overridden. In minor-mode expositions, III is first-level and minor v second-level (p. 144 n. 20).

**egmont (pp. 128–35)**

- The exposition is regular: F minor to A-flat (III), EEC at m. 104. In the recapitulation the drive to the MC is derailed onto D-flat (VI) at m. 207, the dominant of D-flat is prolonged mm. 217–24, MC at m. 224. S follows, transposed, in D-flat; the ESC-substitute PAC at m. 247; the twelve-measure closing zone, transposed, ends at m. 259. Every closure is in D-flat (p. 130).
- Against the fifth-relation defense. The cited Beethoven precedents (Op. 31 no. 1, Op. 53) are major-mode pieces, where a mediant S is unusual, and their recapitulatory submediant is ephemeral and self-corrects to the tonic, by backing up to restart S (Op. 31 no. 1) or by correction within S (Op. 53) (p. 130).
- Fifth-transposition also governs brief interior escapes: in Op. 18 no. 3, C major at mm. 68–71 becomes F major at mm. 199–202; the movement is a second-type continuous exposition (p. 130 n. 4).
- Rosen's claim that Beethoven "invariably" balances an expositional mediant with a recapitulatory submediant isn't true. Counterexamples: *Leonore* 3, S in E (III) recapitulated in C, with no submediant balance in *Leonore* 2 either; Op. 29, first movement, exposition I to VI/vi (A major, A minor), recapitulation S–C in C major-minor-major; Op. 97, first movement, submediant S recapitulated in the tonic (pp. 131–32 and nn. 5–7).
- Rosen's Op. 127 "balance" sets a mediant and a submediant in different rotational positions, before and after the MC (n. 6).
- Minor-mode expositions moving to III recapitulate S and C in the tonic, a third down, not in VI. Fifth-transposition is normative for works that move to the dominant (p. 132).
- Egmont's A-flat and D-flat are related by fifth and are "in some respects rhetorically balanced," yet the recapitulation is nonresolving: Hepokoski rejects fifth-balance as a substitute for tonic resolution (pp. 132–33).
- On the neo-Riemannian alternative, F minor to D-flat is the L relation, hexatonically adjacent in Cohn's terms, and D-flat is prepared in mm. 15–17. Hepokoski calls the question relevant and keeps the period's diatonic tonic/nontonic binary as preferable (p. 133).
- On the program, the recapitulation's failure enacts Egmont's failure to liberate the Netherlands; his execution (m. 278, after Calhoun) is the precondition for the coda's resolution. The link mm. 259–86 shows D-flat as an upper neighbor to V of F (fermata, mm. 285–86); the *Siegessymphonie*, mm. 287ff., resolves and overturns F minor (pp. 133–34).
- A coda is a parageneric, optional zone. Hepokoski asks whether a closure-providing coda resolves the deferred argument or reflects on its absence, and whether an apotheosis-coda collapses the sonata into a "disposable delivery system" (p. 134).
- The easy answer assumes two disputable postulates: the mid-twentieth-century harmonic definition of sonata form (Ratner) and the sonata principle (Cone). Neither distinguishes closure inside sonata space from closure outside it (pp. 134–35).

**beethoven, piano trio in g, op. 1 no. 2, largo con espressione (pp. 136–43)**

- Composed 1793–94, published 1795. E major (VI of the trio's G). P is a nine-measure sentence (Caplin's example of expanded cadential function); TR of dissolving-restatement type from m. 9 toward V, but in a premonitory B minor; dominant lock at m. 18; MC at m. 23 on a half cadence in the dominant minor, caesura-fill mm. 24–25 (p. 136).
- S is over a pulsing F♯. The antecedent, mm. 26–31, prolongs V of B. The consequent at m. 32 begins toward B major, and at m. 33 B major decays to B minor. At m. 34, with F♯ and D as common tones, a fortissimo push into G major (VI of B minor); piano at m. 35. The rhetorical EEC-substitute closes in G at m. 40, the "wrong key" (pp. 137–40).
- Hepokoski gives two readings of mm. 40–47 before the recapitulation at m. 47: a retransition after an exposition ending at m. 40, or a closing zone that dissolves at once. The ambiguity may be the point (p. 140).
- In the recapitulation S enters at m. 67 in the tonic over a pulsing dominant; the consequent at m. 73; E minor at m. 74; a push onto C major, mm. 75–76, which would be the fifth-transposed balance of the exposition's G. Instead C slumps to A minor, mm. 78–79, and S closes at m. 82 in A minor, minor iv of E (p. 140).
- Mm. 82–90 and 90–100 restore E major with a PAC. They're either a recapitulatory extension or a discursive coda that corrects what the sonata failed to do. Hepokoski rejects a sonata-rondo reading (n. 18) and holds the strain of the decision as the expressive point (pp. 140–43).
- Related movements: the E-major Adagio of Op. 2 no. 3 and the E-major Largo of the Third Concerto (p. 136 n. 15); Op. 10 no. 2's A-flat Adagio as a Type 1 sonata with discursive coda (p. 143 n. 18).

**mozart, string quartet in d minor, k.173, first movement (pp. 144–49)**

- Vienna, September 1773; Artaria printed the first two movements with an earlier finale in 1792, three years before the Beethoven trio (p. 144).
- Of seventeen minor-mode sonata movements by Mozart from before or around 1773 that Hepokoski consulted, fourteen move to III and three to minor v, all normatively; the three minor-v cases are slow movements (K.41, K.168, K.171). Four London Sketchbook movements are set aside (p. 144 n. 20).
- K.173's exposition splits its cadences among three nontonic minor keys: A minor (v) at mm. 18, 22, 24; E minor (ii) at m. 33; G minor (iv) at m. 42. That makes four tonal zones, two of them counter-generic (p. 147).
- The exposition is in dialogue with the second type of continuous exposition, which lacks a proper MC and S: an early PAC (m. 18) driven "into a cadential ditch," then repeated backing up to defer closure. K.458 and Haydn 88 are normative examples of the type (pp. 147–48 and n. 23). Haydn's Op. 20 no. 3 may have given K.173 "a general suggestion," not a tonal model (p. 147).
- The recapitulation begins at m. 65; the crux is at m. 80, after which mm. 80–112 transpose mm. 10–42 a fifth down. The D-minor PAC at m. 88 threatens a premature ESC; the phrase from m. 95 moves to A minor (m. 103) and closes on C minor (vii) at m. 112. Retransition from m. 113; repeat of development and recapitulation; the tonic PAC only in the coda at m. 132, pianissimo, with two forte unison reiterations (pp. 148–49).
- The interpretive range runs to melancholy tipping into madness, after Sisman's paper on the eighteenth-century labyrinth of melancholy, with caution about grand claims for a seventeen-year-old's piece (pp. 147, 149).

**mozart, piano concerto in e-flat, k.449, andantino (p. 149)**

- The rehabilitation case: S begins in F (V) at m. 41 and closes in A-flat (♭VII). The recapitulation (from m. 80) interpolates a circle-of-fifths "magic passage," mm. 103–6, that subverts the transposition and produces the ESC in B-flat at m. 116. Generic containment wins.

**three categories (pp. 151–53)**

- Category One covers minor-mode sonatas whose major-mode S and C return in minor. They're tonally closed, resolving, and not deformational: "sonata failure" in expression, not nonresolution. This is Mozart's virtually invariable practice: K.183 and K.550 outer movements; first movements of K.421, K.310, K.478, K.516. Beethoven is more variable: Op. 2 no. 1, Op. 10 no. 1, the Ninth's first movement; sometimes he begins the recapitulatory S in major and extinguishes it into minor. For expositions that move to minor v, the major-mode possibility is "more remote" (pp. 151–52).
- Category Two covers S space kept from a PAC in both exposition and recapitulation, though in the proper keys: Haydn's Op. 20 no. 3, first movement (n. 31); Beethoven's Fifth, finale (S from m. 45 in G); the finales of the Second and Eighth Symphonies and Op. 59 no. 3; Bruckner. Closure is deferred to the coda (pp. 152–53).
- Category Three covers recapitulations ending in a nontonic key: Egmont, Op. 1 no. 2 (one reading), K.173. Later examples: Brahms 3, first movement (F to A major then A minor; recapitulatory S in D major then D minor), read as an "uncorrected" Op. 53 pattern (n. 32); Mahler 6, first movement; Glinka, *Ruslan and Ludmila* overture; Tchaikovsky, *Romeo and Juliet*; Saint-Saëns 3; Rachmaninov 2, finale; Mahler 5, second movement (p. 153).

**what unit 3 takes from it**

- The Sonata Theory vocabulary the Paper 3 prompt uses: MC, EEC, ESC, rotation, default levels, deformation. Fig. 1 is the one-page reference.
- Week 13's three Beethoven movements appear in Hepokoski's nn. 5–7 as tests of the fifth-balance claim. Op. 53 corrects its submediant; Op. 29 recapitulates its submediant S up a third in C; Op. 97 recapitulates its submediant S in the tonic.
- For Paper 4, the default levels and Category One name parts of what the Kreutzer's keys do; which part of the exposition each term describes is for the score.
- The decay of a major S into minor (Op. 1 no. 2, m. 33) as a structural event, the model for reading mode switches as the Paper 4 prompt asks.

## hepokoski, "beyond the sonata principle" (pp. 91–154) · 1772 to 1810; the term from 1952

*Journal of the American Musicological Society* 55, no. 1. This essay is the longer of the pair; its works cited are on pp. 151–54.

**what the essay argues**

- The "sonata principle" is a mid-twentieth-century English-language construct, rarely examined, and it's a background truism (pp. 92–93).
- Its early formulations, above all Cone's, overreach. Applied to unusual pieces, it supplies a catchphrase in place of analysis (p. 96).
- It isn't rejected wholesale: some version of it is relevant to "the vast majority" of sonatas, and it "holds in most cases" (pp. 96, 137).
- Properly restricted, it applies only to post-MC expositional material, S and C, and its expected tonic return in the recapitulation (pp. 112, 118).
- Where a piece departs from even that restricted norm, the departure is the analytical point. Hepokoski proposes Sonata Theory's alternatives: rotation, the trimodular block, the synecdochic and substitution strategies, sonata types (pp. 129–30, 147).

**concept history (pp. 91–102)**

- Four late-century orthodoxies: Rosen's overviews, the motivic quest for unity (Schoenberg, Réti, Keller), Ratner's historical-rhetorical approach, Schenkerian analysis (p. 91); also LaRue, Newman, Tovey, and Caplin (n. 1).
- Haimo's formulation (1995) and Webster's in the second *New Grove* are both hedged (pp. 92–93). Counterexamples come at once: K.338's first closing theme, mm. 64–74, never recurs; the B-minor theme at m. 23 of Op. 10 no. 3 never sounds in the tonic (it recurs at m. 204 on E minor); Egmont's S and C in D-flat (pp. 94–95).
- Tovey's resistance to labels (1927, 1929, 1934) and Schenker's (1935) (pp. 97–99). Ratner (1949) moves the definition from themes to key relations, which lets thematic anomalies be dismissed as secondary (pp. 99–100).
- The term "appears to have been coined" by Barford in 1952, with nothing tonal in it: a dialectic of rules and imagination culminating in Beethoven. Mellers (1957) holds that there are sonatas but no sonata form (pp. 100–101).
- Cone (Oberlin lectures, January 1967; published 1968): "important statements made in a key other than the tonic must either be re-stated in the tonic, or brought into a closer relation with the tonic, before the movement ends." Illustrations: the Eroica coda, the K.482 Andante, the *Pathétique* finale (pp. 101–2).

**cone tested (pp. 102–18)**

- Developmental episodes don't require resolution. Counterexamples: Op. 2 no. 1, finale, a fifty-one-measure A-flat episode (mm. 59–109) never heard again; K.478, first movement, mm. 104–33; K.338; the D-major episode in Haydn 45's first movement, against Webster's claim that the finale "implicitly recapitulates" it; K.156, K.285b, K.458 (pp. 104–8).
- When a new developmental idea does return in a coda (K.330, mm. 59–64 and 145–50), rotation explains it: codas often recycle the opening of the development (pp. 108–10). Coda-rhetoric interpolation (CRI), codalike material placed before the last recapitulatory modules, covers K.448 and K.550 (n. 35). The Eroica coda retraces the development and recapitulation in order; its E-flat minor statement of the "new" theme (mm. 589–93) reanimates the tonic-minor sounding in the development (pp. 110–11).
- The principle applies only to nontonic post-MC zones of expositions and recapitulations, not developments or codas (p. 112). TR material is excluded, however tonal its suggestions (n. 38). Thematized TR material before the real MC is exempt: Op. 10 no. 3's m. 23 as TM1 of a trimodular block (n. 8); K.457's theme in III at m. 23, before the real MC at m. 34, which Rosen took as second group (n. 78).
- In rondos, nontonic central episodes usually don't return (the finales of Beethoven's first three piano concertos and the Violin Concerto; K.281; Op. 2 no. 3; K.361). When they do, codas are retrospective or summary, and they're in the tonic because codas ground the tonic (pp. 112–15).
- "Closer relation" is underdeveloped. How is Egmont's five-flat D-flat closer to F minor than the exposition's four-flat A-flat? The *Pathétique* coda's A-flat moment (mm. 203–6) belongs with Beethoven's wrong-key rondo-theme statements in Op. 2 no. 3 (after the fermata at m. 297, A major decaying to A minor) and Op. 7 (mm. 155–60, E major) (pp. 115–18).

**rosen and haydn's op. 64 no. 3 (pp. 118–30)**

- Rosen's version: tonic and dominant are polarized, and material in the dominant is a "dissonant section" requiring resolution. Hepokoski prefers the tonic moving outward to a projection of itself (p. 118).
- Rosen's exception that proves the rule: Op. 64 no. 3's first S theme doesn't return in the recapitulation but appears in the development on the tonic, in minor (pp. 119–20).
- Hepokoski reads the exposition with an MC at m. 32 (half cadence in F); S1 at m. 33, a dolce Ländler; its repetition decays to F minor at m. 37 ("lights out"); D-flat at m. 40, an augmented sixth at m. 41; a second MC at m. 42; caesura-fill mm. 42–47; a new S at m. 48, its continuation from m. 52; EEC at m. 65 (pp. 120–24).
- The apparent double medial caesura produces a trimodular block (TMB): TM1 after the first MC fails to reach a PAC; TM2 dissolves into transition; TM3, after the second MC, is the more successful S (pp. 123–24).
- The recapitulation suppresses the first MC, omits TM1, recomposes TR to the single MC at m. 152, and leaps at m. 153 to the transposition of m. 52. Nineteen measures of S are suppressed, fourteen kept (pp. 124–25).
- The development is rotational: P1 m. 70, P2 m. 78, S1 m. 87, P2 m. 97, TM3's opening m. 105, retransition m. 118. S1's B-flat minor is an apparent tonic within I–ii–V–i in A-flat (mm. 78, 87, 94, 97), weakly prepared and never secured by a cadence, and it ends on V of C minor (m. 104). The development replicates the TMB's shape: a weak caesura at mm. 86–87, a strong MC at m. 104, TM3 in C minor at m. 105 (p. 128 n. 56). S1 across the three rotations: major-minor, minor-minor, absent (pp. 125–28).
- One purpose of recapitulations is to restore order to problems set in the exposition; Haydn's recapitulations readjust wittily problematized expositions (p. 129).
- In the synecdochic strategy, part of S stands for its whole, normally the concluding modules that drive to the EEC. Incomplete recapitulatory S or C space is non-normative and responds to earlier events (p. 130).

**two axioms and the s zone (pp. 130–40)**

- Module labels: in S1.1, the integer counts the cadential span (S1 leads to the first PAC) and the decimal the module's position within it (n. 65).
- Axiom 1: a work won't state an S or C module in the tonic in the development and again in the recapitulation. Generally correct; exceptions are deformational (pp. 131–32).
- Axiom 2: a recapitulation won't omit S or C modules unless they've had a tonic statement elsewhere. Doubtful (p. 131).
- P1 in the tonic in the development is common (Koch's early tonic station; false recapitulations, disputed by Hoyt; Bonds's list includes Haydn 48 finale and 41, 55, 91 first movements; Sisman's "Jupiter" monograph, pp. 50–52). The second tonic P1 at the recapitulation is unaffected (pp. 132–33 and nn. 61–64).
- S modules are the exposition's most generically critical materials, since only S produces the EEC and, by promise, the ESC (p. 134). Hepokoski's hypothesis: a tonic S1.1 anywhere after the exposition triggers the drive to the ESC; if aborted, those modules are spent (p. 134). Schubert's D. 960 and the *Symphonie fantastique* (m. 200) are later exceptions (n. 67).
- Four further hypotheses: prematurely tonic S modules come from S's beginning, and later modules are reserved for the ESC; tonic S modules in a development show signs of provisionality (parallel minor, apparent tonic, weak MC); S modules are the most sensitive, which is why developments more often use P, TR, or C; C modules are postcadential, Koch's *Anhang* and Reicha's *idées accessoires*, and omitting one breaks a lower-order norm (pp. 135–37).
- Sonata types: Type 1 without development, Type 2 binary without full recapitulation, Type 3 textbook, Type 4 sonata-rondo, Type 5 concerto (p. 137 n. 69). Type 2 is more common in the Mannheim composers, J. C. Bach, and Mozart (K.16, K.22, K.306, K.311, K.525 finale, and others) than in Haydn; Haydn's 44 finale is one (pp. 137–38). The "reversed recapitulation" is a historically unjustified misreading of Type 2, applied for example to K.306 and K.311 (n. 71).
- A well-prepared tonic S1 midway through a rotational development proposes Type 2; its quick abandonment revokes the proposal, a "false tonal resolution," the Type 2 analogue of the false recapitulation. It spends those S modules for the recapitulation (p. 138). Weber's *Oberon* overture (n. 74).

**op. 20 no. 3 and op. 33 no. 3 (pp. 140–48)**

- In Op. 20 no. 3, first movement, an unusual MC (mm. 24–26) leads to a B-flat S zone of eight modules (mm. 27–ca. 94) with no EEC and no closing theme (n. 76). The recapitulation omits S1.1–1.3; the development treats parts of S1.2 and S1.3 only in E-flat. Rosen's rule fails, and the "argument from fifth-transposition" can't save it (pp. 140–42). Hepokoski reads a capricious piece whose sonata trims back expositional overgrowth, with the melancholy link to Sisman again (pp. 142–43).
- In Op. 33 no. 3, first movement: MC at m. 26, S at m. 27 (S1.1 m. 28 in G; S1.2 m. 30 decays to G minor; S1.3 m. 32; S1.4 m. 36), EEC at m. 42, P-based C mm. 43–56. S1.1 and S1.4 are missing from the recapitulation, and neither has a tonic statement in the development. The recapitulation is continuous, without MC or S proper. The development's second rotation brings S1.4 to a strong PAC in E minor (m. 108), which liquidates it; in the recapitulation a "placidly banal" new idea substitutes and makes the ESC at m. 138 (pp. 143–48).
- In Haydn, two-part expositions can be recast as continuous recapitulations and the reverse. Symphony No. 6 has a two-part exposition and a continuous recapitulation. Symphony No. 75 has a continuous exposition (blocked caesura at m. 49, EEC at m. 56) whose recapitulation supplies an MC at m. 134 and the ESC at m. 159 (p. 146 and n. 82).
- In the substitution strategy, an S module is replaced in the recapitulation by a functional counterpart (p. 147). Beethoven's Fourth Concerto, first movement, replaces its EEC module (p. 148 n. 85). The *Così fan tutte* overture omits S from its recapitulation entirely (n. 81).

**envoi (pp. 149–51)**

- Nonresolving recapitulations (K.173, Op. 1 no. 2, Egmont) and S-less recapitulations (the *Idomeneo* overture; slow-movement recapitulations of P and TR only in Haydn's Op. 33 nos. 5 and 6 and Mozart's K.575, Caplin's "truncated recapitulation," n. 88) break the principle outright and are set aside (pp. 149–50).
- The principle served an analytical climate that downplayed themes in favor of keys; it combined quick answers with self-confirmation. In the most interesting cases it's inadequate (pp. 150–51).

**what unit 3 takes from it**

- The S zone as the sensitive part of the form: where the second key is secured, decayed, or replaced. Paper 3's "what does each composer do to the arrival of the second key" is this question.
- Haydn's recapitulations as readjustments of problematized expositions, and his recasting of two-part and continuous layouts (Symphonies 6 and 75), as hypotheses to test on Haydn 90.
- A tonal resolution beginning at or around S is a Type 2 layout, not a reversed recapitulation. Whether any Week 10–11 symphony has that layout is for the scores.
- The false recapitulation as a disputed category, with Sisman's discussion of the "Jupiter" in n. 64.

## burnett and nitzberg on the unit 3 works (not assigned) · 1720s to 1811

These notes are for instructor use. They cover chapter 5, "Tonality and Systems in the Mid- to Late-Eighteenth Century: The Classical Ideal" (pp. 155–232), and chapter 6, "Nineteenth-Century Approaches to Eleven-Pitch-Class Systems Derived from the Viennese Classical Tradition" (from p. 233), with passages from chapters 1, 2, and 8. Per the preface, Burnett wrote chapters 3–7 with Nitzberg's emendations, and Nitzberg wrote chapters 1, 2, and 8. The Waldstein passages are Nitzberg's. The sonata-form definitions of section III of chapter 5 (pp. 180–92) are adapted or quoted from Nitzberg's dissertation, ch. 3 (p. 180 n. 19), and the Haydn 84 exposition analysis appears in it (p. 207 n. 30). "Burnett" below stands for the book.

**the frame (pp. 155–216)**

- Burnett's approach to sonata form is harmonic: periods articulated by cadences, with thematic content that may or may not be structural (p. 181). Ratner and Hepokoski are acknowledged as related approaches (n. 20).
- Terms, from Diagram 5.3 (p. 183): first and second harmonic area, opening statement, counterstatement, bridge, closing period, codetta. A bridge connects the first harmonic area to the second; a transition links subdivisions within the second area (p. 156 n. 2).
- Opening statements are periodic, from the style galant and the dance, or segmented, from the ritornello's Vordersatz, Fortspinnung, Epilog, with a fanfare-like first phrase (pp. 185–87).
- Bridges either extend the tonic, ending on a back-relating dominant ("bifocal close," after Winter), or destabilize it and cadence on V or II♯ (p. 188).
- Mozart and Beethoven usually give the second area three periods (opening, closing, codetta), each closing with a full cadence; Haydn sometimes omits the closing period (pp. 190–91).
- Diagram 5.4 (p. 193) gives two monothematic types. Type A is a continuous bridge that doesn't cadence in the new key until the end; type B is a medial caesura with rest and the opening material transposed to the dominant (p. 192).
- The recapitulation's main purpose is to resolve "the main issues (often chromatic issues)" of the movement into tonic harmony, "the primary reason, we believe," why composers don't need to recapitulate everything (p. 191). Composers "seemed to have deliberately planned" movements around chromatic issues stated at the outset that resolve when transposed into the tonic (p. 216).
- Two outgrowths of the pianoidée before the dominant is reached: the minor dominant at the start of the second area (Monn, Scarlatti, early Haydn) and Wagenseil's single augmented-sixth sonority on the missing pitch, which Burnett calls "more practical." Behind both are Vivaldi's pianoidée in the parallel minor (RV 158) and Sammartini's parallel-minor bridges; ahead, Haydn's Paris-symphony augmented sixths (pp. 156–80, 207). However introduced, the missing pitch of the tonic system "is always a dramatic event" (p. 180).
- Burnett traces a development that climaxes on III or iii, redressed at the recapitulation, to the Baroque penultimate ritornello in iii: Wagenseil's WV 361 and several first movements of Haydn's late symphonies. N. 15 cites Willner for the Haydn cases and faults him for omitting the concerto link (pp. 175–76).

**vivaldi, rv 158, first movement (pp. 155–57; Ex. 5.1, mm. 1–10)**

- Vivaldi's ripieno concertos, often used as opera overtures, as an influence on the early symphony (pp. 155–56).
- The opening ritornello is multi-segmented. Its pianoidée in the parallel minor shifts the system to "0" and is placed directly before three modulatory Epilog segments, so it prepares a bridge.
- Epilog 1, mm. 13–14, is V–I in the tonic. In Epilog 2, mm. 15–16, the bass A moves to B, held as a pedal under D♯, which restores 3♯ and acts as V/V. Epilog 3, mm. 17–18, is the formal cadence in E. The second ritornello follows at once in V.
- The passage from pianoidée to second ritornello "foreshadows" the move to the dominant in later sonata forms (p. 156). Burnett infers that the major tonic switching to its parallel minor "must have been heard as" dissonant, Riepel's "schwarze Gredel" (pp. 156–57).
- Ex. 5.1 prints mm. 1–10 only; the Epilogs he describes aren't in the example.

**sammartini, symphony in c, first movement (pp. 158–63; Diagram 5.1, p. 161; Exx. 5.2a, 5.2b, pp. 159, 163)**

- Not on the syllabus. Burnett calls it Sammartini's "no. 1 in C (c. 1720s)," with Churgin's editions as sources (nn. 6–7), and gives no J-C number. Seminar identification: J-C 7.
- Context: Sammartini as maestro di cappella in Milan (Burney); Milan's orchestral culture before Mannheim, under Austrian rule of Lombardy, 1708–96 (p. 158).
- Diagram 5.1 calls the form a "Vivaldi-influenced, irregular ritornello form." It's a ripieno-concerto ritornello form, after Vivaldi or Albinoni; the "second harmonic area" is the ritornello transposed to V (p. 158).
- The opening ritornello cadences on the tonic at m. 16 and is extended four measures, alternating minor tonic and major dominant, the last three over a viola dominant pedal (Ex. 5.2a, mm. 1–20). It leads straight into the second ritornello in V; the melodic F♯ is the leading tone without V/V harmony (p. 158).
- E♭, which would shift the system to 3♭, is corrected each time by F♯; the alternation ends at m. 19 on an uncontested F♯, so "0" holds (p. 160).
- Most composers spell the first move away from the tonic flat; D♯ would imply iii and undercut the later move to iii (p. 160).
- After the second ritornello in V, now with an Epilog, the music swings to E minor with a lyrical idea in four-measure units. The phrasing is galant 2 + 2 throughout, against Vivaldi's asymmetrical ritornellos (p. 160).
- In E minor the E♭s become D♯s and the system rises to 3♯. In mm. 49–79 D♯ conflicts with C♮, the systems moving back and forth "not unlike that of a development section." C♮ is uncontested from m. 79 (p. 163).
- The E-minor lyrical theme returns in the tonic minor at m. 98 (Ex. 5.2b, mm. 98–112): D♯s become E♭s, 3♭. E♭ resolves to D at the end of m. 101, returns, and resolves to E♮ in the next measure. F♯ on beat 2 of m. 103, in an Italian augmented sixth on A♭, restores "0." A four-measure ritornello in the tonic major ends the movement (p. 163).
- Every dissonant gesture (the tonic minor, E♭ and D♯, the lyrical theme in a remote area) is restated and resolved in the tonic; Burnett says Sammartini "anticipated Classical developmental techniques by four decades" (p. 163).

**wagenseil, sinfonia in e, wv 393 (1760), first movement (pp. 166–71; Ex. 5.4, complete exposition, pp. 168–69)**

- Context: Viennese court composer from 1739, pupil of Fux; conservative in his ties to binary dance form, galant in his key-centered chromatic voice leading; an influence on Haydn and the young Mozart, and on Beethoven through his pupil Schenk (pp. 166–67).
- In Wagenseil's general procedure the dominant is hinted at in the bridge, and the bridge cadence and the codetta are its only confirmations, with no extended dominant area. Burnett traces it to the binary dance's tonic prolonged to a half cadence at the double bar (p. 167).
- WV 393 has a 13-measure opening statement in three four-measure phrases and a 32-measure bridge from m. 13. The first half of the bridge introduces A♯; the second half reaches II♯ at m. 25. A B-major (V) chord comes at m. 31; in the next measure an augmented sixth on G♮, the missing pitch of the 4♯ system, resolves to II♯7 (V7/V) and V (pp. 167–70).
- M. 35 is the first decisive gesture toward the new area, still in the bridge and without a formal cadence: "what Hepokoski calls an 'unachieved medial caesura'" (n. 11). It starts an auxiliary cadence completed at m. 45, the start of a five-measure codetta and the first anchoring of the second area. There's no formal division before the codetta; m. 35 divides "trying to leave the tonic" from "trying to arrive at the dominant." The rhythm is "almost consistently governed by four-measure hypermeasures and associated extensions" (p. 170).
- The augmented sixth on ♭3 reduces a whole parallel-minor phrase to one sonority with a voice-leading function. Haydn's Paris symphonies use the same device (p. 170).
- Koch's single-period exposition, which cadences on V without rules for where the second area falls, is the theoretical parallel (pp. 170–71).
- Editions cited (p. 167 n. 9): Diletto musicale; DTÖ 31; Kucaba, *The Symphony 1720–1840*, ser. B, vol. 3. The seminar copy is Paumgartner's Diletto musicale 387. Whether that copy is WV 393 needs checking against it.

**haydn, symphony no. 2, and stamitz (pp. 176–80)**

- Haydn 2 appears in one sentence: the second harmonic area begins in the minor dominant in the first movements of Haydn's symphonies nos. 1, 2, and 4, a Viennese practice (Monn), with Beethoven's Op. 2 no. 3 as a later instance (p. 176).
- The minor dominant is the pianoidée moved onto the dominant. It invites a system shift down three signatures and readily introduces the tonic system's missing ♭3; its release into the major dominant restores the tonic system (p. 179).
- Stamitz appears as Mannheim's leading figure, with Wagenseil and Monn as his Viennese counterparts (p. 176), and as sharing Monn's bridge cadence and rest before the harmonic change, with J. C. Bach and Leopold and Wolfgang Mozart (p. 180).

**j. c. bach (pp. 181, 188, 211–16)**

- J. C. Bach's expositions are quadripartite: a tonic period, a bridge, and a second area (V in major, the relative major in minor) in three periods, each set off by a downbeat cadence with rests and each in a contrasting topic. Bach prefers expositions that aren't repeated, as in overtures. Tonal motion centers on the cadence (pp. 211–12).
- His bridges are non-modulatory and end on a back-relating dominant (pp. 188, 212). Reverse recapitulations are common in his music, after the Mannheim composers (p. 181 n. 21).
- In Op. 9 no. 2 (1773), first movement, Diagram 5.6 (p. 213), A♮/A♭ is the main dyad conflict. F♯, the missing pitch of the 3♭ system, is first heard at m. 70 in the closing period, cancelled by E♭ on the next downbeat; at the development's climax (C minor, then A♭ swinging to G minor, m. 106) it shifts the system to "0" for two measures, until E♭ at m. 110. The recapitulation omits the "singing allegro" topic. The exposition's E♭–E♮–F (m. 62, mm. 67–68) returns as A♭–A♮–B♭ in the tonic (mm. 168–69) and resolves the conflict (pp. 212–16).
- Op. 6 no. 6 isn't discussed. Op. 9 no. 2 is the J. C. Bach movement Burnett analyzes.

**haydn, symphony no. 84 in e-flat, first movement (pp. 204–11; Fig. 5.1, the 3♭ matrix, p. 206)**

- The Paris symphonies were commissioned by the Comte d'Ogny for the Concert de la Loge Olympique and the Concert Spirituel (p. 204).
- The introduction presents the movement's dyad conflicts in order: D♭/D♮ (mm. 3–4), B♭/B♮ (m. 8), A♭/A♮ (m. 9), G♭/G♮ (mm. 16–17). The order runs backward along the chromatic octave from pcs 10–11. The A♭/A♮ dyad expands to the trichord A♭–A♮–B♭ at m. 13 and the tetrachord G–A♭–A♮–B♭, an octave lower in the second violin, at m. 14. E♮ is absent from the introduction, saved for the Allegro. G♭, the missing pitch of the 3♭ system, is saved for last (pp. 204–5).
- In mm. 16–20, over a dominant pedal, a diminished harmony on A♮ sets G♭ against A♮, the missing pitch of the 6♭ system, so G♭ can't shift 3♭ to 6♭. Burnett calls this a "system dyad conflict," deeper than a neighbor-note dyad conflict, and it governs the second half of the exposition (p. 206).
- The Allegro, cut time, opens with a piano eight-measure statement; the PCA begins with pcs 0–2 in the viola at m. 25. A five-measure tutti and an almost autonomous counterstatement at m. 33 follow; the tutti becomes the bridge; a V/V pedal, mm. 53–57. At mm. 62ff., E♭/E♮, A♭/A♮, and D♭/D♮ return over diminished harmonies, and pc 3 is spelled F♯, which with E♭ forms the system dyad conflict complementary to G♭/A♮, to avoid a shift. An authentic cadence on B♭ comes at m. 73 with three beats of rest (pp. 206–7).
- The second area seems to begin at m. 74, but the voice exchange that normally opens it comes only at m. 88, with a G♭ augmented sixth spelled with F♭. The medial caesura attempted at m. 73 isn't achieved, since no complete progression in the dominant comes before the authentic cadence at m. 104. Mm. 74ff. are a "false second harmonic area," part of an extended bridge subsumed under the tonic (pp. 207–8).
- Within that bridge: pcs 0–2 again, mm. 74–80; G♭ enters at m. 81 in the minor dominant and "wins out" at m. 85, shifting 3♭ to 6♭; C♭/C♮, mm. 86–87; F♭/F♮, mm. 88–95. The G♭7 spelling implies C♭, a feint Burnett hears copied at the development climax of Beethoven's Op. 1 no. 3. G♭ becomes G♮ at m. 93 and resolves to F at m. 94; an F pedal, mm. 94–100; F♯ at m. 101; the authentic cadence at m. 104 (pp. 208–9).
- The development follows the reordered hexachord (A♭ E♭ B♭ f/F c g): B♭ – c – [A♭ – D♭] – C – F – [c – D♭] – B♭ – E♭. The false recapitulation is on F major (II♮) at m. 148; only F major's A♮ can cancel the 6♭ system set off by the D♭ and G♭ from m. 128. F♯ at m. 152 shifts up to "0"; E♭ at m. 161 restores 3♭; D♭ as ♭VII at m. 181 is the climax and brings G♭ and 6♭ again; rapid shifts, mm. 182–92; A♮ wins at mm. 191–92; the retransition from m. 192; F♯ at m. 201, cancelled by the tonic recapitulation at m. 202 (pp. 209–10).
- In the recapitulation D♭ is the seventh of E♭7 at m. 214; A♭/A♮ at m. 216; pcs 3–7, mm. 225–31; second-area material in the tonic at m. 237; the tonic minor at m. 244, bringing G♭ and 6♭, with A♮ blocking the return; the codetta at m. 260 resolves D♭ to D♮. The movement ends in the uncorrected 6♭ system (pp. 210–11).
- The finale resolves both: D♭ in a false recapitulation in A♭, resolved at the retransition, m. 189; G♭ to G♮ at m. 269, before the coda at m. 271; at the end, A♮ against G♭ keeps 3♭ (p. 211).

**mozart, "jupiter," first movement (p. 187)**

- One sentence: its opening statement is of the segmented, ritornello-derived type, with Mozart 39, Beethoven 5, and Haydn 87.

**beethoven, string quintet in c, op. 29, first movement (pp. 251–57; Diagram 6.2, p. 252; Ex. 6.4, mm. 1–41, pp. 253–54; Ex. 6.5, mm. 264–80, p. 256)**

- "A work not generally discussed in the literature," and a "systems piece" (p. 251).
- The exposition's harmonic areas stay within the C matrix, between the "0" and 3♯ systems, and turn on the major and minor of the submediant and on C♯/C♮ (p. 252).
- The missing pitch of "0" first appears as E♭, a chord tone of F♯°7 in the opening tonic period (mm. 7, 15), as in the Waldstein. In the bridge it's spelled D♯ throughout. The A-minor passage in the bridge sets C♮ against D♯ until D♯ wins on the last eighth of m. 39, for the second area in A major. The rest of the exposition alternates A major and A minor until C♮ wins in A minor, capped by the codetta (p. 252).
- The development begins in C major, V/IV by m. 103; F minor at m. 107 brings E♭ and 3♭ and becomes the "development key"; D♭ at m. 111 brings 6♭; D♭ supports the trichord C–C♯–D and becomes C♯ at m. 117, leading to a G6/4 with D in the bass at m. 119; A♮ shifts to 3♭ and F♯ at m. 120 to "0"; E♭ and F♯ conflict from m. 123, with the minor subdominant again at mm. 151ff. E♭ in the development balances D♯ in the exposition (p. 255).
- In the recapitulation what was in the minor submediant returns in the tonic minor, raising E♭; the A-major sections return in C major with F♯ cancelling 3♭. The 3♭/"0" conflict balances the exposition's 3♯/"0" (p. 255).
- The C♮/C♯ trichord resolves in the closing period's final PCA ascent, mm. 264ff., all twelve pitch classes in 17 measures. D♯ displaces E♭; the first violin's D♯ resolves to E at m. 276; pcs 5–9 at mm. 277–78; A♯–B–C at the start of the coda, m. 280; diatonic to the end (pp. 255–56).
- Cherubini's *Lodoïska* overture (1791) has the same C♮–C♯–D trichord and A minor/A major juxtaposition (p. 257).
- When the second area is on the sharp side, the missing pitch is usually spelled as an augmented second; Burnett cites Op. 29 and the Waldstein (p. 273). The first movements of Op. 29, the Archduke, and the Ninth have second areas that don't arpeggiate the tonic triad (p. 328).

**beethoven, waldstein, first movement (pp. 5–7, 31–32, 354; Nitzberg)**

- Chapter 1 (pp. 5–6) treats the Waldstein in the same discussion as Haydn's Op. 64 no. 3. The opening statement holds the E♮/E♭ dyad conflict and ends on a cadential 6/4 with E♭. F♯ and B♭ grow from the diatonic F–B tritone; F♯ yields a C♯ appoggiatura; the bass descends C–B♭–A♭ to a mixed IV6; the half cadence is decorated ♭6/4–5/3 with E♭. The opening statement holds all twelve pitch classes and five chromatic pairs.
- The counterstatement begins at m. 14; at m. 18, G♯ in a ♯4/2 begins sharpward respellings, G♯–D♯–A♯, the A♯ in an augmented sixth that tonicizes III as the second area. Across the sonata there are ten enharmonic pairs and 22 spellings, each traced back to the opening E♭ (p. 6).
- Nitzberg contrasts the reading with Piston-style chord labeling, Riemannian mode mixture, motivic analysis, and a Schenkerian E dividing C–G (pp. 6–7).
- In chapter 2 (pp. 31–32) the exposition is subdivided by major thirds. E♭ sends the system to 3♭; F♯ in the counterstatement restores "0"; D♯ brings E major and the 3♯ system. The 3♯ system holds, uncontested, over most of the exposition without its missing pitch, C♮. E major is subsumed under the A–D♯ system. The recapitulation's transposition of the second area to VI (A major) within the tonic "0" system supports that reading. C♮ returns in the closing period at m. 70 (against D♯) and at m. 74 at the codetta; this restores "0" for the repeat.
- The C–F♯ tritone is system-consonant and the A–D♯ tritone system-dissonant (p. 32). In chapter 8, the C–A minor-third relation of system roots is the background of the foreground C–E major third (p. 354).

**beethoven, archduke trio, first movement (pp. 251, 328)**

- The first area is in B♭ (2♭ system), the second in G major (1♯). The top line of the piano falls B♭ to G at mm. 4–5.
- The opening statement, mm. 1–33, has exactly eleven pitch classes, without C♯/D♭. In the bridge, mm. 33–51, C♯ is first a neighbor to D against B♭ harmony, with no shift; at m. 35 a chromatic 5–6 swings B♭ to D major, and C♯ shifts the system to 1♯, uncontested until m. 81 in the closing area. Beethoven changes the signature to one sharp eight measures before the second area and back to two flats when B♭ returns at the end of the exposition.
- In the recapitulation the second area is in B♭; the closing area turns to the tonic minor, C♯ becomes D♭, and 2♭ falls to 5♭, held through codetta and coda. Burnett reads the time in 5♭ as balancing the exposition's time in 1♯. E♮ at m. 281, seven measures from the end, restores 2♭; C♯ returns five measures from the end against B♭ harmony; the close is a "systems summary" (p. 251).

## where the readings collide

**four kinds of explanation**

- Each reading explains a compositional choice differently. Schmid explains by temperament, background, and personal contact; Brown by inherited tradition, occasion, and eighteenth-century *Charakter*; Hepokoski by generic norms and their deformation, read hermeneutically; Lester by the theory available to composers and how it was taught.
- For any one moment in the "Jupiter" (the C-minor episode, the K quotation, the finale theme), students can state what each kind of explanation would claim and what evidence it would need.

**haydn 13 and the "jupiter" theme: schmid against brown**

- Schmid (pp. 150–51) says Haydn's no. 13 finale (symphony dated 1763) sets the motive against "a number of different counterpoints" with a four-part stretto like Mozart's 1774 Credo, and that Mozart may have seen it in Paris in 1764 or through Michael Haydn.
- Brown (p. 180) says the no. 13 finale hints at Fux's third species at the outset and otherwise largely avoids counterpoint; the pitch sequence is shared, but source distribution and treatment make it an unlikely model. He looks to Michael Haydn's own works (Mozart's copy of P.43; the 1788 letter) and to chant (pp. 180–87).
- Schmid's F-major Mass Credo is K.192/186f, whose association with the theme Brown rejects (p. 186).
- Schmid names Michael Haydn as a conduit for Joseph's symphony; Brown makes Michael Haydn's own works the source. Brown doesn't cite Schmid. The comparison isolates the evidence for each claim: proximity in Schmid; autograph copies, publication records, and a dated letter in Brown.

**mozart's teaching: lester against brown**

- Lester (p. 773) says Mozart taught harmony and composition with the fundamental bass to two pupils in the mid-1780s. There's no note; the chapter bibliography lists the Ployer and Attwood study editions (p. 776).
- Brown (p. 161) lists Attwood, Ployer, and Freystädtler among Mozart's composition students, 1785–87, with Mann tying the teaching to Fux and Haydn's "Elementarbuch," "Fuxian strict as well as free composition"; the studies are edited in NMA X/30 (n. 18).
- Lester's two pupils and two of Brown's students are the same, in the same years: the teaching is described as Rameauian harmony in one account and Fuxian composition in the other. Albrechtsberger's 1790 recasting of Fux, which begins from the harmonies under the cantus (Lester, pp. 773–74), joins the two.

**the "jupiter" c-minor episode: brown's parenthesis against hepokoski's decay**

- Brown (p. 166) calls the Sturm und Drang switch at m. 81 parenthetical, removable without structural loss. His Figure 1 labels it "Pb, ext.," iv/V, after a GP.
- Hepokoski ("Beyond," p. 123) treats a secondary theme that destabilizes into minor as a common extension strategy with local implications, not an expressively neutral option. The apparent double MC and TMB (pp. 123–24) and the modal decay of Op. 1 no. 2 ("Egmont," p. 137) are models for testing whether mm. 56–100 contain one MC or two and where the EEC falls.

**the "jupiter" false recapitulation**

- Brown's Figure 1 marks a false retransition at m. 157 and a false recapitulation in IV at m. 161, with Pᵏ over the descending bass to follow.
- Hepokoski ("Beyond," pp. 132–33 and nn. 63–64) treats false recapitulation as a contested category (Bonds; Hoyt disputes it) and cites Sisman's "Jupiter" monograph (pp. 50–52) for the issue. A tonic P1 in a development is the usual case; Mozart's is in IV.

**refrain, ritornello, rotation**

- Brown hears the fanfare refrains as descendants of trumpet-sinfonia ritornellos (p. 163). Unit 2's ritornello (Dreyfus, Burnett) is the near background.
- Hepokoski hears recurrence as rotation: ordered recycling of modules, with closing zones and codas that turn back to P ("Beyond," pp. 109–10; "Egmont," p. 128 n. 2).
- The same phenomenon has two historical lineages: a refrain inherited from the Baroque ritornello against a norm of the sonata as genre.

**op. 20: learned style against caprice**

- Schmid (pp. 152–53) treats Op. 20 as Haydn's turn to Baroque counterpoint, "probably" under Werner's influence, serious in form and content, the model for Mozart's 1773 quartets.
- Hepokoski ("Beyond," pp. 140–43; "Egmont," pp. 147, 152) calls Op. 20 no. 3's first movement "extravagantly capricious," a staged disorder, a possible "general suggestion" for K.173, and a Category Two case.
- The two readings address different movements and different questions; set side by side, they show that "influence" names a different thing in each.

**harmony and form**

- Lester (p. 774) says eighteenth-century theory left no framework for relating harmony to phrase and form.
- Ratner (1949), citing eighteenth-century theorists, defined Classical form by key relations ("Beyond," pp. 99–100). Hepokoski reintroduces thematic layout and cadence as generic markers.
- Rameau's key emerges from cadential progression (Lester, p. 762); Rosen's keys are polarized; Hepokoski's tonic projects outward ("Beyond," p. 118). These are three models of what it means to reach a second key.

**fifths, signatures, and the system method**

- Hepokoski rejects fifth-relation as a substitute for tonic resolution and rejects Rosen's "invariably" ("Egmont," pp. 130–33). He asks how D-flat (five flats) is closer to F minor than A-flat (four flats) ("Beyond," p. 115).
- Burnett's systems are counted in signatures and laid out in fifths. In system terms, Egmont's recapitulation is one signature flatward of its exposition.
- A system change and a formal event are separate observations, and the Paper 3 and 4 prompts ask where they coincide. Hepokoski doesn't discuss systems; by his Egmont argument, a system close to the tonic wouldn't count as tonal resolution (the notes' inference).

**neo-riemannian proxies**

- Hepokoski raises hexatonic substitution four times (F minor and D-flat; B major and G; E minor and C major; G minor and E-flat) and subordinates it to the period's diatonic sonata norms without dismissing it ("Egmont," pp. 133, 137–38 n. 17; "Beyond," p. 147 n. 83).

**burnett against the assigned readings (instructor only)**

- On Op. 29, Hepokoski ("Egmont," p. 132 n. 7) and Burnett (pp. 251–55) agree on the keys: exposition to A major and A minor (VI, vi), recapitulation in C major and minor. Hepokoski finds no fifth-based balance in the recapitulation. Burnett finds a system balance: 3♭ against "0" in the recapitulation answers 3♯ against "0" in the exposition. Hepokoski's objection to Rosen's Op. 127 balance, that it compares different rotational positions (n. 6), applies to this kind of claim.
- On the Waldstein, Hepokoski says the recapitulatory submediant is ephemeral and self-corrects to the tonic ("Egmont," p. 130). Nitzberg says the recapitulation's A major within the tonic "0" system supports hearing the exposition's E major under the 3♯ system (pp. 31–32).
- On the Archduke, both have the G-major second area returning in the tonic. Burnett adds the closing area's turn to the tonic minor and the 5♭ system.
- On Haydn's Op. 64 no. 3, Nitzberg (chapter 1, p. 5 and n. 11) takes Hepokoski's terminology but places the "apparent" medial caesura at m. 33, against Hepokoski's MC at m. 32 and S1 at m. 33. He makes D♭ (m. 40) the controlling pitch through the development, from B♭ minor at m. 87 to C♯ at m. 102. Hepokoski ("Beyond," pp. 120–28) reads the same measures as the decay and liquidation of S1.
- On the medial caesura, Burnett gives the complete-progression test as Hepokoski's definition of an unachieved MC (p. 170 n. 11) and applies it to WV 393's m. 35 (p. 170) and Haydn 84's m. 73 (p. 207); WV 361's caesura is achieved by an authentic cadence at m. 26 without a rest (p. 172). Hepokoski defines the MC by the caesura and the S that follows ("Beyond," p. 123), and the EEC by the first satisfactory PAC ("Egmont," p. 128).
- On reverse recapitulation, Burnett uses the term for the Mannheim composers and J. C. Bach and says K.311's recapitulation goes beyond it (p. 181 and n. 21). Hepokoski calls it a historically unjustified misreading of the Type 2 sonata and names K.311 ("Beyond," p. 138 n. 71).
- On the "Jupiter" opening, Burnett has a segmented opening statement derived from the ritornello (p. 187); Brown has fanfare refrains descended from trumpet-sinfonia ritornellos (p. 163). Both reach the Baroque ritornello, one by phrase segmentation and one by refrain.
- On form defined by harmony, Burnett's approach is "basically harmonic," and Classical composers "did not follow any rule books" (pp. 181, 192). Hepokoski calls the harmonic definition one of two disputable postulates ("Egmont," p. 134) and analyzes by generic norms.
- On what a recapitulation is for, Burnett says it resolves the main, often chromatic, issues into the tonic, which is why material can be omitted (p. 191). Hepokoski says it restores order to problems set in the exposition, with omissions as synecdoche or substitution ("Beyond," pp. 129–30, 147).

**what the reading doesn't cover**

- Haydn 2, 84, and 90; Stamitz; J. C. Bach's Op. 6 no. 6; RV 158; Wagenseil's Sinfonia in E; the Kreutzer.
- No assigned reading applies the hexachord systems to a Classical work. Burnett does, for instructor use (see his section above).

## the paper 4 directed questions

From `units/unit-3.html`, on the first movement of the Kreutzer.

- Take the major/minor mode switch as the organizing gesture. Which dyad conflicts realize it, and where is each first heard?
- Follow the system battle (3♯ against 0, and the flat side). Where do the dyad conflicts also act as system modulations?

Where the readings meet each question:
