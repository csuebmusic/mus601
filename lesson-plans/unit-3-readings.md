## lester, "rameau and eighteenth-century harmonic theory" (pp. 753–77) · 1558 to 1817

Chapter 24 of the Cambridge History, directly after Hyer's "Tonality" (unit 2). Sections: seventeenth-century antecedents; Rameau; Rameau's legacy.

**What the chapter claims**

- Each component of harmonic tonality arose before tonal syntax did, in a separate tradition with its own pedagogy: tertian harmony, voice leading, the bass as foundation, root generation, cadential direction, diatonic and chromatic interaction, harmony against meter (p. 753).
- Rameau was the first to recognize that these components interact to create tonality. Most of his theorizing, in treatises from 1722 to 1761, recombines long-standing concepts into one directional perspective (p. 753).
- Rameau was a major composer, and his theory answers to music as he understood it compositionally. When speculation and practice conflicted he invoked "license" (pp. 753, 772).
- Later eighteenth-century theory bequeathed a preoccupation with verticalities over linear factors and no framework for relating local harmonic structure to phrasing and form (p. 774).

**The antecedents (pp. 754–59)**

- Chordal theory. Zarlino (1558) defines a harmony's quality by the third above the bass, but as a rule of thumb for avoiding improper sonorities. For him C–E–A modifies C–E–G; it isn't an inversion of A–C–E. The tenor, not the bass, governs his structure (p. 754).
- Avianius (1581): single intervals are parts of chords. Burmeister (1599) and Magirus (1611): 5/3 and 6/3 are the only consonant harmonies. Iberian guitar *alfabeto* codifies the same chords (pp. 754–55).
- Harnisch (1608): 6/3 and 6/4 rearrange 5/3. Lippius (c. 1610) names all three *trias harmonica*, on the model of the Trinity. Fux (1725) explains the 5/3–6/3 inversion at the start of three-part species (p. 755).
- Thorough bass conceives chords from the bass, not a root, but keyboard practice made players aware that one right-hand chord fits several basses. Keller (1705) gives C–E–G over C, E6, G6/4, and also over A7, which isn't inversionally related (pp. 755–56).
- Normative bass harmonizations. Praetorius (1619): a 5/3 over a bass B contains a diminished fifth, so the figure over B is 6/3. Generalized: the figure over any bass solmized *mi* is 6/3, including the leading tone, the mediant of a major key, and secondary leading tones. Penna (1672) and Heinichen (1711, 1728) give rules for unfigured basses. Heinichen separates pattern rules from scale-step rules (p. 756).
- Campion's Rule of the Octave (1716): a normative harmonization of ascending and descending major and melodic-minor scales, Ex. 24.1 (pp. 756–57). The rules codify norms but aren't a theory of harmony (p. 757).
- Cadential direction. Vicentino (1555) and Zarlino (1558) recognize that some voice combinations create so strong an expectation of a cadential goal that its absence registers: "evading the cadence." In a 7–6 suspension before a cadential octave, the sixth drives toward the octave rather than resting as the resolution of the seventh (pp. 757–58).
- Major and minor keys. Keys come to be defined by the quality of the triad on the final, not only by final and octave species. Lippius first argues that the essential difference between modes is that triad. German traditions defend the modes well into the eighteenth century (p. 758).
- The generative fundamental. Descartes (c. 1618) derives consonances from a generating string: the fifth is direct, the fourth its "shadow." The same reasoning makes the major third primary and can't generate a minor third as the lowest interval of a triad, a problem never resolved. Mersenne on unity generating diversity; Sauveur's overtone series in the early eighteenth century (pp. 758–59).

**Rameau's system (pp. 759–63)**

- Four premises, from the *Traité de l'harmonie* (1722) and revised over forty years. The root-position triad and the seventh chord are the source of all harmonies. The root (*son fondamental*) generates them. Progression is a succession of roots, the *basse fondamentale*, with voice leading as the connection of chord tones. The fundamental bass is directed motion that yields key, because its motions match cadences (pp. 759–60).
- What the premises explain. The fifth in a 6/5 chord is dissonant as the seventh above the root; the "irregular fourth" of V4/3 that stays as a common tone is the dominant's fundamental (p. 760).
- Book I derives all chords from divisions of the monochord. It can't generate the minor third, justify octave identity, or derive the diminished seventh (pp. 760–61).
- Book II. The fundamental bass isn't meant to be played. Dissonance, above all the seventh, is the motor of progression, on an Enlightenment model of material causation. The first notated example in the *Traité* is the *cadence parfaite*, Plate 24.1, written on separate staves per voice (p. 761).
- The *dominante-tonique* carries two dissonances: the seventh ("minor dissonance," descends) and the leading tone ("major dissonance," ascends). The tonic has none. The dominant seventh defines the following chord as tonic, so a key is a focus that emerges from the progression, not a given pitch field (pp. 761–62).
- The *cadence irrégulière*: IV with added sixth to I, a rising fifth in the fundamental bass, Plate 24.2 (pp. 762–63).
- All music is a series of interconnected cadences, most evaded: one or both chords inverted, a seventh or added sixth on the arrival chord, or a minor third in the dominant seventh, which makes it a plain "dominant" rather than a *dominante-tonique* (p. 763).

**Supposition, irregular motion, double emploi (pp. 764–66)**

- Every dissonance should behave like a seventh. A ninth chord on G is a seventh chord on B with G "supposed" below. A 4–3 suspension A–D–E is a seventh chord on E with A supposed, so the resolving fourth is a concealed seventh resolving by falling fifth, Ex. 24.2. Supposition became a contested point in later controversies over the theory (pp. 764–65).
- Root motion by third yields successive consonant triads, each of which would then be a tonic. Only in later writings does Rameau separate a *ton régnant* from local tonicizations (p. 765).
- Root motion by step is explained by an interpolated fundamental bass: in Ex. 24.3 an A is inserted between C and D so that both moves are falling fifths. For IV to V, the subdominant's added sixth is reread as a supertonic seventh: *double emploi*, named in the *Nouveau système* (1726). It gives I–ii6/5–V7 with no stepwise root motion (pp. 765–66).

**Rameau's analyses (pp. 766–68)**

- His own vocal fugue (*Traité*, Book III, chapter 44); a monologue from Lully's *Armide*, twice, with different explanations (1726, 1754) (p. 766).
- Ex. 24.3 (*Traité*, Book III, chapter 41): a bass set to a sarabande-like treble. All but three root motions are cadential fourths and fifths. The analysis decides where cadences conclude (mm. 4, 8) and where they're voiced to keep the phrase moving. The appoggiatura dominant of m. 6: the seventh of the D7, held over as an inner-voice C, motivates the progression (pp. 766–68).

**After the *Traité* (pp. 768–72)**

- The *sous-dominante* and *double emploi* (1726) become central in the *Génération harmonique* (1737): the tonic flanked by an upper and lower fifth, the "triple proportion" F:C:G = 1:3:9. Three functions, though Rameau never uses the word: tonic triad; dominant, descends by fifth, has a seventh; subdominant, ascends by fifth, has an added sixth. The added sixth is the dominant's added third placed below the subdominant, Fig. 24.1 (p. 768).
- The model changes from mechanical dissonance-resolution to the tonic's gravitational pull, on Newton (p. 768 n. 13).
- *Démonstration du principe de l'harmonie* (1750), Plate C: the major scale derived from harmony, two tetrachords C–F and G–C, each harmonized in triple proportion, F–C–G for the lower and C–G–D for the upper. The scale arises from harmony, the position Rameau defends against Rousseau (p. 769).
- The *corps sonore*. Sauveur's demonstration that the harmonic division of a string is audible in its resonance moved Rameau from a Cartesian deductive system (1722 preface) to a Newtonian empirical one: music as "a physico-mathematical science" (1737) (pp. 769–70).
- The *corps sonore* can't generate the minor triad. Rameau proposed an undertone series and dropped it when d'Alembert showed it illusory. Nor can it explain the subdominant, which isn't in the tonic's overtone series; in the triple proportion the subdominant, not the tonic, is the lowest term (p. 771).
- The philosophes: Voltaire, Rousseau, Diderot, d'Alembert's précis of 1752. Rameau ended his life perceived as defensive and, late, mystical (p. 771). Tartini (1754) proposed the *terzo suono* as an alternative source and also failed to derive both triads (pp. 771–72). Serre (1753) and Roussier (1765) attack the practitioner-theorist (p. 772).

**Legacy (pp. 772–74)**

- The fundamental bass spread without attribution: Heinichen (1728), Lampe (1737), C. P. E. Bach, who denounced Rameau but opened his 1762 thorough-bass manual with 5/3, 6/3, 6/4 (p. 772).
- Kirnberger separates "essential" sevenths, unprepared, from "accidental" dissonances, prepared. Marpurg (1755) on harmonic versus passing and changing notes. General terms for nonharmonic tones arrive only in the nineteenth century (p. 773).
- Kirnberger and Nichelmann analyze Bach with the fundamental bass. Mozart taught harmony and composition with it to two pupils in the mid-1780s. Koch's "species counterpoint" is a graded series of chord vocabularies. Albrechtsberger, Beethoven's teacher in the early 1790s, recasts Fux in 1790 so that the student first finds the harmonies under the cantus firmus (pp. 773–74).
- Symbols: Trydell's "harmonical figures" (1766), Vogler's few Roman numerals soon after, Weber's (1817), hence *Stufentheorie*. Tonic-dominant-subdominant thinking in Schröter, Sorge, Daube; function theory only with Riemann (p. 774).

**What unit 3 takes from it**

- The vocabulary the Classical composers were taught: root progression, cadence as the model of all motion, the *dominante-tonique*, the subdominant as the dominant's mirror. Mozart's teaching is the documented link.
- Rameau's key as a focus that emerges from cadential progression. Set it against Rosen's polarized keys and Hepokoski's tonic projected outward ("Beyond," p. 118) when students describe the arrival of the second key.
- Lester's closing gap, no theory relating harmony to phrase and form, is what Hepokoski's Sonata Theory addresses: the EEC and ESC define formal events by cadence.
- The triple proportion and the *Démonstration* fundamental basses (F, C, G; C, G, D) are adjacent fifths, the arrangement Burnett's hexachords use. The unit 2 synthesis records the same line-of-fifths layout in Hauptmann.

## schmid, "mozart and haydn," translated by ernest sanders (pp. 145–61) · 1732 to 1809

*Musical Quarterly*, April 1956, the bicentenary year. No footnotes. Two plates between pp. 156 and 157: Barbara Krafft's 1819 Mozart portrait and a page of Leopold Mozart's letter of February 14, 1785.

**What the essay claims**

- "Viennese Classicism" is an inadequate label for two composers whose lives enclose each other: Haydn's seventy-seven years contain Mozart's whole career, and his best-known works postdate Mozart's death (pp. 145, 149).
- The two aren't rooted in the same traditions. Haydn had direct contact with the Viennese Baroque (Fux, Caldara, his predecessor Werner); Mozart received the Baroque through his father and, later, through van Swieten (pp. 145–46).
- Influence ran from Haydn to Mozart until the early 1780s and then in both directions; Haydn's late style is "essentially autogenous" (pp. 154, 159, 161).

**Backgrounds (pp. 146–50)**

- Folk music. Haydn's from Rohrau's Leitha district, German, Croatian, Hungarian, and Gypsy; Mozart's only as stylized student songs, guild songs, and dances in Leopold's notebooks, from the milieu of Rathgeber's *Tafelkonfekt* (pp. 146–48).
- Haydn puts folk material in his most ambitious works, the London symphonies; Mozart keeps it for cassations and divertimentos (p. 148).
- Character read from portraits and birthplaces: Haydn's "peasant stock," slow maturity, likened to Bruckner, his mature works coming in his forties; Mozart's refinement and early genius (pp. 148–49). Minuets: Mozart's courtly, Haydn's trios near the Ländler (pp. 149–50).

**Contacts, documented and proposed (pp. 150–59)**

- Haydn's symphony "No. 13 in the Breitkopf Gesamtausgabe," finale dated 1763 in the manuscript, uses the four-note motive of the "Jupiter" finale, with varied counterpoints and a four-part stretto like that of Mozart's F-major Mass Credo (1774). The motive appears in the slow movement of Mozart's first symphony (London, early 1765). Schmid proposes that Mozart saw Haydn's symphony in Paris in 1764, where both composers were first printed that year, or through Michael Haydn, Salzburg Konzertmeister from 1762 (pp. 150–51).
- Mozart's later uses of the motive: Sanctus of the C-major Mass (1776), B-flat Symphony (1779), E-flat Violin Sonata (1785), the "Jupiter" (p. 151).
- *Acht Sauschneider müssen sein*: Haydn's Capriccio (autograph 1765) and Mozart's *Galimathias musicum* (1766) set the same tune (pp. 151–52).
- Leopold's letter of August 1771 asks Nannerl for a Haydn trio in F and a C-major trio labeled "Wagenseil" (p. 152).
- Op. 20 (1772), under Werner's influence, reverts to Baroque procedure: three fugal finales, the first violin's leadership reduced. Gassmann's fugal quartet finales follow a year later. Mozart's six Viennese quartets of 1773 adopt the contrapuntal texture and the minuet as third movement; the first one's Andante uses the theme Haydn uses in the fugal finale of Op. 20 no. 5, in the same key (pp. 152–53).
- Haydn's symphonies of the 1770s move from severe counterpoint to integrated part-writing and thematic development. Mozart jotted the themes of Haydn's nos. 47, 62, and 75 on the back of an autograph from the early 1780s. Pohl hears no. 62's Andante in the letter duet of *Figaro*; Geiringer hears Mozart's influence in no. 75 (pp. 153–54).
- Opera: Haydn's *L'incontro improvviso* and *Die Entführung*; Haydn's letter to Prague after *Don Giovanni* (October 1787), ceding opera buffa to Mozart (p. 155).
- Meeting places: the houses of Kees, where Haydn's symphonies of the 1780s were rehearsed; Greiner; Genzinger; Storace, where Kelly heard a quartet of Haydn, Dittersdorf, Mozart, and Vanhal (pp. 156–57).
- The "Haydn" quartets (1782–85), modeled on Op. 33; Haydn's remark to Leopold in February 1785; "I have learned from Haydn how to write quartets"; the rebuke to Koželuch (p. 158).
- Shared exposure to Baroque music through van Swieten: Mozart's C-minor Mass and Haydn's St. Cecilia Mass (pp. 158–59).
- In 1788 Mozart wrote his last three symphonies while Haydn, "having just finished his Paris symphonies," was working on the "Oxford" (p. 159).

**After 1791 (pp. 160–61)**

- Mozart's farewell to Haydn in December 1790; Haydn's letters to Genzinger and Puchberg on Mozart's death; advice to Broderip to buy the manuscripts (p. 160).
- Mozartean traits in late Haydn: the bass motive of the *Nelson Mass* Qui tollis after the *Requiem* Tuba mirum; wind writing and instrumental individualization (p. 161).

**The essay as a source**

- No quotation or claim is sourced. The letters, the Beethoven deathbed anecdote, Kelly's quartet, Carpani, and Niemtschek's dedication need tracing to editions before a student can cite them.
- The character portraits from physiognomy and birthplace are the essay's method in its first half, and the essay is itself a document of 1950s reception.
- The influence claims cite proximity and plausibility: a print in Paris, a conduit in Michael Haydn, a tune in common. Schmid labels several as assumptions (pp. 151–52).

**What unit 3 takes from it**

- A chronology of contact for Weeks 10–12, from Haydn's early symphonies through the Paris set to 1788.
- A test case in evaluating a secondary source: what kind of evidence each claim offers, and what a student would have to verify before citing it. Brown's article answers several of the same questions with different evidence (see "where the readings collide").
- Nothing on Haydn 84 or 90 specifically.

## brown, "eighteenth-century traditions and mozart's 'jupiter' symphony k.551" (pp. 157–95) · 1705 to 1791

*Journal of Musicology*, Spring 2003, published after Brown's death; a revision of a paper for the Hofstra Mozart conference of February 1991 (p. 157). The JSTOR cover title reads "Traditional"; the article's own title page reads "Traditions."

**What the article argues**

- Analyses of K.551 treat it in isolation. Brown places it in its musical context: Viennese court traditions back to Charles VI, composers alive in the 1780s, and the circumstances of 1788 (pp. 158–59).
- The first movement belongs to the Viennese C-major trumpet overture and symphony. The slow movement is a French keyboard sarabande, the minuet is of the serious type with a mixed trio, and the finale is a contrapuntal acclamation, an Alleluia or Gloria followed by fanfare. Together they meet "every Viennese celebratory requirement" (abstract, pp. 194–95).
- The symphony's departures from celebratory affect (Sturm und Drang, a buffa quotation, a duel) are ordered under "a consistent hierarchy of affect" (p. 170). Sulzer's and Koch's *Charakter* is the eighteenth-century category that fits (pp. 192–94).
- The symphony suits a victory celebration after the Turkish War; it may have gone unheard in Mozart's Vienna because the occasion came only with the peace of August 1791 (pp. 190–92).

**The Viennese background (pp. 158–63)**

- Revisions to the biography: Mozart had cash-flow problems rather than poverty, partly from the war's general lull; the G-minor Symphony as personal anguish has been reconsidered; the trilogy was meant for a subscription series in autumn or winter 1788 (pp. 158–59).
- Scholarship has linked Mozart to North Germany (Handel, the Bachs) and to an anti-Mozart court. Against this: the court theaters staged his operas; he succeeded Gluck as Kammermusicus (November 1788); Salieri respected him and lent him old scores from the court library (Hüttenbrenner) (pp. 159–60).
- Documented study: Froberger's hexachord Fantasia arranged in 1782; copies of Reutter's church music, one page also with sketches for the "Jupiter" finale (Tyson) (p. 161).
- Teaching: composition students 1785–87, Attwood, Ployer, Freystädtler; Mann ties the teaching to Fux's *Gradus* and Haydn's "Elementarbuch" (p. 161).
- Rheinhardt's *Rubriche generali*, the ceremonial calendar of Charles VI's chapel (pp. 161–62). The court opera overtures (Fux, Caldara, Reutter, Bonno, Wagenseil) scored for one or two choirs of trumpets with timpani (p. 162).
- The court trumpet corps numbered sixteen trumpeters and two timpanists in 1721 and was gone by 1776. In the Classical orchestra only fanfare formulas remain, and they survive as a series of works with martial themes, of which the "Jupiter" is "a direct outgrowth" (p. 162).
- Trumpet sinfonias are almost all in C; the Italian opera sinfonia prefers D. Mozart's opera sinfonias: D eleven times, C five, G and E-flat once each (p. 162).
- Of Mozart's seven C-major concert symphonies, five predate Vienna; among them only K.338's first movement trumpets. Of the last six, the first movements of K.425 and K.551 are saturated with fanfare rhythm (p. 163).

**First movement (pp. 163–70)**

- Mozart places fanfares after the opening flourish as refrain closings to P, T, and K: Pᵏ, Tᵏ, Kᵏ (p. 163). In no other Mozart work does the same material close each section (p. 163 n. 27).
- Refrains in the old trumpet sinfonias: cyclic binders between movements (Ariosti's sinfonia to *Le profezie d'Eliseo*, 1705, with improvised trumpets, Fig. 2), more often first-movement ritornellos. Fanfare refrains after an opening statement are rare: in Vienna only Caldara's Introduzione to *Enone* (1734), Fig. 3; outside the trumpet repertoire Kraus's C-minor Symphony (Vienna, 1783) (pp. 163, 166).
- Three departures from the imperial affect. The switch to a C-minor Sturm und Drang within S, Ex. 1, mm. 81–86 (models: K.338 mm. 13–14; Haydn 48 mm. 12–14 and 51–63). These episodes are parenthetical: removing them would leave a monolithic movement, "at the cost of high drama" (p. 166).
- K, mm. 101–10, quotes Mozart's insertion aria "Un bacio di mano," K.541, for Anfossi's *Le gelosie fortunate*: "You are a bit innocent, my dear Pompeo; go study the ways of the world." A private allusion; Brown speculates that Pompeo is Joseph II (p. 168).
- The development combines Pᵏ with a descending chromatic bass, mm. 171–80, Ex. 4a, unique in eighteenth-century symphonies; its one Mozart analogue is the duel in the *Don Giovanni* Introduzione, Ex. 4b. Brown asks whether it alludes to battle music in the dynastic operas (p. 170).

**Second movement (pp. 170–74)**

- A French sarabande with second-beat emphasis, Ex. 5. Absent from Mozart's earlier symphonic slow movements and from the Viennese symphony after 1750 (Gassmann, Ordonez, Vanhal, Dittersdorf); vestiges in a few Wagenseil symphonies; among pre-1750 C-major court trumpet sinfonias only Rheinhardt's overture to *La più bella* (1715) has one (pp. 170–73).
- The tradition is keyboard, shown by the 32nd-note swirls that fill the silences of mm. 2 and 4. Models available in Vienna: Bach's English Suite BWV 806 (added motion in mm. 10, 12, 20, 24), Fux, and, closest, Muffat's *Componimenti musicali* (c. 1739), suite 3, Ex. 6 (pp. 173–74).
- Non-sarabande passages: the transition and development with dissonance and hemiola; a galant secondary theme (Sadie: Gluck's "Che puro ciel"); recapitulation roulades that make keyboard demands of the bass parts. The affect stays high-style throughout: Mattheson's *grandezza*, Sulzer's "noblest triple gestures" (p. 174).

**Minuet (pp. 174–76)**

- Reichardt's two types, serious and gay. The minuet is serious: downbeat start, descending chromatic material, fanfare, canonic episodes, Fig. 4.
- The trio opens with a consequent before its antecedent, a buffa twist of syntax, replaying the circular strophic form of the song "Sei du mein Trost," K.391. After the double bar a long-note theme anticipates the finale's opening. The trio mixes gay and serious, where the norm puts serious in the minuet and gay in the trio (p. 176).

**Finale (pp. 176–90)**

- Contrapuntal finales have a theatrical and symphonic history: Fux's trumpet overtures (1709, 1716, 1722, 1731) close with fugues. Table 1 lists contrapuntal symphony movements before 1788 by Ordonez, Gassmann, Vanhal, Schacht, Reicha, and others (pp. 176–78).
- Richter: nine of his roughly eighty-three probably authentic symphonies have movements titled fugue or fugato; five of the seven available combine sonata functions, binary repeats, and fugal texture, Table 2. Kirkendale: Richter alone, like Mozart, adds further entries after the opening fugato. No direct parallel beyond the sonata-fugue synthesis (pp. 177–78).
- Ordonez's Symphony D5, finale of a seven-movement "Sinfonia solenne" (probably 1760s): long-note antecedent, sonata orientation, and a structural climax at the recapitulation, Ex. 7. Mozart puts his peroration in the coda (pp. 178–79).
- Gassmann and Vanhal end their fugues with the subject in octaves, the standard Viennese close and the opposite of Mozart's (pp. 179–80).
- Joseph Haydn's contrapuntal finales: nos. 14, 3, 40, 70 (Sonnleithner's "framed" fugue), 5, 52. No. 13's finale opens with the same pitches as the "Jupiter" theme, Ex. 9, but its source distribution and treatment make it an unlikely model (p. 180).
- Michael Haydn as the likelier source. The fugal finale of P.43/MH 287 (c. 1780–81) survives for 45 measures in Mozart's hand and opens with the "Jupiter" pitches reordered. P.19/MH 384 (September 1784, Artaria) supplies a motive used in the quintuple fugue of the coda and the closing theme at m. 115, Ex. 10. P.31/MH 478 (finished February 19, 1788) shares dotted fanfares, a diminished-seventh Sturm und Drang moment, and the combination of sonata and counterpoint, Table 3 (pp. 180–83).
- The C–D–F–E theme. Hyatt King traces it to Palestrina, Klenz through Mozart, Wollenberg to the hymn "Lucis creator" and Fux. Zaslaw and Klenz tie it to the Credo of K.192 as a statement of faith; Brown rejects that, since K.192 was known only in Salzburg and no Credo chant begins with these pitches (pp. 185–86).
- Brown's candidates: the Gloria of Mass IV in the *Liber usualis*, used by Michael Haydn in the *Missa Sancti Aloysii*; a family of Alleluia chants with Viennese connections, used by Michael Haydn in the gradual "Qui sedes" (September 1, 1787). Mozart's letter of August 2, 1788, asks Nannerl to borrow Michael Haydn's two Tutti-masses and the gradual, eight days before K.551 entered his catalogue. Brown leans to the Alleluia, "marginally" (pp. 186–87).
- The finale as acclamation (Pa) answered by fanfare (Pᵏ), each functional unit ending in a fanfare, Fig. 5 and Ex. 14, after imperial processions in which vocal acclamations were followed by a trumpet *Tusch*. Pᵏ is treated in canon and stretto, Ex. 15, mm. 332–44. First-movement allusions recur diminished: *minore* Sturm und Drang at mm. 127 and 335, the duel at m. 233 (pp. 187–90).

**Occasion and character (pp. 190–94)**

- Summer 1788: Vienna emptied by the season and the war; the *Wiener-Zeitung* and its war supplements. K.543 the most normal of the three; K.550's sighs and pathopoeia fit a public mood if the war continued, and its clarinet revision suggests a contemporary performance; K.551 is for "solemn celebration," with its battle music and Sturm und Drang recalling the costs (p. 190).
- The "Jupiter" recalls ceremonies after the earlier Turkish campaign (1697–1707); trumpets, counterpoint, and solemnity emblematize the house of Austria (pp. 191–92).
- Sulzer (1771): a composition must have a fixed character; the composer may emphasize a figure outside it. Koch: retaining a *Charakter* is among the most important requirements. Mozart arranges characteristic styles hierarchically under one ceremonial statement (pp. 192–94).

**What unit 3 takes from it**

- The Paper 3 secondary-source model for the "Jupiter": a segmentation (Fig. 1), a refrain reading of the closing material, and a historical argument from repertoire, documents, and catalogues.
- The refrain question in the Paper 3 prompt. Brown's Pᵏ/Tᵏ/Kᵏ is the refrain, and his n. 27 says Mozart uses it nowhere else.
- The topical reading (fanfare, sarabande, serious minuet, learned style, Sturm und Drang, buffa) next to Hepokoski's generic one, for the same moments.
- A context for the Week 10–11 repertoire: Wagenseil and the court overture tradition; the "Linz" as the other C-major fanfare symphony.

## hepokoski, "back and forth from egmont: beethoven, mozart, and the nonresolving recapitulation" (pp. 127–54) · 1773 to 1810, with later examples

*19th-Century Music* 25, nos. 2–3. A companion to "Beyond the Sonata Principle" (p. 127 n. 1). Both preview Hepokoski and Darcy's *Elements of Sonata Theory*, cited as forthcoming.

**What the essay argues**

- A nonresolving recapitulation is a rhetorical recapitulation, parallel in layout to the exposition, crafted to "fail" in its generic mission of tonal closure. Rare around 1800, easy to identify, and hard to interpret (pp. 127–28).
- Rhetorical and tonal structures don't coincide in such pieces. Closure deferred to a coda is closure outside sonata space, in a zone not historically made for it (p. 134).
- A sonata form is a set of norms that a piece invites listeners to measure it against. Form resides in that dialogue, not on the page or the acoustic surface; a piece can be "both a sonata and not a sonata" (p. 135).
- Expositions that leave the generic course predict nonresolving recapitulations, though a corrective recapitulation can rescue them (p. 149).

**The terms (pp. 128–29)**

- The rhetorical recapitulation is a matter of thematic and modular layout; tonal resolution is a separate matter (p. 128).
- A rotation is an ordered array of thematic modules recycled, usually altered, later in the work; developments may be fully or partly rotational (p. 128 n. 2).
- The EEC, the point of essential expositional closure, is the first satisfactory perfect authentic cadence in the subordinate key that proceeds onward to differing material. What "satisfactory" means is deferred to *Elements* (p. 128).
- The ESC, the point of essential structural closure, is the corresponding PAC in the tonic in the recapitulation. It marks a resolving recapitulation (p. 128).
- P, TR, S, C are primary theme, transition, secondary theme, closing theme. The MC, the medial caesura, is the mid-expositional cadential break of a two-part exposition (p. 128 and n. 3).
- Fig. 1a–b (p. 129), from *Elements*, lays out the exposition and the whole structure, with the "essential sonata trajectory" to the ESC.
- A sonata deformation is a work that contravenes an essential generic marker yet asks to be heard through sonata norms (p. 136).
- First- and second-level defaults: the most standard choice, and the next most standard when the first is overridden. In minor-mode expositions, III is first-level and minor v second-level (p. 144 n. 20).

**Egmont (pp. 128–35)**

- Exposition regular: F minor to A-flat (III), EEC at m. 104. In the recapitulation the drive to the MC is derailed onto D-flat (VI) at m. 207, the dominant of D-flat is prolonged mm. 217–24, MC at m. 224. S follows, transposed, in D-flat; the ESC-substitute PAC at m. 247; the twelve-measure closing zone, transposed, ends at m. 259. Every closure is in D-flat (p. 130).
- Against the fifth-relation defense. The cited Beethoven precedents (Op. 31 no. 1, Op. 53) are major-mode pieces, where a mediant S is unusual, and their recapitulatory submediant is ephemeral and self-corrects to the tonic, by backing up to restart S (Op. 31 no. 1) or by correction within S (Op. 53) (p. 130).
- Rosen's claim that Beethoven "invariably" balances an expositional mediant with a recapitulatory submediant is not true. Counterexamples: *Leonore* 3, S in E (III) recapitulated in C, with no submediant balance in *Leonore* 2 either; Op. 29, first movement, exposition I to VI/vi (A major, A minor), recapitulation S–C in C major-minor-major; Op. 97, first movement, submediant S recapitulated in the tonic (pp. 131–32 and nn. 5–7).
- Minor-mode expositions moving to III recapitulate S and C in the tonic, a third down, not in VI. Fifth-transposition governs works that move to the dominant (p. 132).
- Neo-Riemannian alternative: F minor to D-flat is the L relation, hexatonically adjacent in Cohn's terms, and D-flat is prepared in mm. 15–17. Hepokoski grants the question and keeps the period's diatonic tonic/nontonic binary (p. 133).
- Program: the recapitulation's failure enacts Egmont's failure to liberate the Netherlands; his execution (m. 278, after Calhoun) is the precondition for the coda's resolution. The link mm. 259–86 shows D-flat as an upper neighbor to V of F (fermata, mm. 285–86); the *Siegessymphonie*, mm. 287ff., resolves and overturns F minor (pp. 133–34).
- A coda is a parageneric, optional zone. Hepokoski asks whether a closure-providing coda resolves the deferred argument or reflects on its absence, and whether an apotheosis-coda collapses the sonata into a "disposable delivery system" (p. 134).
- The two disputable postulates behind the easy answer: the mid-twentieth-century harmonic definition of sonata form (Ratner) and the sonata principle (Cone). Neither distinguishes closure inside sonata space from closure outside it (pp. 134–35).

**Beethoven, Piano Trio in G, Op. 1 no. 2, Largo con espressione (pp. 136–43)**

- Composed 1793–94, published 1795. E major (VI of the trio's G). P a nine-measure sentence (Caplin's example of expanded cadential function); TR of dissolving-restatement type from m. 9 toward V, but in a premonitory B minor; dominant lock at m. 18; MC at m. 23 on a half cadence in the dominant minor, caesura-fill mm. 24–25 (p. 136).
- S over a pulsing F♯. The antecedent, mm. 26–31, prolongs V of B. The consequent at m. 32 begins toward B major, and at m. 33 B major decays to B minor. At m. 34, with F♯ and D as common tones, a fortissimo push into G major (VI of B minor); piano at m. 35. The rhetorical EEC-substitute closes in G at m. 40, the "wrong key" (pp. 137–40).
- Two readings of mm. 40–47 before the recapitulation at m. 47: a retransition after an exposition ending at m. 40, or a closing zone that dissolves at once. The ambiguity may be the point (p. 140).
- Recapitulation: S at m. 67 in the tonic over a pulsing dominant; the consequent at m. 73; E minor at m. 74; push onto C major, mm. 75–76, which would be the fifth-transposed balance of the exposition's G. Instead C slumps to A minor, mm. 78–79, and S closes at m. 82 in A minor, minor iv of E (p. 140).
- Mm. 82–90 and 90–100 restore E major with a PAC. Either a recapitulatory extension, or a discursive coda that corrects what the sonata failed to do. Hepokoski rejects a sonata-rondo reading (n. 18) and holds the strain of the decision as the expressive point (pp. 140–43).
- Related movements: the E-major Adagio of Op. 2 no. 3 and the E-major Largo of the Third Concerto (p. 136 n. 15); Op. 10 no. 2's A-flat Adagio as a Type 1 sonata with discursive coda (p. 143 n. 18).

**Mozart, String Quartet in D minor, K.173, first movement (pp. 144–48)**

- Vienna, September 1773; Artaria printed the first two movements with an earlier finale in 1792, three years before the Beethoven trio (p. 144).
- Of seventeen earlier minor-mode sonata movements by Mozart that Hepokoski consulted, fourteen move to III, three to minor v, all normatively. Fn. 20 lists them (p. 144).
- K.173's exposition splits its cadences among three nontonic minor keys: A minor (v) at mm. 18, 22, 24; E minor (ii) at m. 33; G minor (iv) at m. 42. Four tonal zones, two of them counter-generic (p. 147).
- A dialogue with the second type of continuous exposition, which lacks a proper MC and S: an early PAC (m. 18) driven "into a cadential ditch," then repeated backing up to defer closure. Haydn's Op. 20 no. 3 as a general model; K.458 and Haydn 88 as normative examples of the type (pp. 147–48 and n. 23).
- Recapitulation from m. 65; the crux at m. 80, after which mm. 80–112 transpose mm. 10–42 a fifth down. The D-minor PAC at m. 88 threatens a premature ESC; the phrase from m. 95 moves to A minor (m. 103) and closes on C minor (vii) at m. 112. Retransition from m. 113; repeat of development and recapitulation; the tonic PAC only in the coda at m. 132, pianissimo, with two forte unison reiterations (pp. 148–49).
- Interpretive range: melancholy tipping into madness, after Sisman's paper on the eighteenth-century labyrinth of melancholy, with caution about grand claims for a seventeen-year-old's piece (pp. 147, 149).

**Mozart, Piano Concerto in E-flat, K.449, Andantino (p. 149)**

- The reverse case: S begins in F (V) at m. 41 and closes in A-flat (♭VII). The recapitulation (from m. 80) interpolates a circle-of-fifths "magic passage," mm. 103–6, that subverts the transposition and produces the ESC in B-flat at m. 116. Generic containment wins.

**Three categories (pp. 151–53)**

- Category One: minor-mode sonatas whose major-mode S and C return in minor. Tonally closed, resolving, and not deformational; "sonata failure" in expression, not nonresolution. Mozart's virtually invariable practice: K.183 and K.550 outer movements; first movements of K.421, K.310, K.478, K.516. Beethoven more variable: Op. 2 no. 1, Op. 10 no. 1, the Ninth's first movement; sometimes he begins the recapitulatory S in major and extinguishes it into minor (pp. 151–52).
- Category Two: S space kept from a PAC in both exposition and recapitulation, though in the proper keys. Haydn's Op. 20 no. 3, first movement; Beethoven's Fifth, finale (S from m. 45 in G); the finales of the Second and Eighth Symphonies and Op. 59 no. 3; Bruckner. Closure is deferred to the coda (pp. 152–53).
- Category Three: recapitulations ending in a nontonic key: Egmont, Op. 1 no. 2 (one reading), K.173. Later: Brahms 3, first movement (F to A major then A minor; recapitulatory S in D major then D minor), read as an "uncorrected" Op. 53 pattern (n. 32); Mahler 6, first movement; Glinka, *Ruslan and Ludmila* overture; Tchaikovsky, *Romeo and Juliet*; Saint-Saëns 3; Rachmaninov 2, finale; Mahler 5, second movement (p. 153).

**What unit 3 takes from it**

- The Sonata Theory vocabulary the Paper 3 prompt uses: MC, EEC, ESC, rotation, default levels, deformation. Fig. 1 is the one-page reference.
- Week 13's three Beethoven movements appear in the notes as tests of the fifth-balance claim. Op. 53 corrects its submediant; Op. 29 recapitulates its submediant S up a third in C; Op. 97 recapitulates its submediant S in the tonic.
- For Paper 4, the categories and default levels name what the Kreutzer's keys do: minor v as a second-level default, and the minor-mode recapitulation of Category One.
- The decay of a major S into minor (Op. 1 no. 2, m. 33) as a structural event, the model for reading mode switches as the Paper 4 prompt asks.

## hepokoski, "beyond the sonata principle" (pp. 91–154) · 1772 to 1810; the term from 1952

*Journal of the American Musicological Society* 55, no. 1. The longer of the pair. Works cited, pp. 151–54.

**What the essay argues**

- The "sonata principle" is a mid-twentieth-century English-language construct, rarely examined, that functions as a background truism (pp. 92–93).
- Its early formulations, above all Cone's, overreach. Applied to unusual pieces, it supplies a catchphrase in place of analysis (p. 96).
- Properly restricted, it applies only to post-MC expositional material, S and C, and its expected tonic return in the recapitulation (pp. 112, 118).
- Where a piece departs from even that restricted norm, the departure is the analytical point. Hepokoski offers Sonata Theory's alternatives: rotation, the trimodular block, the synecdochic and substitution strategies, sonata types (pp. 129–30, 147).

**Concept history (pp. 91–102)**

- Four late-century orthodoxies: Rosen's overviews, the motivic quest for unity (Schoenberg, Réti, Keller), Ratner's historical-rhetorical approach, Schenkerian analysis (p. 91); also LaRue, Newman, Tovey, and Caplin (n. 1).
- Haimo's formulation (1995) and Webster's in the second *New Grove*, both hedged (pp. 92–93). Counterexamples at once: K.338's first closing theme, mm. 64–74, never recurs; the B-minor theme at m. 23 of Op. 10 no. 3 never sounds in the tonic (it recurs at m. 204 on E minor); Egmont's S and C in D-flat (pp. 94–95).
- Tovey's resistance to labels (1927, 1929, 1934) and Schenker's (1935) (pp. 97–99). Ratner (1949) moves the definition from themes to key relations, which lets thematic anomalies be dismissed as secondary (pp. 99–100).
- Barford coined "sonata-principle" in 1952 with nothing tonal in it: a dialectic of rules and imagination culminating in Beethoven. Mellers (1957): there is no sonata form, only sonatas (pp. 100–101).
- Cone (Oberlin lectures, January 1967; published 1968): important statements in a key other than the tonic must be restated in the tonic, or brought into closer relation with it, before the movement ends. Illustrations: the Eroica coda, the K.482 Andante, the *Pathétique* finale (pp. 101–2).

**Cone tested (pp. 102–18)**

- Developmental episodes don't require resolution. Counterexamples: Op. 2 no. 1, finale, a fifty-one-measure A-flat episode (mm. 59–109) never heard again; K.478, first movement, mm. 104–33; K.338; the D-major episode in Haydn 45's first movement, against Webster's claim that the finale "implicitly recapitulates" it; K.156, K.285b, K.458 (pp. 104–8).
- When a new developmental idea does return in a coda (K.330, mm. 59–64 and 145–50), rotation explains it: codas often recycle the opening of the development (pp. 108–10). The Eroica coda retraces the development and recapitulation in order; its E-flat minor statement of the "new" theme (mm. 589–93) reanimates the tonic-minor sounding in the development (pp. 110–11).
- Conclusion: the principle applies only to nontonic post-MC zones of expositions and recapitulations, not developments or codas (p. 112). TR material is excluded, however tonal its suggestions (n. 38).
- Rondos: nontonic central episodes usually don't return (Beethoven's first three concerto finales, K.281, Op. 2 no. 3, K.361). When they do, codas are retrospective or summary, and they're in the tonic because codas ground the tonic (pp. 112–15).
- "Closer relation" is underdeveloped. How is Egmont's five-flat D-flat closer to F minor than the exposition's four-flat A-flat? The *Pathétique* coda's A-flat moment (mm. 203–6) belongs with Beethoven's wrong-key rondo-theme statements in Op. 2 no. 3 (m. 297, A major) and Op. 7 (mm. 155–60, E major) (pp. 115–18).

**Rosen and Haydn's Op. 64 no. 3 (pp. 118–30)**

- Rosen's version: tonic and dominant are polarized, and material in the dominant is a "dissonant section" requiring resolution. Hepokoski prefers the tonic moving outward to a projection of itself (p. 118).
- Rosen's exception that proves the rule: Op. 64 no. 3's first S theme doesn't return in the recapitulation but appears in the development on the tonic, in minor (pp. 119–20).
- Hepokoski's reading of the exposition. MC at m. 32 (half cadence in F); S1 at m. 33, a dolce Ländler; its repetition decays to F minor at m. 37 ("lights out"); D-flat at m. 40, an augmented sixth at m. 41; a second MC at m. 42; caesura-fill mm. 42–47; a new S at m. 48, its continuation from m. 52; EEC at m. 65 (pp. 120–24).
- The apparent double medial caesura produces a trimodular block (TMB): TM1 after the first MC fails to reach a PAC; TM2 dissolves into transition; TM3, after the second MC, is the more successful S (pp. 123–24).
- The recapitulation suppresses the first MC, omits TM1, recomposes TR to the single MC at m. 152, and leaps at m. 153 to the transposition of m. 52. Nineteen measures of S are suppressed, fourteen kept (pp. 124–25).
- The development is rotational: P1 m. 70, P2 m. 78, S1 m. 87, P2 m. 97, TM3's opening m. 105, retransition m. 118. S1's B-flat minor is an apparent tonic within I–ii–V–i in A-flat (mm. 78, 87, 94, 97), weakly prepared, never secured by a cadence, and it ends on V of C minor (m. 104). S1 across the three rotations: major-minor, minor-minor, absent (pp. 125–28).
- One purpose of recapitulations is to restore order to problems set in the exposition; Haydn's recapitulations readjust wittily problematized expositions (p. 129).
- The synecdochic strategy: part of S stands for its whole, normally the concluding modules that drive to the EEC. Incomplete recapitulatory S or C space is non-normative and responds to earlier events (p. 130).

**Two axioms and the S zone (pp. 130–40)**

- Axiom 1: a work won't state an S or C module in the tonic in the development and again in the recapitulation. Generally correct; exceptions are deformational (pp. 131–32).
- Axiom 2: a recapitulation won't omit S or C modules unless they've had a tonic statement elsewhere. Doubtful (p. 131).
- P1 in the tonic in the development is common (Koch's early tonic station; false recapitulations, disputed by Hoyt; Bonds's list includes Haydn 48 finale and 41, 55, 91 first movements; Sisman's "Jupiter" monograph, pp. 50–52). The second tonic P1 at the recapitulation is unaffected (pp. 132–33 and nn. 61–64).
- S modules are the exposition's most generically critical materials, since only S produces the EEC and, by promise, the ESC (p. 134). Hypothesis: a tonic S1.1 anywhere after the exposition triggers the drive to the ESC; if aborted, those modules are spent (p. 134).
- Four further hypotheses: prematurely tonic S modules come from S's beginning, and later modules are reserved for the ESC; tonic S modules in a development show signs of provisionality (parallel minor, apparent tonic, weak MC); S modules are the most sensitive, which is why developments more often use P, TR, or C; C modules are postcadential, Koch's *Anhang* and Reicha's *idées accessoires*, and omitting one breaks a lower-order norm (pp. 135–37).
- Sonata types: Type 1 without development, Type 2 binary without full recapitulation, Type 3 textbook, Type 4 sonata-rondo, Type 5 concerto (p. 137 n. 69). Type 2 is common in the Mannheim composers, J. C. Bach, and Mozart (K.16, K.22, K.306, K.311, K.525 finale, and others); Haydn's 44 finale (pp. 137–38). The "reversed recapitulation" is a historically unjustified misreading of Type 2 (n. 71).
- A well-prepared tonic S1 midway through a rotational development proposes Type 2; its quick abandonment revokes the proposal, a "false tonal resolution," the Type 2 analogue of the false recapitulation. It spends those S modules for the recapitulation (p. 138). Weber's *Oberon* overture (n. 74).

**Op. 20 no. 3 and Op. 33 no. 3 (pp. 140–48)**

- Op. 20 no. 3, first movement: after an unusual MC (mm. 24–26), a B-flat S zone of eight modules (mm. 27–ca. 94) with no EEC and no closing theme (n. 76). The recapitulation omits S1.1–1.3; the development treats parts of S1.2 and S1.3 only in E-flat. Rosen's rule fails, and the "argument from fifth-transposition" can't save it (pp. 140–42). Reading: a capricious piece whose sonata trims back expositional overgrowth; the melancholy link to Sisman again (pp. 142–43).
- Op. 33 no. 3, first movement: MC at m. 26, S at m. 27 (S1.1 m. 28 in G; S1.2 m. 30 decays to G minor; S1.3 m. 32; S1.4 m. 36), EEC at m. 42, P-based C mm. 43–56. The recapitulation is continuous, without MC or S proper. The development's second rotation brings S1.4 to a strong PAC in E minor (m. 108), which liquidates it; in the recapitulation a "placidly banal" new idea substitutes and makes the ESC at m. 138 (pp. 143–48).
- The substitution strategy: an S module replaced in the recapitulation by a functional counterpart (p. 147). Beethoven's Fourth Concerto, first movement, replaces its EEC module (n. 85).

**Envoi (pp. 149–51)**

- Nonresolving recapitulations (K.173, Op. 1 no. 2, Egmont) and S-less recapitulations (the *Idomeneo* overture; truncated slow-movement recapitulations in Haydn's Op. 33 nos. 5 and 6 and Mozart's K.575) break the principle outright and are set aside (pp. 149–50).
- The principle served an analytical climate that downplayed themes in favor of keys, combining quick answers with self-confirmation. In the most interesting cases it's inadequate (pp. 150–51).

**What unit 3 takes from it**

- The S zone as the sensitive part of the form: where the second key's arrival is secured, decayed, or replaced. Paper 3's "what does each composer do to the arrival of the second key" is this question.
- Haydn's recapitulations as readjustments of problematized expositions, a hypothesis to test on Haydn 90.
- Sonata types for the Week 10–11 symphonies: binary (Type 2) layouts are common in the earlier works, and recapitulations that begin at S are Type 2, not reversed.
- The false recapitulation as a disputed category, with Sisman's discussion of the "Jupiter" in n. 64.

## where the readings collide

**Four kinds of explanation**

- Each reading explains a compositional choice differently. Schmid by temperament, background, and personal contact. Brown by inherited tradition, occasion, and eighteenth-century *Charakter*. Hepokoski by generic norms and their deformation, read hermeneutically. Lester by the theory available to composers and how it was taught.
- For any one moment in the "Jupiter" (the C-minor episode, the K quotation, the finale theme), students can state what each kind of explanation would claim and what evidence it would need.

**Haydn 13 and the "Jupiter" theme: Schmid against Brown**

- Schmid (pp. 150–51): Haydn's no. 13 finale (1763) uses the motive with stretto like Mozart's 1774 Credo; Mozart may have seen it in Paris in 1764 or through Michael Haydn.
- Brown (p. 180): the pitch sequence is shared, but source distribution and treatment make no. 13 an unlikely model. He looks to Michael Haydn (Mozart's own copy of P.43; the 1788 letter) and to chant, and rejects the K.192 Credo association (pp. 180–87).
- Both name Michael Haydn as conduit. Brown doesn't cite Schmid. The comparison isolates the evidence for each claim: proximity in Schmid; autograph copies, publication records, and a dated letter in Brown.

**Mozart's teaching: Lester against Brown**

- Lester (p. 773): Mozart taught harmony and composition with the fundamental bass to two pupils in the mid-1780s. No citation.
- Brown (p. 161): three composition students, 1785–87 (Attwood, Ployer, Freystädtler), with Mann tying the teaching to Fux and Haydn's "Elementarbuch"; the studies are edited in NMA X/30 (n. 18).
- The same years and probably the same pupils, described as Rameauian harmony in one account and Fuxian counterpoint in the other. Albrechtsberger's 1790 recasting of Fux, which begins from the harmonies under the cantus (Lester, pp. 773–74), joins the two.

**The "Jupiter" C-minor episode: Brown's parenthesis against Hepokoski's decay**

- Brown (p. 166): the Sturm und Drang switch at m. 81 is parenthetical, removable without structural loss. His Figure 1 labels it "Pb, ext.," iv/V, after a GP.
- Hepokoski ("Beyond," p. 123): a secondary theme that destabilizes into minor is a common extension strategy with local implications; it isn't an expressively neutral option. The apparent double MC and TMB (pp. 123–24) and the modal decay of Op. 1 no. 2 ("Egmont," p. 137) are models for testing whether mm. 56–100 contain one MC or two and where the EEC falls.
- The Paper 3 prompt asks for exactly this passage.

**The "Jupiter" false recapitulation**

- Brown's Figure 1 marks a false retransition at m. 157 and a false recapitulation in IV at m. 161, with Pᵏ over the descending bass to follow.
- Hepokoski ("Beyond," pp. 132–33 n. 63–64): false recapitulation is a contested category (Bonds; Hoyt disputes it), and he cites Sisman's "Jupiter" monograph (pp. 50–52) for the issue. A tonic P1 in a development is the usual case; Mozart's is in IV.

**Refrain, ritornello, rotation**

- Brown hears the fanfare refrains as descendants of trumpet-sinfonia ritornellos (p. 163). Unit 2's ritornello (Dreyfus, Burnett) is the near background.
- Hepokoski hears recurrence as rotation: ordered recycling of modules, with closing zones and codas that turn back to P ("Beyond," pp. 109–10; "Egmont," p. 128 n. 2).
- Same phenomenon, two historical lineages: a refrain inherited from the Baroque ritornello against a norm of the sonata as genre.

**Op. 20: learned style against caprice**

- Schmid (pp. 152–53): Op. 20 is Haydn's turn to Baroque counterpoint under Werner, serious in form and content, the model for Mozart's 1773 quartets.
- Hepokoski ("Beyond," pp. 140–43; "Egmont," p. 147): Op. 20 no. 3's first movement is "extravagantly capricious," a staged disorder, a general model for K.173's derailed exposition, and a Category Two case.
- The two readings address different movements and different questions; set side by side, they show that "influence" names a different thing in each.

**Harmony and form**

- Lester (p. 774): eighteenth-century theory left no framework for relating harmony to phrase and form.
- Ratner (1949), cited from eighteenth-century theorists, defined Classical form by key relations ("Beyond," pp. 99–100). Hepokoski reintroduces thematic layout and cadence as generic markers.
- Rameau's key emerges from cadential progression (Lester, p. 762); Rosen's keys are polarized; Hepokoski's tonic projects outward ("Beyond," p. 118). Three models of what the arrival of a second key is.

**Fifths, signatures, and the system method**

- Hepokoski rejects fifth-relations as a source of tonal balance ("Egmont," pp. 130–32; "Beyond," p. 142) and asks how D-flat (five flats) is closer to F minor than A-flat (four flats) ("Beyond," p. 115).
- Burnett's systems are counted in signatures and laid out in fifths. In system terms, Egmont's recapitulation is one signature flatward of its exposition.
- For Papers 3 and 4: a system change and a formal event are separate observations. The prompt asks where they coincide. Hepokoski's position is that a system close to the tonic doesn't count as tonal resolution.

**Neo-Riemannian proxies**

- Hepokoski entertains hexatonic substitution (F minor and D-flat; B major and G; E minor and C major) three times and declines it for this repertoire ("Egmont," pp. 133, 138 n. 17; "Beyond," p. 147 n. 83). A forward link to 602.

**What the reading doesn't cover**

- Haydn 2, 84, and 90; Stamitz; J. C. Bach's Op. 6 no. 6; RV 158; Wagenseil's Sinfonia in E; the Kreutzer.
- No reading in the unit applies the hexachord systems to a Classical work.

## the paper 4 directed questions

From `units/unit-3.html`. Kreutzer, first movement.

- Take the major/minor mode switch as the organizing gesture. Which dyad conflicts realize it, and where is each first heard?
- Follow the system battle (3♯ against 0, and the flat side). Where do the dyad conflicts also act as system modulations?
- Why a minor v in the second harmonic area? What would a stable major V or ♭III cost the movement?
- Name the single idea the movement serves, and show how the coda settles the conflicts it opened.

Where the readings meet each question:

- Mode switch: Hepokoski's decays of major into minor as structural events ("Egmont," p. 137; "Beyond," pp. 122–23) and Category One (pp. 151–52), where Beethoven sometimes begins the recapitulatory S in major and extinguishes it into minor.
- System battle: no unit 3 reading; the table below.
- Minor v: second-level default for minor-mode expositions ("Egmont," p. 144 n. 20; p. 151). Hepokoski calls an EEC in minor v in a major-mode exposition a strong reversal of expectations (p. 137); the Kreutzer's main body is in minor, where minor v is generic.
- The coda: "Egmont," p. 134 on codas as parageneric zones; Category Two's closure deferred to the coda (pp. 152–53). Whether the Kreutzer's recapitulation resolves inside sonata space is the question that separates its coda from Egmont's.

- C♮ against C♯ is both the A-major/A-minor mode switch and the 3♯/0 system change. D♯ in A minor is the augmented-second spelling that moves 0 to 3♯.

- A stable ♭III (C major) shares A minor's 0 system. A stable major V (E) is 4♯, one signature beyond the 3♯ of A major. A minor v (E minor) is 1♯.

## citation discrepancies in the readings

- Brown's JSTOR cover gives the title as "Eighteenth-Century Traditional and Mozart's 'Jupiter' Symphony K.551" and the author as "A. Peter Brown Brown." The title page reads "Traditions." Students exporting from JSTOR or a database record will reproduce the error.
- Brown's text dates Koch's statement on *Charakter* "as late as 1812" (p. 194); n. 70 cites the *Musikalisches Lexikon* (Frankfurt am Main, 1802/1817), cols. 313–14.
- Brown n. 62 refers to "Ex. 20" for the closest Alleluia similarities (p. 186). The article's examples end at Ex. 15.
- Brown's Figure 1 exposition row places "m. 9" and "m. 24" one label to the left of the recapitulation row's equivalents (see the Figure 1 table).
- Lester dates the *Code de musique pratique* 1761 (p. 771). The Cambridge History's pedagogy chapter in Part I (p. 55) gives 1760.
- Lester's text calls Rameau's second cadence the Irregular Cadence (*cadence irrégulière*) (p. 762); Plate 24.2's caption reads "The imperfect cadence" (p. 763).
- Lester gives no source for Mozart's teaching with the fundamental bass (p. 773).
- Hepokoski's "Egmont" essay is 19th-Century Music 25, nos. 2–3 (Autumn 2001–Spring 2002). "Beyond" cites it as 25 (2002), with no issue numbers ("Beyond," p. 96 n. 10 and Works Cited).
- Both Hepokoski essays cite *Elements of Sonata Theory* as forthcoming. Students cite the published book (Oxford University Press, 2006) for the terms.
- Schmid has no notes. Every quotation in it (Beethoven's deathbed remark, Haydn's letters to Prague, Genzinger, and Puchberg, Leopold's letter of February 1785, Mozart on Koželuch, Carpani) needs tracing to an edition before a student cites it.
- Schmid gives no Köchel or Hoboken numbers. His "first symphony" (London, 1765), "F-major Mass of 1774," "C-major Mass of 1776," "B-flat major Symphony of 1779," and "E-flat major Violin Sonata (1785)" need identifying before citation (p. 151).
