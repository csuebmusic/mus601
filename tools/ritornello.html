The concerto ritornello

The fast movement of the late-Baroque concerto alternates statements of a ritornello with solo episodes. Fischer's three segments, as Dreyfus defines them by harmonic function, give the ritornello its grammar. Burnett and Nitzberg read the same movements as a plan along the reordered hexachord of the key, with system shifts at its furthest points. The diagrams draw the segmentations and ritornello formations the two readings publish.

the three segments

A ritornello is the tutti theme that opens a concerto movement and returns, whole or in part and in other keys, between the solo episodes. Torelli standardized the form in his Op. 8 (1709), transposing the opening theme to degrees of the hexachord so that each auxiliary cadence becomes a complete period (Burnett, pp. 133–34). Wilhelm Fischer named the ritornello's three segments in 1915. Dreyfus keeps the German terms and redefines them as harmonic functions “clothed in thematic identities” (p. 330).

The Vordersatz [V] defines the tonic by reference to its dominant, with root-position triads moving from I to V. Whether it does so outranks how: broken arpeggios, scales, and a string of short motives all qualify (Dreyfus, p. 331). Burnett's Vordersatz can close with an authentic cadence, as Torelli's fugal one does, or stop on a half cadence, as Vivaldi's in Op. 9 no. 2 does (pp. 138, 145).

The Fortspinnung [F] has no defined tonic and no authentic cadence. It moves by conventional voice-leading sequences (10-7-10-7, 5-6-5-6, 10-10-10) or by freer counterpoint, and it delays strong tonal articulation (Dreyfus, p. 331). Burnett's Fortspinnung follows “either a diatonic or a harmonic sequential pattern” and can modulate (p. 138). He also uses the word for the general spinning-out technique of the seventeenth century, already in Marini (p. 98).

The Epilog [E] is the clausula, the formal cadence in the tonic closing on the first scale degree in the upper voice. Its end is fixed and its beginning isn't: it takes over where the Fortspinnung reaches foreign territory, for instance the dominant that sets up the tonic resolution (Dreyfus, p. 331). Dreyfus finds Bach's ritornellos “nearly without exception, tonally closed” (p. 329). Burnett allows an Epilog that confirms the dominant, as Torelli's does (p. 138 n. 26).

The pianoidée [P], Krüger's term (1932), is a segment some Vivaldi ritornellos add after the Fortspinnung. It turns the major tonic to its parallel minor, usually at a lower dynamic with reduced scoring (Burnett, p. 145). Dreyfus's segmentation has three segments and no fourth.

A segment can divide into parts, numbered [V1 V2] or [E1 E2]. Two parts are syntactically equivalent when they fill the same function differently (Dreyfus, p. 336 n. 12). Segments are identified by function rather than contour, so a boundary needs no melodic caesura, and adjoining segments often share notes and chords (p. 333).

Burnett distinguishes two ritornello types. The fugal type can't be segmented and returns whole, as in Torelli's Op. 8 no. 3. The segmented type is the one Vivaldi and Bach use (pp. 134, 138). Dreyfus leaves out fugal ritornellos and those with unison themes, such as BWV 1052's (p. 328 n. 6). Burnett segments the unison ritornello of Vivaldi's Op. 9 no. 2 (p. 145).

marked and unmarked

Dreyfus sets the three segments in a binary matrix borrowed from linguistics (Table I, p. 334). Tonic definition, I to V, is present in the Vordersatz and absent in the other two. Tonic resolution, V to I, is present in the Epilog and absent in the other two. Only some absences count. For tonic definition the marked and unmarked pair is [V] and [F], and for tonic resolution it's [E] and [F]. The absence of tonic definition in [E] and of tonic resolution in [V] isn't significant (pp. 333–34).

The ritornello as a whole is marked and the solo episode unmarked. Dreyfus defines an episode by the absence of complete segments rather than by a contrasting theme. In the first movement of the E-major violin concerto, BWV 1042, mm. 70–78, fragments of the Vordersatz appear twice without the move to the dominant, and the complete Vordersatz arrives in the subdominant under a change of register and dynamics (pp. 334–36). The tutti-solo contrast is, for Dreyfus, part of the decoration: “Who is playing rarely matters” (p. 339).

Dreyfus concludes that the ritornello holds “the essential properties of the harmonic system in a tonal microcosm” (pp. 333–34).

statements and their notation

Dreyfus numbers every statement of ritornello material R1, R2, and so on, and lists the segments it contains in brackets: [V1 V2 F1 F2 E], [V1], [F* E] (pp. 338, 354). An asterisk marks a decorated segment. A statement can be split across solo material, with measures in two places, and two statements in different keys can be coupled into one.

This course writes R for a statement with every segment of the full ritornello and r for one that returns incomplete, numbering both in one series. In the seminar's reading of Vivaldi's RV 522, R1 is [V F E1 E2], and r2 is the second Epilog alone. The charts below follow this notation.

An incomplete statement has consequences. A Vordersatz alone is a complete harmonic progression, enough to stabilize a key (Burnett, p. 140). In the Echo from BWV 831, R3 is [F* E] and resolves the material before it, which gives the preceding solo section pretensions to the status of a Vordersatz (Dreyfus, p. 354).

Burnett glosses Dreyfus's “marked” statement as one that holds enough of one or more segments to stabilize a harmonic area (p. 140). In Dreyfus, marked names the features of Table I and the ritornello as against the solo episode (pp. 333–36). Cite each sense from its own author.

the keys of the statements

Burnett reads a movement's keys along the central hexachord of the key reordered in fifths. In major it reads IV I V ii vi iii. A minor key takes its relative major's hexachord, “there being no such thing as an A minor hexachord,” and reads VI III VII iv i v (pp. 117–18, 127). Triad qualities are fixed, and a third is raised only for an applied dominant (p. 123).

Burnett's model, from Corelli: in major, I, V, vi, sometimes iii with a system shift, then back through IV or ii to I; in minor, i, III, iv or VI, V, i (pp. 132–33). A penultimate ritornello in iii followed directly by the tonic, with no retransition, is common Baroque procedure (p. 153).

the operations

Dreyfus names the operations that generate a movement from its ritornello, set in capitals (pp. 339–55). Bach's ritornello movements matter “more ... as paradigms than as syntagms” (p. 334).

  1. SEGMENT: the ritornello displays its functional subdivisions, and a partial statement has logical consequences. The Echo's R3, [F* E], resolves the preceding material (p. 354).
  2. DECORATE: a change of surface that leaves the syntax intact, written with an asterisk, [V1*]. Inversion of the voices, diminution, and changes of meter and rhythm all qualify. The test is to substitute the corresponding segment of the full ritornello: a seamless substitution shows the syntactic identity (pp. 339, 354).
  3. ORCHESTRATE: a kind of decoration in which piano and forte cover the polarity of ritornello and episode. In BWV 35/1, R8, [F1] at mm. 103–5 is marked piano with a bit of solo counterpoint, and the forte at m. 105 is “a playful deception”: the ritornello was present all along (p. 339).
  4. ARRAY: segments transposed into significant keys. The order of keys reflects the usual hierarchy of scale steps, and Dreyfus stresses that no assigned harmonic plan prefigures it (p. 355).
  5. MODESWITCH: translation of a segment into the opposite mode. It checks for voice-leading errors, rejects the segments it renders non-occurrent, and excludes or alters those whose meaning it changes (pp. 341–43, 355).
  6. RESEARCH: discovery of links between segments already arrayed and modeswitched. In the Echo, R6 and R7 look like one modulating ritornello and are two segments in different keys, joined by a voice-leading coincidence at the end of [V1] in m. 48 (p. 355).
  7. The ideal ritornello is the full set of segments a movement presupposes. It need not be the opening gesture, and in some movements it occurs nowhere intact (p. 346).

the system in A major

In tonal music a signature governs two keys, a major tonic and its relative minor, on one system named by its major root (Burnett, pp. 117–18, 123). The system holds eleven pitch classes: the major scale, the allowable flat on the seventh degree, and the raised thirds of the applied dominants to V, ii, and vi. The twelfth is missing. Spelled as the minor third above the root, it brings the parallel minor and moves the system three signatures flatward. Spelled as the augmented second, it moves the system three signatures sharpward. “Minor thirds are system roots; augmented seconds form tritones with system roots” (p. 128).

In unit 1 the missing pitch moved a modal system by a fifth. In a tonal movement it moves the system by three signatures (pp. 118, 128).

Only one matrix exists at a time, and every harmonic event falls inside the tritone axes of the tonic system. In an A-major movement, C♯ minor belongs to the 6♯ system on F♯, not to a separate 4♯ system (p. 147). The board is Burnett's Figure 4.1. Press a system for its pitch classes, or step through his reading of Vivaldi's Op. 9 no. 2, first movement (pp. 145–51).

Burnett distinguishes two kinds of conflict. A dyad conflict sets a chromatic pitch against its diatonic form under one name, D♯ against D♮. A system conflict sets the two pitches that move the system against each other, C♮ against D♯ (pp. 147–48). “Resolution” then has two senses: a sharp moving up or a flat moving down, and an inflected pitch returning to its diatonic form (p. 151).

Burnett pairs hexachordal opposites under one implied signature: E with c♯, A with f♯, D with b. A chromatic pitch can bind the two areas of a pair, as D♯ binds the dominant and the mediant in Op. 9 no. 2 (p. 148).

two readings of one movement

Burnett reads a concerto movement as a harmonic plan along the reordered hexachord. Ritornellos stabilize its degrees, and system shifts mark its furthest points (pp. 123, 133–34, 148–49). Dreyfus holds that the number of ritornellos, the order of keys, and the overall balance are “anachronistically imposed by formal models derived from later eighteenth-century music.” The sense of the movement proceeds from the relations among ritornellos and from their absence during the solo episodes (p. 334). The two agree that the order of keys follows the hierarchy of scale steps (Dreyfus, p. 355).

The first movement of Brandenburg Concerto no. 2, BWV 1047, in F, is the one movement both analyze. Burnett notes that Dreyfus reads it without system shifts (p. 152 n. 33). The measure numbers differ by one or two at three points, and the score settles where each statement begins.
