Affinities, cofinals, and transposition

The North Italian half of eleventh-century modal theory: what the modes were for, why the quality of a final recurs a fifth above it, and what a chant melody does when the gamut cannot supply the pitches it needs.

what a mode was for

In the ecclesiastical tradition a mode is a category for melodies rather than a pitch system. It developed as a practical means of classifying antiphons and relating them to psalm tones, and extended over the rest of the chant repertory it remained a scheme of classification, resting on final, ambitus, and beginnings. Frans Wiering, in a 1995 dissertation, calls the definition at its core the omnis cantus definition: a mode is the rule by which every song is to be judged, and the final is the source of that rule.

The species doctrine and the affinities are attempts to ground that classification in the structure of the scale. Whether the grounding ever succeeded is a live question in the scholarship, put most sharply by Harold Powers in the title of his 1992 essay “Is Mode Real?”, which argues against treating modality as an inherent property of the music in the way tonality is treated for later repertory.

qualities and modi vocum

Guido of Arezzo (c. 900 to c. 1050) takes up the qualities (qualitates) and affinities (affinitates) of the notes in chapters 7 to 9 of the Micrologus, of 1025/26. He explains them by intervallic patterns he calls modi vocum, modes of notes: the configuration of intervals around a note gives it a kind, and that kind is its quality. His first modus vocum is a whole tone below the note with the ascending series T–S–T–T above it, the configuration around A and D. These intervallic modes are not the church modes.

Octave equivalence leaves seven distinct notes, so there can be at most seven qualities. There turn out to be four. Three pairs share a quality because they share a modus vocum, A with D, B with E, and C with F. The seventh note, G, stands alone with a quality and a modus vocum of its own and no affinity to any other note. Guido does not explain why he assigns G a modus vocum so different from the rest.

the affinities

The pairs come from two six-note segments of the scale, Γ–E and C–a. They are identically structured and they lie a fourth apart, so each note of one has a counterpart in the other with the same intervals around it: A with D, B with E, C with F.

Guido states that affinities occur at the perfect fourth and the perfect fifth, and he gives each pair a third member, the note a fifth above the final: a for D, b♮ for E, c for F. Those three sit in a segment of the same structure again, G–e. The three sets are A/D/a, B/E/b♮, and C/F/c.

Of the three segments, C–a is the one holding the four finals, with a whole tone above and below them. Γ–E replicates it at the fourth below and G–e at the fifth above. Cohen calls the two lower ones substantively identical to the hard and natural hexachords, though not functionally identical; on the gamut the three segments are the durum on Γ, the naturale on C, and the durum on G.

In chapter 8, discussing affinities at the upper fourth, Guido allows G partial affinities with both D and C: G and C have the series T–T–S above them, and G and D share the descending series T–S.

Pseudo-Odo takes up the same phenomenon in the Dialogus de musica, compiled c. 1000, under the name similitudines vocum, likenesses of the notes, assigning each note of his scale except b♭ to one, two, or three modes. Hucbald of Saint-Amand (c. 840 to 930) had already noticed it in his Musica of c. 900 and called it socialitas, and the authors of the late ninth-century Enchiriadis treatises explained it by the recurrence of identical intervallic configurations around notes five steps apart.

cofinals

The affinities answer a practical problem: where to put a chant melody on the scale. A number of traditional melodies predate the modal and scalar systems imposed on them and call for pitches the gamut does not provide, chiefly low B♭, E♭, and F♯. Some theorists advocated low B♭ as a regular note of the scale and some practical sources used it, though this was never a common solution, and the period had no concept of chromatic alteration to make E♭ and F♯ available.

The usual solution was to move the melody to the fifth above and end it on a cofinal, affinalis or confinalis in later theorists: a for the protus, b♮ for the deuterus, c for the tritus. Up there the gamut carries both b♭ and b♮, which often makes the required pitches and intervals available. The tetrardus has no cofinal, since G has no affinity.

transposition at the upper fourth

Guido mentions further affinities at the fourth above the finals, some of them requiring b♭, and disapproves of using b♭ in this way. His objection is that it produces a confusion and transformation of the established qualities: in the segment g–a–b♭–c the g takes on protus quality, the a becomes deuterus, the c becomes tetrardus, and the b♭ itself counts as a tritus note.

Other theorists of the time accepted the procedure, under the name transformation. It stayed much less common than transposition to the cofinal at the upper fifth for as long as the music in question was plainchant, and it is increasingly accepted from the thirteenth century, especially among theorists of the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries writing about mode in polyphony. From the early fourteenth century theorists admit the transformative transpositions at the upper fourth as a matter of course, with the protus situated on G as re and the deuterus on a as mi.

what survives a transposition

Species theory later validated the upper-fourth transposition, since it preserves the modal species for all four categories exactly, while the upper-fifth cofinals do not. The protus shows the difference. At its cofinal a the fifth a–e runs T–S–T–T, still the first species, while the fourth above it, e–a, runs S–T–T, which is the second species and not the first. Transposed instead to G with b♭, the fifth G–d runs T–S–T–T and the fourth d–g runs T–S–T, both the first species, and the mode arrives intact.

what happens to the affinities afterward

From the late thirteenth century the hexachord syllables are brought into modal theory, beginning with Jerome of Moravia's Tractatus de musica (c. 1272 to 1304), and they take on a significance as markers of modal quality and affinity they hadn't carried before. For the first three categories, re, mi, and fa in the natural and hard hexachords give both the finals, D, E, and F, and the cofinals, a, b♮, and c, while sol gives G for the tetrardus. From the early fourteenth century theorists increasingly admit the transposition at the upper fourth with b♭, the protus sitting on G as re and the deuterus on a as mi.

By about 1500 the flat signature has divided the Guidonian gamut into two scales. Cantus durus, for pieces without a flat signature, takes the natural and hard hexachords and uses b♮. Cantus mollis, for pieces with one flat, takes the natural and soft hexachords and uses b♭. Some theorists recognize a third scale, cantus fictus, with a two-flat signature, joining the soft hexachord to a fictive hexachord whose ut lies on b♭ and which carries e♭ as fa. Those practices together with species theory bring complete acceptance of the transposition at the upper fourth.

The tritus runs the other way. It uses b♭ regularly in its untransposed position on F, so for the tritus it's cantus durus that signals transposition, putting the final at the old upper-fifth cofinal c, or an octave lower on C. Cantus fictus moves things a further fourth up, so that C, solmized as re in the fictive hexachord on b♭, can serve as an irregular final for the protus.

The cofinals themselves do not survive. A growing conviction that the octave species formed by joining a species of fifth to a species of fourth were the true determinants of mode brought dissatisfaction with the upper-fifth cofinals, and Glarean's Dodecachordon (Basel, 1547) rejects them, promoting a and c to regular finals of modes of their own: Aeolian and Hypoaeolian on a, Ionian and Hypoionian on c.
