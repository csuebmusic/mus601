6. Tonal types and modal categories

A tonal type is the combination of clef, system, and final of the lowest voice. Powers builds his argument from the way editors and publishers ordered their collections, and Rore's first book of madrigals of 1542 is the specimen. The first seventeen madrigals of the print follow the order of the eight modes. Rore's collection, together with the Susato motet anthologies and works by Lasso and Palestrina, is the principal evidence from which Powers articulated the theory. The markers are the ones editors and publishers such as Susato, Scotto, and Gardano appear to have used in ordering their collections, and the ordered print of Rore's motets that Gardano issued in 1545 suggests the arrangement came from the composer.

Powers holds that the relationship of the twenty-four possible tonal types to systems of eight or twelve modes is varied and not necessarily causal. A tonal type may serve as a compositional means of representing a mode, or as an editorial means of classifying one, and it need not do either. The distinction he draws is between polyphonic works written in the modes and works that may be assigned to modes after the fact.

The opposing position takes melodic features, the articulation of species, and the characteristic use of the repercussion as modal determinants, features set out in chant theory and applied by analogy to the individual voices of a polyphonic complex. Meier argues it at length, positing modality as an inherent property of sixteenth-century polyphony, so that by definition all music of the period is in the modes.

Lasso's Lagrime di San Pietro no. I, the Paper 1 work, is a natural test: its tonal type is ♮, c1 clefs, final D. What follows from that, and what does not, is the question.
