5. Eight modes become twelve

Glarean promotes the old cofinals a and c to finals of their own modes, and Zarlino later renumbers the whole set. Switch between the two schemes to see which numbers and names a given final carries.

Four of the twelve are new, marked in the accent. They are not new pitches: a and c were the cofinals of the protus and the tritus, the notes a fifth above D and F, where a chant went when the gamut could not supply what it needed. Glarean makes them finals in their own right. The tetrardus has no cofinal, so G contributes nothing new here.

Zarlino's renumbering orders the finals from C to a with no gap, the discarded diapason on B aside, so the former Ionian becomes Mode 1. He also realigns the Greek names against that order of finals, equating new Mode 1 on C with Dorian and Mode 3 on D with Phrygian. Judd gives those two equations and continues with “and so forth,” which is how the rest of the column below is filled in.

One motet shows what this costs. Zarlino composed Ego veni as a representation of Mode 5 for a putative eight-mode cycle. It was printed as Ionian in 1549, cited as Mode 11 in the 1558 Istitutioni, and became Mode 1 in the 1573 revision. Four labels in twenty-four years, for one piece.

Twelve never replaces eight. Dressler and Aiguino keep eight in 1561 and 1562, Aiguino refutes twelve- and fourteen-mode systems in 1581, Cerreto refutes twelve in 1601 and 1626, and Palestrina and Lasso write eight-mode cycles through the second half of the century.
