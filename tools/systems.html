4. The three-hexachord system

The sixteenth-century gamut system as Burnett and Nitzberg set it out: a signature names a system, the system carries three hexachords a fifth apart, and the central hexachord reordered as fifths gives the roots the music can use. One pitch class is missing, and its spelling decides which way the system moves.

reading the board

The signature identifies the system: a natural signature a naturalis system, a one-flat signature a mollis system, a two-flat signature a 2♭ system, and so on. The central hexachord governs the system, the hexachord a fifth above it is the dominant hexachord and the one a fifth below the subdominant hexachord. The number of flats a piece may add is contingent on the number in the subdominant hexachord.

Reordering the central hexachord as fifths gives the pitch classes that can carry triads and the cadential points available within a single composition, each able to support a major or a minor third. In modality those fifths relate to each other rather than to a background final.

The system is an eleven-pitch-class field: the chromatic aggregate minus one pitch, a minor third or augmented second above the root of the central hexachord. Once a piece is under way, that missing pitch is what moves the system. Spelled as the minor third above the root it transposes the system down a fifth; spelled as the augmented second it transposes the system up a fifth. A piece may stay in one system throughout, unfold fewer than eleven pitch classes, or modulate once or more.

on the unit's repertoire

Rore's “Da le belle contrade d'oriente” (1566) is set in an F mode in cantus mollis, a 1♭ system. Its outer sections are diatonic and stay in that system, unfolding eleven pitch classes that omit G♯ and A♭; the chromatic middle section shifts systems repeatedly. Absalon, fili mi runs 2♭ to 3♭ to 4♭. The Prophetiae Sibyllarum prologue shifts durus to mollis.
