1. The four categories of mode and the species of the fourth and fifth

Eleventh-century modal theory in the South German writers, in Berno of Reichenau's Prologus in tonarium. Four categories of mode, defined by final and built from one species of fifth and one species of fourth, each dividing into an authentic and a plagal ambitus.

what a species is

A species is one ordering of the tones and semitones between the consecutive notes of a fixed consonance. In the diatonic scale those inter-step intervals are whole tones and semitones, and the number of distinct species of a consonance is one fewer than the number of notes it spans: three species of fourth, four species of fifth, seven species of octave. Fourth, fifth, and octave here always mean the perfect forms.

The doctrine reaches the Middle Ages from Greek harmonics through Boethius (c. 480 to 523/26), De institutione musica iv.14–17, where the octave species are the ones tied to the modes. The species of the fourth and fifth acquire that function in the eleventh century, in the treatises of the South German school. The account below is the one in Berno of Reichenau's Prologus in tonarium, written after 1021, in the chapters following the fifth and in the passages interpolated into the treatise. The attribution and dating of those interpolations are disputed.

A species recurs wherever the scale presents the same sequence of tones and semitones. The first species of fourth, T–S–T, is A–B–C–D and equally d–e–f–g. The segments in the tables below are the positions Berno's continuous ascending series assigns, running the fourths and then the fifths upward from the bottom of the scale.

the four categories

Four finals, D, E, F, and G, give four categories of mode, named with the Byzantine ordinals protus, deuterus, tritus, and tetrardus. A category is defined by its pair of species: which species of fifth and which species of fourth the mode is built from.

Each category divides into two ambitus. An authentic mode has its fourth above its fifth, and its octave runs upward from the final. A plagal mode has the fourth below the fifth, and its final stands a fourth above the bottom of the octave. Authentic and plagal partners share the same two species and differ in their order alone. Counting the two ambitus of each category separately gives the eight numbered modes; the species belong to the category, and the position of the fourth against the fifth gives the ambitus.

The modal octaves are theoretical constructs, and the ranges the chant treatises assign run wider. An authentic melody descends a step below its final and reaches as high as nine or ten steps above it. A plagal melody descends a fifth below the final, a fourth for the tritus, and ascends a sixth or a seventh above.

Cohen calls the four categories maneriae. The term entered modal theory with this meaning in the twelfth century, in the Cistercian treatises, later than the theory set out here.

the tetrardus and the octave D–d

The numbering lines up in the first three categories. Protus takes the first species of fifth with the first species of fourth, deuterus the second with the second, tritus the third with the third. The tetrardus breaks the pattern, since a fourth species of fourth does not exist. It takes the fourth species of fifth with the first species of fourth, at D–G in the plagal and d–g in the authentic. The fourth category is the exception in the other eleventh-century doctrine as well: G has no affinity, and so no cofinal.

Modes 1 and 8 both occupy the octave D–d, the fourth species of octave, and the species divide it two ways. In mode 1 the final is D, with the first species of fifth below the first species of fourth. In mode 8 the final is G, with the first species of fourth below the fourth species of fifth. The division, rather than the outer boundaries, tells the two apart.

what happens to the species afterward

The species of the consonances spread through Italy in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and were a common component of modal theory across Western Europe by the sixteenth. With Marchetto of Padua, whose Lucidarium is of 1317/18, the species are governed by the final, and they are named by solmization: the first species of fifth spells re–mi–fa–sol–la and is written in shorthand by its boundary syllables as re–la. Modal and hexachordal relationships beyond the final and the ambitus become associated with modal theory at this point.

In the seventeenth century the species become a rule for imitative writing. Girolamo Diruta in the second part of Il Transilvano (1609), Marco Scacchi in the 1640s, and Angelo Berardi in the Miscellanea musicale (1689) require the subject of a modally correct fugue in an authentic mode to present the proper fifth and the answer to present the proper fourth, reversed in a plagal mode. Diruta gives the species of mode 1 as re–la and re–sol, between D la sol re, A la mi re, and D la sol re. Exact imitation was held to contradict modal integrity, and the tonal answer was argued in these terms.
