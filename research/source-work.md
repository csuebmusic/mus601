# source work

Every analytical paper in this seminar carries a source requirement, and it is the same size every time: the authoritative edition of each work you analyze, plus two secondary sources, each of the two annotated. The quantity stays small on purpose. Getting an edition citation and two solid sources exactly right, five times over, builds a skill that a long list you cannot evaluate does not.

What changes across the term is everything except the quantity: how much of the finding is done for you, which research tool comes into play, which citation form you have to handle, and which test of trustworthiness is the explicit lens for that paper. By the final paper you will have exercised each of these independently.

## the constant requirement

For each paper, cite the authoritative edition of every work you analyze. On the comparative papers that means three editions for Paper 1 and two for Papers 2 and 3; on Papers 4 and 5 it means one. Which edition you cite is a scholarly decision, not a matter of what happened to be free online. An IMSLP scan is often a public-domain reprint or a performance edition rather than the modern critical edition a scholar would cite, and the difference matters when your argument turns on an accidental, a slur, or a dynamic.

Alongside the editions, find two secondary sources that bear on your analysis, and annotate both. Secondary sources are scholarly books, journal articles, dissertations, and reference entries. Program notes, blog posts, and textbook summaries do not count toward the two.

## across the five papers

**Paper 1, Lasso, Monteverdi, and Schütz.** Your first RILM search, done with guidance. One of your two secondary sources may come from the Unit 1 bibliography, Powers or the relevant Christensen chapter among them; you still cite and annotate it correctly, you simply are not finding it blind. The second you find yourself in RILM. Citation forms in play: the score or critical edition, and the chapter in an edited volume. The lens is edition authority and publisher venue: why this edition rather than another, and what a university press anthology gives you that a concert program does not. Annotations use the template below.

**Paper 2, Vivaldi and Bach.** Both secondary sources are yours to find, one of them through JSTOR, and WorldCat locates the specific edition volume you need. The assigned Dreyfus article can anchor one of the two; the second must be found. New citation form: the journal article. The lens is peer review and currency, and the pair of concertos makes the edition question live in a useful way, since Bach's editorial tradition is far more standardized than Vivaldi's, so the two edition citations are not equally easy to defend. Annotations are free-form now, in the same three parts. A short source-finding log is due ahead of the paper: what you searched, what you rejected, and why.

**Paper 3, Mozart and Haydn.** Grove and Oxford Music Online enter here, along with footnote-chasing: following a citation out of an assigned reading, Hepokoski being the obvious place to start, rather than beginning a fresh database search. At least one of your two secondary sources should come to you that way. New citation form: the online reference-work entry. The lens is author standing, why a claim from a named scholar in a defined analytical tradition carries the weight it does, alongside the editorial vetting behind a Grove entry. Before you submit, trade source picks with a classmate.

**Paper 4, Beethoven.** ProQuest Dissertations enters, and if your analysis engages a specific recorded performance, so does the question of a recording's authority. The edition citation here is defended rather than stated: Beethoven has genuinely competing editions, Henle Urtext against the older complete-works editions, so give a short paragraph on why you are working from the one you chose, and on why this recording if you use one. At least one of your two secondary sources must be found independently through ProQuest or JSTOR. New citation forms: the dissertation and, where relevant, liner notes. The lens is edition and recording authority in full, together with the difference between committee-vetted and peer-reviewed standards.

**Paper 5, Schubert.** Nothing new is introduced. The edition, two secondary sources you locate and evaluate on your own, both fully annotated, with no template, no pre-identified reading, and no peer check behind you. Use whichever citation forms your sources actually require; recognizing which form applies is part of what is being asked. Apply all five tests of trustworthiness without being prompted. If you take the two Webster articles as your pair, notice that they can be read against each other rather than one at a time. That is the move 602 will ask for at length.

## judging a source

Five questions, applied to everything you cite.

- Peer review. Was the work vetted by other scholars before publication, and by what standard? A dissertation is committee-vetted, which is a different thing from peer-reviewed, and worth naming as such.
- Author standing. Who wrote it, what else have they written, and where does their work sit in the literature on this repertoire?
- Venue. A university press, a refereed journal, a major reference work, and a concert program carry different weight, and the difference is not snobbery: it reflects who checked the claims.
- Currency. How old is it, and does age matter here? A 1985 article can still be the authority on its subject. Decide which case you are in, and be prepared to argue it.
- Edition and recording authority. For scores and recordings, what makes this text or this performance the one to cite?

## the annotated entry

An annotation runs a short paragraph, roughly 100 to 150 words, and does three things: summarize the source, judge it, and say what it is good for in your paper. It is not a blurb and not a book report. Head each entry with the full Chicago bibliography citation, then the paragraph.

For Paper 1, use this structure directly:

> **Summary.** In one or two sentences, what does this source argue or establish? Name the claim, not the topic.
>
> **Judgment.** Which of the five tests apply here, and how does the source come out on them? Where is it strong, and where would you hold it at arm's length?
>
> **Use.** What does this source do for your analysis specifically? Point at the passage, the movement, or the analytical problem it bears on.

From Paper 2 onward, write the paragraph as continuous prose rather than under the three labels. The three jobs stay the same.

## submitting the source work

Annotations go in a separate document submitted with the paper, so they sit outside the paper's six-to-eight-page count and take no space from the analysis. The edition and secondary sources themselves appear in the paper's footnotes and bibliography as usual, in Chicago notes-bibliography form. Paper 4's defense of the edition belongs in the paper itself, as a short paragraph, and counts toward the page limit.

The source work is part of the paper's score rather than a separate grade. A paper with a strong analysis resting on an edition that cannot be defended, or on two sources that will not bear the weight put on them, is not finished work.
