Source work

The source requirement is the same on every analytical paper: an authoritative edition of each work you analyze, plus two secondary sources.

Editions are cited inside the paper, in its footnotes and bibliography, and are never annotated. The two secondary sources are cited there too, and each also gets an annotation, in a separate document handed in with the paper.

what you do for every paper

  1. Find an authoritative edition of each work you analyze.
  2. Find two secondary sources that bear on your analysis, using the research tools, and judge each one.
  3. Write an annotation of each of the two.
  4. Cite the editions and both sources in Chicago, notes-bibliography.

The syllabus gives the due date for each paper and the works it takes.

primary and secondary sources

A primary source is the music itself or a document from its own moment: the score, the manuscript or first printing behind that score, a letter or a treatise written in the composer's lifetime, a recording of a performance.

A secondary source is scholarship about the music, written later by someone studying it: a book, a journal article, a dissertation, a signed entry in a reference work.

Zarlino's Le istitutioni harmoniche (1558) is a primary source when you are asking how sixteenth-century theorists described mode, and a modern article about Zarlino is a secondary source. A critical edition is both at once: its score is a primary source, and its editorial preface is scholarship.

an authoritative edition

One edition of each work you analyze, and which one is your choice: three editions for Paper 1, two for Papers 2 and 3, one for Papers 4 and 5. An authoritative edition is prepared from the evidence for the piece, with a named editor who says what that evidence was and what was changed.

  • Critical edition. Works back to the surviving sources: the composer's autograph, the first printing, manuscript copies in circulation. Names its editor, states which source it takes as its base, and reports where it emends and where the sources disagree. The modern complete-works editions are of this kind, among them the Neue Mozart-Ausgabe and the Neue Bach-Ausgabe from Bärenreiter.
  • Urtext edition. Aims at the same text as a critical edition, with a lighter apparatus. Henle's is the one you will meet most often.
  • Nineteenth-century collected edition. The Bach-Gesellschaft Ausgabe, Breitkopf und Härtel's Beethovens Werke, and their kind. Scholarly work of their own moment, and later editions have often superseded them.
  • Performing edition. Serves a player. Adds fingerings, bowings, and dynamics, and often prints editorial slurs and accidentals as though the composer had written them.

Cite a critical or Urtext edition where one exists. A collected edition is citable if you can say what it is and why you are working from it. A performing edition is not a citable text.

finding an edition on IMSLP

IMSLP holds scores whose copyright has lapsed, which in practice means older editions. A given file may be a nineteenth-century collected edition, a performing edition carrying a later editor's additions, or a scan with nothing on it to say what it is. These differ from a modern critical edition in small things: whether an accidental is the composer's or an editor's, where a slur begins and ends, whether a dynamic stood in the source.

Before citing a file, find its publication information: the publisher, the city, the date, and the editor where one is named. It sits on the title page, and often again in small type at the foot of the first page of music. Cite the file as whatever it turns out to be. A file that gives none of this cannot be cited as an edition.

the two secondary sources

Two pieces of scholarship that bear on your analysis, on every paper, both annotated. Scholarly books, articles in refereed journals, doctoral dissertations, and signed reference entries count toward the two. Program notes, blog posts, textbook summaries, record-label copy, and the output of an AI tool do not.

Bearing on your analysis means the source touches the music, the composer, the genre, or the analytical problem you have taken up.

judging a source

Apply these to everything you cite.

  • Peer review. Was the work vetted by other scholars before publication, and by what standard? A dissertation is read and approved by a committee, a different standard from peer review, and worth naming as such.
  • Author standing. Who wrote it, what else have they written, and where does their work sit in the literature on this repertoire?
  • Venue. A university press, a refereed journal, a major reference work, and a concert program carry different weight, set by who checked the claims.
  • Currency. How old is it, and does age matter here? A 1985 article can still be the authority on its subject. Decide which case you are in, and be prepared to argue it.
  • Edition and recording authority. For scores and recordings, what makes this text or this performance the one to cite?

the research tools

Two tabs in our Canvas site open into the libraries. Library Guide lists the music databases, and a second tab opens the University Libraries. The Music Department's own collection is catalogued as the Music Resource Library.

RILMThe field's index to writing about music: books, articles, dissertations, and essays, with an abstract and subject headings for each. Where you establish that something has been written on your topic, and what.
JSTORA full-text archive of scholarly journals. Where you read an article once you know it exists, and where you find its stable link for the bibliography.
WorldCatA combined catalog of library holdings. Where you confirm an edition's editor, series, publisher, and date, and find which library holds a copy.
Grove Music OnlineSigned reference articles on composers, works, genres, and terms, each with a work-list and a bibliography. Part of Oxford Music Online. Citable in its own right.
ProQuestDoctoral dissertations and theses, many of them in full text.
IMSLPA library of public-domain scores. What it holds ranges from critical editions out of copyright to unidentifiable scans.
footnote-chasingNot a database. Reading the notes and bibliography of a source you trust, and following them to the scholarship it was built on.

the annotated entry

An annotation runs 100 to 150 words and does three things: summarize the source, judge it, and say what it is good for in your paper. Head each entry with the full bibliography citation, in the form set out under the citation forms, then the paragraph.

For Paper 1, use this structure directly:

Summary. In one or two sentences, what does this source argue or establish? Name the claim, not the topic.

Judgment. Which of the five tests apply here, and how does the source come out on them? Where is it strong, and where would you hold it at arm's length?

Use. What does this source do for your analysis specifically? Point at the passage, the movement, or the analytical problem it bears on.

From Paper 2 onward, write the paragraph as continuous prose rather than under the three labels. The three jobs stay the same.

Chicago, notes-bibliography

Music scholarship cites in the notes-bibliography system of the Chicago Manual of Style, the humanities system: a numbered footnote at each point of citation, plus a bibliography at the end. Chicago's other system, author-date, uses parenthetical citations in the text and is standard in the sciences and social sciences. Our papers do not use it.

A note points at the specific page, measure, or claim you are drawing on at that moment. A bibliography entry identifies the source as a whole.

note

1. Harold S. Powers, “Tonal Types and Modal Categories in Renaissance Polyphony,” Journal of the American Musicological Society 34, no. 3 (Autumn 1981): 439.

The author's name runs in normal order, elements are separated by commas, publication data sits in parentheses, and the entry ends at the specific page.

bibliography

Powers, Harold S. “Tonal Types and Modal Categories in Renaissance Polyphony.” Journal of the American Musicological Society 34, no. 3 (Autumn 1981): 428–70.

The author's name inverts, elements are separated by periods, nothing is parenthesized, and the entry ends with the full page range.

Give the full note the first time you cite a source. After that, use the shortened form: last name, short title, page.

5. Powers, “Tonal Types,” 441.

Chicago no longer recommends ibid. Use the shortened form every time, even for consecutive citations of the same source.

the citation forms

score or critical edition

Cite the edition you actually worked from, and cite it as an edition, with its editor and series.

note

1. Claudio Monteverdi, Tutte le opere di Claudio Monteverdi, ed. Gian Francesco Malipiero (Asolo: G. F. Malipiero, 1926–42), 8:112.

bibliography

Monteverdi, Claudio. Tutte le opere di Claudio Monteverdi. Edited by Gian Francesco Malipiero. Asolo: G. F. Malipiero, 1926–42.

The general pattern, for an edition not part of a collected series:

Composer Last, First. Title of Work. Edited by [editor]. [Series and volume, if any]. Place: Publisher, year.

Where a work sits inside a collected or critical edition, give the series title and the volume.

chapter in an edited volume

note

1. Cristle Collins Judd, “Renaissance Modal Theory: Theoretical, Compositional, and Editorial Perspectives,” in The Cambridge History of Western Music Theory, ed. Thomas Christensen (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002), 371.

6. Judd, “Renaissance Modal Theory,” 380.

bibliography

Judd, Cristle Collins. “Renaissance Modal Theory: Theoretical, Compositional, and Editorial Perspectives.” In The Cambridge History of Western Music Theory, edited by Thomas Christensen, 364–406. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002.

The note abbreviates “edited by” to “ed.”; the bibliography spells it out. The bibliography gives the chapter's full page range; the note gives the page you are citing.

book

note

1. Henry Burnett and Roy Nitzberg, Composition, Chromaticism and the Developmental Process: A New Theory of Tonality (Aldershot: Ashgate, 2007), 88.

bibliography

Burnett, Henry, and Roy Nitzberg. Composition, Chromaticism and the Developmental Process: A New Theory of Tonality. Aldershot: Ashgate, 2007.

journal article

note

1. Laurence Dreyfus, “J. S. Bach's Concerto Ritornellos and the Question of Invention,” Musical Quarterly 71, no. 3 (1985): 331.

bibliography

Dreyfus, Laurence. “J. S. Bach's Concerto Ritornellos and the Question of Invention.” Musical Quarterly 71, no. 3 (1985): 327–58.

Volume, then issue as “no.”, then year in parentheses, then pages after a colon. If you read the article through JSTOR, add the DOI or the stable URL at the end of the bibliography entry. A database name on its own is not a locator.

online reference work, including Grove

note

1. [Author First Last], “[Entry Title],” Grove Music Online, [year], [permalink].

bibliography

[Author Last, First]. “[Entry Title].” Grove Music Online. Oxford University Press, [year]. [permalink].

Grove entries are signed. Name the author rather than treating the entry as anonymous, and copy the permalink Grove supplies rather than the URL in your browser bar, which usually encodes your session. Where an entry has been revised, use the revision date Grove gives.

dissertation

note

1. [First Last], “[Title of Dissertation]” (PhD diss., [University], [year]), [page].

bibliography

[Last, First]. “[Title of Dissertation].” PhD diss., [University], [year]. ProQuest ([order or publication number]).

The dissertation title takes quotation marks rather than italics, and the degree and institution stand where publication data would.

liner notes and recordings

note

1. [First Last], liner notes to [Composer], [Title], [performers], [label] [catalog number], [year], compact disc.

bibliography

[Last, First]. Liner notes to [Composer], [Title]. [Performers]. [Label] [catalog number], [year], compact disc.

the recording itself

[Composer Last, First]. [Work, with catalog number]. [Performers]. [Label] [catalog number], [year], compact disc.

Give the medium at the end (compact disc, LP, or streaming service with the URL). Where the recording matters to your argument, name the performers and the release.

citing the music itself

Measure numbers go in your text, not in a footnote: the cadence at m. 47 or mm. 112–19. Abbreviate one measure as m. and more than one as mm. A footnote is for the edition you are reading, cited once in full early in the paper, and for anything a reader would need to check you: a rehearsal figure, a differing reading in another edition, an editorial accidental.

Your own musical examples are numbered figures with captions, and the caption credits the edition the example is drawn from.

what goes wrong most often

Page ranges are abbreviated in Chicago's number style: 428–70, not 428–470, though ranges crossing a hundred keep both digits, as in 364–406. Use an en dash in ranges rather than a hyphen. Journal titles are italicized; article and chapter titles take quotation marks. The bibliography is alphabetized by author surname and is not numbered. Every source cited in a note appears in the bibliography, and nothing appears in the bibliography that the paper does not use.

Generative AI tools misformat these patterns and invent sources outright. Check every citation against the source in your hands and against Chicago, notes-bibliography. For anything this page does not cover, see the Chicago Manual of Style, 18th edition, chapters 13 and 14.
