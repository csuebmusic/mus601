the research tools

Alexander Street is in use on every paper. The rest arrive one at a time and accumulate, so by Paper 4 all of them are yours to choose among. ProQuest, WorldCat, and IMSLP are open to anyone; the others go through the library proxy and ask for your NetID and password.

Alexander Street: Music and Performing Arts

search-alexanderstreet-com.proxylib.csueastbay.edu

A collection of digitized scores, streaming audio, and video. This is where you find an authoritative edition of each work you analyze.

Search the composer and the work in the advanced search, then filter by content type to separate scores from audio. A digitized score is a scan of some particular edition, so find the title page and the publication information, work out which edition the scan reproduces, and cite it as that.

RILM Abstracts of Music Literature

proxylib.csueastbay.edu, through EBSCOhost

The international bibliography of writing about music, from 1967 forward with earlier coverage in places. It indexes journal articles, books, chapters in edited volumes, dissertations, conference papers, Festschriften, critical commentaries to complete editions, and reviews, in some 140 languages and from 178 countries. Every record carries a bibliographic citation, an abstract in English whatever the language of the source, and controlled subject headings. RILM indexes the scholarship and holds the full text of only a small part of it, so a record tells you an article exists and you go elsewhere to read it.

Start from the composer and the work, in that order, before adding the analytical problem. Read the abstract rather than the title, since titles in musicology are often oblique. Take the subject headings from a record that looks right and search those directly, which surfaces the neighboring scholarship a keyword search misses. Narrow by document type when you want a particular kind of source, and by date when currency is the question.

JSTOR

login.proxylib.csueastbay.edu

A full-text archive of scholarly journals, with deep back runs of the core musicology titles: the Journal of the American Musicological Society, Music Theory Spectrum, 19th-Century Music, the Journal of Music Theory, the Musical Quarterly. Coverage for many titles runs to a moving wall a few years short of the present.

When you already have a citation from RILM, search the article title rather than keywords. The stable URL and the DOI on the article's landing page are what a Chicago bibliography entry takes; a database name is not a locator. The reference list at the end of a JSTOR article is a fast way into the scholarship that article was built on.

Oxford Music Online

login.proxylib.csueastbay.edu

One platform holding three separate reference works. Grove Music Online is the principal scholarly music reference source in English: signed articles by specialists, each with a work-list and a bibliography, citable in their own right. The Oxford Dictionary of Music and The Oxford Companion to Music are written for a general readership. They are useful for orientation, and they do not count as scholarly sources for a paper.

Search inside Grove specifically rather than across the platform, so the general-readership entries stay out of your results. Read the article, then work from its bibliography, which is a reading list assembled by a specialist. The work-list is where you confirm catalog numbers and dates. Grove entries are signed and dated, so take the author's name and the revision date, and copy the permalink the entry supplies rather than the URL in your browser bar.

ProQuest Dissertations & Theses

proquest.com, no sign-in

The dissertation and thesis database. Without a subscription your results are the open-access dissertations and theses, which is a large share of them and not all, so a search that returns nothing is not evidence that nothing was written. A dissertation is often the only extended study of a narrow subject, and its bibliography is built by someone who read everything on that subject. It is vetted by a committee, which is a real standard and a different one from peer review.

Search the composer and the work, then broaden to the genre or the analytical problem, since a chapter on your piece may sit inside a study with an unrelated title. Read the abstract and the table of contents before downloading. Note the degree, the institution, the year, and the ProQuest number, which the citation takes.

alongside: WorldCat and IMSLP

Neither is part of the sequence. Both are there when you need to locate a book or settle a citation.

WorldCat is the combined catalog of library holdings. Use it to find which library holds a book, which is where a CSU+ or interlibrary loan request starts, and to confirm the details a citation needs and a bookseller listing leaves out: editor, series, volume, publisher, date.

IMSLP holds scores whose copyright has lapsed. A file there may be a nineteenth-century collected edition, a performing edition carrying a later editor's additions, or a scan with nothing on it to say what it is, so it takes the same identification a score from Alexander Street takes.

the scores

Analyze from the score packet handed out in seminar, and take your musical examples from it. Separately, find an authoritative edition of every work the paper analyzes on Alexander Street, and cite that edition in the paper.

An authoritative edition is prepared from the evidence for the piece, with a named editor who says what that evidence was and what was changed. A critical edition works back to the surviving sources, the composer's autograph, the first printing, manuscript copies in circulation, and reports where it emends and where the sources disagree. An Urtext edition aims at the same text with a lighter apparatus. A performing edition serves a player: it adds fingerings, bowings, and dynamics, and often prints editorial slurs and accidentals as though the composer had written them.

paper 1: RILM to JSTOR

A double step. Search RILM, read the abstract, and judge from it whether the article is worth having. Then find that article in JSTOR and read it. RILM establishes what exists; JSTOR supplies the text.

paper 2: Oxford Music Online

Search inside Grove, read the entry on each concerto or its composer, and work out from the entry's bibliography to a secondary source on each of the two works.

paper 3: ProQuest

Mozart and Haydn symphonies are heavily worked ground. Search the work first, then the genre and the analytical problem, since a chapter on your symphony may sit inside a dissertation on something broader.

papers 4 and 5: your choice

No prescribed tool, and every tool above is available. The count rises to two secondary sources on a single work, which is the first time you have to find two pieces of scholarship that speak to each other rather than one per piece.

using a source in your paper

A source earns its place by doing something in the argument. A source you cite once for a date is doing less than the requirement asks of it.

Three things a secondary source does in a paper of this kind. It supplies an analytical frame you adopt or resist, as Hepokoski's medial caesura does for a sonata exposition. It establishes a fact your analysis rests on: an attribution, a dating, a reading in a source. It states a position you argue with, which is the strongest of the three.

Judge everything you cite on five questions. Was it peer reviewed, and by what standard? Who wrote it, and where does their work sit in the literature on this repertoire? What is the venue, since a university press, a refereed journal, a major reference work, and a concert program carry different weight? How old is it, and does age matter here, given that a 1985 article can still be the authority on its subject? And for a score, what makes this edition the one to cite? Where a source is weak on one of these and you use it anyway, say so in the note.

citing

Chicago, notes-bibliography, 18th edition: a numbered footnote wherever you draw on a source, and a bibliography at the end listing everything the paper cites. Chicago's other system, author-date, uses parenthetical citations in the text and is standard in the sciences and social sciences. Our papers do not use it. Anything not covered below is in The Chicago Manual of Style, chapters 13 and 14.

what the 18th edition changed

Guides written for the 17th edition are still all over the web, and they produce citations that look correct and are not. The changes that touch this course:

  • Place of publication is dropped from book and score citations (14.30). Ashgate, 2007, rather than Aldershot: Ashgate, 2007.
  • Month and season are dropped from journal article citations (13.26). 34, no. 3 (1981), rather than 34, no. 3 (Autumn 1981).
  • The page range of a chapter in an edited volume is no longer required in the bibliography entry (14.8). The note still gives the page you cite.
  • Shortened citations replace ibid., including for consecutive references to the same source (13.37).
  • Up to six authors are named in a bibliography entry, up to two in a note, then et al. (13.23, 13.107).
  • Three em dashes no longer stand in for a repeated author name in the bibliography (13.72, 13.113).
  • AI-generated content has its own guidance (14.112).

the two forms

A note points at the page, measure, or claim you have in hand at that moment. A bibliography entry identifies the source as a whole. The same source takes both, and they differ in name order and punctuation.

In a note the author's name runs in normal order, elements are separated by commas, publication data sits in parentheses, and the entry ends at the page you cite.

note

1. Harold S. Powers, “Tonal Types and Modal Categories in Renaissance Polyphony,” Journal of the American Musicological Society 34, no. 3 (1981): 439.

In a bibliography entry the name inverts, elements are separated by periods, nothing is parenthesized, and the entry ends with the full page range.

bibliography

Powers, Harold S. “Tonal Types and Modal Categories in Renaissance Polyphony.” Journal of the American Musicological Society 34, no. 3 (1981): 428–70.

Give the full note the first time you cite a source. After that use the shortened form: last name, short title, page.

5. Powers, “Tonal Types,” 441.

digitized score from IMSLP

IMSLP is open, so the URL goes in. Identify the edition first. A file that gives no publication information anywhere cannot be cited as an edition. Where a scan gives no date, n.d. stands in its place.

the music itself

Measure numbers go in your text rather than in a note: the cadence at m. 47, or mm. 112–19. One measure is m., more than one is mm. A note carries the edition, cited in full once early in the paper, and anything a reader needs in order to check your claim: a rehearsal figure, a differing reading in another edition, an editorial accidental.

Your musical examples are numbered figures with captions, and the caption credits the edition the example comes from.

what goes wrong most often

Page ranges abbreviate in Chicago's number style: 428–70, not 428–470, though a range crossing a hundred keeps both digits, as in 364–406. Ranges take an en dash rather than a hyphen. Journal and book titles are italicized; article and chapter titles take quotation marks. The bibliography is alphabetized by author surname and is not numbered. Every source cited in a note appears in the bibliography, and nothing appears there that the paper does not use.

Generative AI tools misformat these patterns and invent sources outright. Check every citation against the source in your hands.
