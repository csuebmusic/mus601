## cohen, "notes, scales, and modes in the earlier middle ages" (pp. 307–63) · c. 800 to c. 1500

The prehistory of every term the unit uses. Three sections: the ninth century, the eleventh-century syntheses, and a postscript to c. 1500.

**What the chapter is for**

The ninth century established a scale system and a systematic modal theory based on it, which is where two features of theoretical thought that still shape the culture originate: an abstract background scale system, and the intervallic analysis of pitch structures.

**The three elements the Carolingians integrated**

- The still evolving repertory of Gregorian chant melodies, the concrete actuality of liturgical song (*cantus*), which is the constant touchstone and ultimate object of all theory construction.
- The system of eight *toni* used by the church to classify and organize those melodies, whose organization, nomenclature, and procedures bear the marks of its origin in Byzantine liturgical practices.
- Concepts, constructions, and procedures of analysis adapted from ancient Greek harmonics, the scientific study of the pitch components of music: pitch itself, notes, intervals, scales, modes. Transmitted to the Carolingians by late Roman and earlier medieval writers, and called in Latin the science or art of music, *ars musica*.

The first two together are the cantus tradition; the third is the harmonics tradition. The Carolingian cantors and scholars took integrating all three as their task, using each to illuminate the others.

**The motive and the result**

- The practical motivation was the awareness that a more systematic and rationalized understanding of the cantus tradition would promote liturgical uniformity, always an ideal of the Carolingian monarchs and higher clergy, by securing more accurate transmission of the sacred melodies and more disciplined, uniform performance of them.
- What the integration produced was a new way of understanding the concrete actualities of liturgical song, conceptualizing entities and events that had formerly been grasped only or primarily empirically, in ways that were truly theoretical in involving abstraction and system construction.
- The named transmitters of the harmonics tradition, where Cohen names them, are Boethius and Martianus Capella, alongside other late Roman writers on the ancient *ars musica*. Boethius, Cassiodorus, and Isidore are the authorities from whom Aurelian's opening chapters draw their extracts.

**What the eight *toni* were for**

- They functioned primarily as a classificatory system, securing a smooth and euphonious melodic connection between the end of the psalm tone and the return of the antiphon, by supplying categories against which any antiphon could be matched to its most appropriate psalm tone. The standard system gave one psalm tone per category.
- A second level of classification came from the alternative cadence formulas or terminations, the *differentiae*, which accommodate the various ways antiphons of a given mode may begin.

**What the earliest documents do and do not contain**

- Earliest tonaries: the St.-Riquier Tonary, c. 800, and the Carolingian Tonary of Metz, archetype c. 825–55. Modal treatises often travel with a tonary and probably began as collections of glosses on the modal terminology used in tonaries.
- *De octo tonis* circulates in several versions, both as an independent treatise attributed to Alcuin and as the first part of chapter 8 of Aurelian of Réôme's *Musica disciplina*. Its original version may date back as far as the late eighth century.
- The Carolingian *toni* were adapted from the *oktoechos*, used by the Byzantine clergy since at least the seventh century to classify their liturgical melodies into eight categories called *echoi*. The Greek terminology in the earliest extant modal documents is part of the evidence.
- Four *maneriae* named by Greek ordinals: protus, deuterus, tritus, tetrardus. Each pairs an authentic with a plagal, from *authentes* or *authentikos* (having authority) and *plagios* (oblique, collateral, hence derived, subordinate), Latinized as *aut(h)ent(ic)us* and *pla(g)i(us)*, later *plagalis*, or translated rather than transliterated as *auctoralis* and *lateralis*. The pair is described as master and pupil, *magister* and *discipulus*.
- No final. No criterion distinguishing the four main categories from each other. Authentic and plagal are separated only by vague range language, *altiores* against *inferiores*, with the plagal range treated as the lower part of the authentic and contained within it.
- Intonation formulas (*echemata* in later Byzantine terminology) carry the modal identity instead: *Nonanoeane*, *Noeagis*, and from about the tenth century Latin texts such as "Primus tonus sic incipit" and model antiphons such as "Primum quaerite regnum Dei."

**Why *tonus* is unusable as a technical term**

It carried four additional senses: the whole tone as an interval; a single pitched sound, for which the more usual terms were *sonus*, *sonitus*, *phthongos* (often corrupted to *ptongos*), and *vox*; a grammatical accent, also called *accentus* and *tenor*; and one of Boethius's terms for the transposition keys of Greek theory. *De octo tonis* conflates all of these except the first with each other and with vague hints at mode as a regulatory principle, which is why its definition fails. Later ninth-century treatises introduce *modus* and *tropus* as the proper designations, on Boethius's authority. *Musica enchiriadis* chapter 8 asserts that *tonus* in this sense was used improperly (*abusive*), a dictum many later theorists repeat, and *tonus* stays in use anyway.

**Aurelian as the pre-theoretical state**

*Musica disciplina*, attributed to Aurelian of Réôme and customarily dated to the 840s, though the dating is now in question.

- Chapters 1 to 7 cover the ancient *ars musica* through extracts from Boethius, Cassiodorus, and Isidore. Chapter 2 contains one of the earliest attempts to connect the two traditions, exemplifying the octave, fifth, fourth, and whole tone by citing plainchant, and then, less justifiably, linking those four intervals to the four authentic modes.
- Chapters 10 to 17 describe the melodic junctures between antiphons and psalm tones, called *varietates*, *differentiae*, *divisiones*, and *definitiones*, in a metaphorical, non-pitch-specific vocabulary that covers melodic motion and contour as well as tempo and vocal timbre.
- The vocabulary is largely grammatical, drawn from the three verbal accents (acute, grave, circumflex), which are frequent in chapter 19's syllable-by-syllable descriptions of the psalm tones. The accentual terms do not indicate specific melodic events consistently. Nowhere is there an attempt at intervallically determined instruction, much less a translation of melodies into notes.
- The mode of a melody is sought at the juncture between psalm tone and antiphon: either at the melody's beginning, or at the close of the conjoined psalm tone, or both. The later doctrine that the last note determines the mode is absent.
- Modal classification is simply given by tradition, and criteria for it are absent. The junctures differentiate the *varietates* of a *tonus*, not the *toni* themselves, and why they are sub-types rather than distinct *toni* remains unexplained.
- The vertical spatial image of pitch is still inchoate. The ancient technical terms are *oxeia*/*acuta*, sharp or pointed, and *bareia*/*gravis*, heavy, which are also the grammarians' terms for the prosodic accents, and which stayed standard through the Middle Ages and beyond.

**Hucbald, *Musica*, c. 870–900**

- Aurally based and pragmatic: like *Musica enchiriadis*, no interval ratios and no monochord division. The scale and its intervals are taught empirically from chant melodies and intonation formulas, though the concepts themselves are adapted from late Roman writing on the *ars musica*, above all Boethius's *De institutione musica*.
- Nine melodic intervals (*spatia*, *intervalla*, *modi*, *species*), with the semitone and whole tone as their elements and as the spaces separating adjacent scale notes, empirically defined yet understood as precisely determined and invariable in size.
- A cithara string diagram maps a melody with the range of a major sixth onto six lines tuned T–T–S–T–T. Cohen calls the resemblance to the later Guidonian hexachord striking and perhaps not entirely coincidental, citing Crocker's "Hermann's Major Sixth." Only the lines signify notes; the spaces represent intervals, so this is closer to an icon of an instrument than to staff notation.
- The note (*ptongus*, *sonus*) established as the element of melody, with the same status and function that letters have in language, defined after Boethius as an incidence of the voice brought forth at one pitch, suitable for melody. Notes ascend and descend *in modum scalarum*, like a flight of stairs, each set apart by the quantity of its proper interval.
- Adapts the diatonic Greater Perfect System into ascending T–S–T tetrachords from *proslambanomenos*, then adds the *synemmenon* tetrachord from the Lesser Perfect System, giving five tetrachords, a two-octave diatonic scale plus the *trite synemmenon* (b♭).
- Alphabetic notation for the sixteen distinct pitches, mostly Greek capitals sometimes slightly modified, drawn from Boethius's list of Lydian vocal and instrumental signs. The exception is *proslambanomenos*, written with the grammarians' *daseia* or *tau jacens*. He urges that the signs be used alongside neumes, which at this date are adiastematic and give no precise indication of notes and intervals.
- Hucbald and the *Enchiriadis* treatises are the first known sources to define and use the concept of the modal final and to locate the finals as specific notes within a scale. Hucbald names them by their Greek names, equivalent to D, E, F, and G, and they form the second-lowest T–S–T tetrachord of his system, equivalent in all but name to the later "tetrachord of the finals."
- *Socialitas*, his term for what is later called affinity: each final has a union of connection with the note five steps higher, where many melodies are found to end as if by rule and to run their course in the same mode. He finds an equal relationship with the notes four steps below (in one case five), which pertain to beginnings rather than endings.
- Beginnings are restricted in all cases to the eight or nine notes between the fifth step above and the fifth below the final, demonstrated with vertically oriented diagrams, Greek note names, his own notation, and the letters t and s for the tone and semitone positions.

**The *Enchiriadis* treatises**

- *Musica* and *Scolica enchiriadis*, most scholars agreeing on c. 850 to c. 900, others favoring the tenth century. Clearly sister treatises, apparently not by the same author, and *Scolica* is not a commentary on *Musica*. *Scolica* teaches interval ratios and a monochord division; *Musica* does not.
- The scale is a series of T–S–T tetrachords whose notes are named by the four modal ordinals. Four tetrachords plus the first two notes of a fifth are deployed, named for their functional roles: *graves*, *finales*, *superiores*, *excellentes*. Every tetrachord is disjunct from both neighbors, so the system is periodic at the fifth rather than the octave, and augmented octaves occur at three points, between the tritus of any tetrachord and the deuterus eight steps higher.
- The explanation of modality this scale exists to support: there are just four modal qualities, and a melody's quality follows from the pattern of tones and semitone exhibited with respect to its final. Each final has its own pattern by virtue of its position in the tetrachordal matrix, and bestows the resulting quality on any melody ending on it. This is the first theoretical explanation of modality as a quality of melodies.
- Because the pattern recurs every five steps, *Scolica* sometimes analyzes in pentachords running between analogously positioned notes: from any protus to the protus in the tetrachord above is T–S–T–T. The consequence is that there are only four truly different notes, all others being replications at higher and lower pitch, which is the same operation octave periodicity performs for us with pitch classes. The system posits a total identification of tetrachordal position, intervallic pattern, modal quality, and note identity.
- Ambitus is secondary. Both the authentic and plagal ambitus of any mode reach the fifth below the final, which is why the *graves* are needed, and the fifth above, which is why the *superiores* are; the authentic extends to the ninth above, which is why the *excellentes* are.
- *Musica enchiriadis* chapter 12 demonstrates the principle by shifting one melody up through the matrix step by step, so that it turns from protus to deuterus to tritus to tetrardus and back to protus. *Scolica* makes the point with even greater clarity.
- Daseian notation uses variations of four distinct signs. Those for protus, deuterus, and tetrardus all derive from the *daseia* tilted to the right with three different curved lines added at the top; the tritus signs differ from all the rest, which marks the location of the central semitone, and it is semitone placement that determines every note's identity.
- *Scolica* treats semitone alteration as *vitia*, *absonia*, *dissonantia*: singing one note a semitone higher or lower shifts the deuterus–tritus semitone and rewrites the qualities of every other note in the series, including those whose pitch is unchanged. The author is uncomfortable with these faults and admits them reluctantly, presumably to accommodate pitches that occurred in chant but not in the regular scale.
- The octave problem: the system replicates at the fifth, so two fifths span a ninth, while singers routinely doubled at the octave. The compound organum diagrams solve it by writing the same Daseian signs eight lines higher, superimposing an octave-based pitch set on the fifth-based system. *Scolica* explains the cause through its concept of modal intervallic pattern; *Musica* emphasizes the octave's unique character, calling octave-related notes not so much *consonae* as *aequisonae*, since in that *symphonia* the note recurs anew, and invokes the *mutatio mirabilis* wrought by the octave's perfection.
- The recurring series A–G, A–G, A in the octave diagrams of chapters 10 and 11 appears to be a Frankish innovation with no earlier medieval witness, and foreshadows the letter notation of the *Dialogus*.

**Alia musica and the origin of the Greek modal names**

- Three layers on Chailley's analysis, intermingled in the text, generally placed in the later ninth or earlier tenth centuries, by three anonymous authors. Author 1 defines the modes by monochord ratios alone and refers to notes only by numbers or by letters standing for numbers. Author 2 is the principal treatise. Author 3 is the *Nova expositio*.
- Author 2's contribution is the importation of two doctrines of Greek harmonics from Boethius: the species of the octave, and the ethnic names of the *tonoi*.
- Species means a particular ordering of the intervals within a consonance. The distinct species of any consonance are one fewer than the notes it spans: three species of fourth, four of fifth, seven of octave.
- The error is structural. Boethius's *modi*, *toni*, and *tropi* are transposition keys, and his octave species, listed in descending order from the top of the scale, stand in a subtly inverse and in a sense causal relation to them: the descending intervals between the starting notes of the species determine the intervals of upward transposition between the modes. Author 2 begins from the assumption that Boethius's *modi* must be the eight ecclesiastical *toni*, an assumption made almost inevitable by Boethius's own statement that *modus*, *tropus*, and *tonus* name the same thing and by his set being eight in number.
- He then equates species with mode outright, conceives each mode as an octave scale mapped onto the background scale, and reverses the order to run upward from the bottom, while keeping the pairing of ordinal number to ethnic name unchanged. The series therefore inverts symmetrically around the two middle octaves, on E and D, which change places: Boethius's fourth species E–e, associated indirectly with his Dorian, becomes Author 2's fifth and is identified with a mode named Phrygian, and Boethius's fifth species D–d, his Phrygian, becomes Author 2's fourth and is named Dorian. The same inversion occurs for the pairs 3 and 6, 2 and 7, and 1 and 8.
- Boethius had also included a spurious eighth mode, the Hypermixolydian, citing Ptolemy erroneously, whose associated eighth octave species is simply the first an octave lower. Author 2 has no choice but to assign that number, name, and octave species, which in his ascending arrangement is a–aa, the first species an octave higher, to the eighth church mode, the plagal tetrardus, where it does not fit. Modes 1 to 6 come out satisfactorily, with each plagal a fourth below its authentic.
- Author 3 supplies the first stage of the solution and, with it, one of the crucial developments in medieval modal theory: the division of the modal octaves into their constituent fourth and fifth, differently for authentics and plagals. Authentics are bounded below by the finals and divided by the *superiores* into a fifth below a fourth; plagals are bounded above by the note a fifth above the final and divided by the final into a fourth below a fifth.
- Mode 8 is moved to D–d, which puts it a fourth below Mode 7 with its final G at the fourth step. The fourth-species octave is then shared with Mode 1, and the two are distinguished by how they divide it: Mode 8 has the *media chorda* G as the guardian of its quality, Mode 1 has a. Author 3 does not explicitly call G the final, but his rule that each plagal has a fifth above and a fourth below its final implies it. Hermannus Contractus later replaces Hypermixolydian with Hypomixolydian, bringing Mode 8 into line with the other plagals.

**The two eleventh-century schools**

From about the year 1000 the method of intervallic analysis becomes firmly established in two distinct and ultimately compatible doctrines: the affinities and *modi vocum*, from the North Italian school of Pseudo-Odo and Guido, and the modal species of the consonances, from the South German school of Berno and Hermannus Contractus.

**Pseudo-Odo and Guido**

- The *Dialogus de musica*, also known as *Enchiridion musices*, was formerly attributed to Odo of Cluny (d. 942) and was compiled c. 1000 by an anonymous Lombard monk. It is a source for much of Guido, whose four extant texts date c. 1026 to c. 1033.
- Both begin with the monochord, and both turn it from an instrument for demonstrating ratios in the *ars musica* into a didactic tool for laying out the scale and training the ear, the first step in a method for teaching singers to read new melodies at sight from letter notation.
- Pseudo-Odo's scale is Hucbald's GPS-plus-*synemmenon* without tetrachordal subdivision, plus a low G named *gamma* below the official first step A, added to accommodate plagal protus melodies reaching the fifth below the final. He names the notes with the octave-based Latin letters still in use, differentiates octaves by capital, lower-case, and doubled lower-case, and gives the two forms of b: *b quadratum* for *b durum* and *b rotundum* for *b molle*, from which the modern flat, natural, and sharp signs derive.
- Guido keeps all of it and adds ♮♮/bb, cc, and dd above aa; ee is added later to complete a hexachord on g. Neither divides the scale into tetrachords; both stress octave equivalence, and Guido names the registers *graves*, *acutae*, *superacutae*.
- The status of b♭ stayed disputed. It enters the gamut only because the *synemmenon* tetrachord of the Lesser Perfect System could be added to the Greater to form the *systema ametabolon*, whose five tetrachords span eighteen notes but only sixteen distinct pitches. Most theorists treated b♭ as an extraneous addition rather than a full member of the scale, Guido adamantly so. German theorists writing around 1100 were less suspicious and even urged including a low B♭, a *synemmenon grave*.
- Solmization is presented in the *Epistola de ignoto cantu* as a more efficient way to learn to sing an unfamiliar melody at sight and to transcribe by ear, built on associating each note with a phrase of *Ut queant laxis* that begins on it. Guido does not state the resulting six-note segment explicitly.
- The hexachord is in effect a T–S–T tetrachord plus a whole tone at each end. Its size and symmetry make position finding easy, and it is the largest scale segment whose internal series replicates itself at intervals other than the octave, namely the fourth and fifth. Three species or *proprietates*: *durum* on G with hard b as *mi*, *naturale* on C, *molle* on F with soft b as *fa*. At all available positions they give the seven overlapping *deductiones*.
- The *littera* or *clavis* combines with the *vox* or *voces* in a composite name, so the lowest note is gamma-ut, whence gamut, and the middle G is G-sol-re-ut. Each composite name is a *locus*. The note b in the upper two octaves is always b fa b mi, and there the two *voces* denote two distinct pitches, which is why mutation between them is forbidden.
- The Hand, universally attributed to Guido although no extant text of his mentions it. Each *locus* occupies a joint or fingertip of the left hand, and the device became so central to pedagogy that the gamut itself came to be called the Hand.
- Staff notation, explained in the *Prologue to an Antiphoner*: the revolutionary feature is that spaces as well as lines represent notes. Some lines or spaces are labeled at the left margin with the appropriate *littera*, F in red ink and C in yellow, each bordering a semitone. Guido does not specify a number of lines; four became standard by the thirteenth century, and the colors were abandoned during it. The letters used as keys to the staff are the origin of clefs.
- Pseudo-Odo's definition of mode: a rule that distinguishes each melody on the basis of its final. He defines each of the eight modes by final, ambitus, and the full series of tones and semitones within the ambitus, located on the background scale in a diagram giving the *forma* of the mode.
- Ambitus in the standard doctrine as Guido states it: authentic melodies may go one step below the final and up to nine or ten above it; plagal melodies may descend a fifth below the final, a fourth for tritus, and rise a sixth or seventh above. Post-Guidonian theorists reduce these practical ranges to modal octaves, the fifth above the final plus a fourth above it for authentics or below it for plagals.
- Pseudo-Odo also takes up the recurrence of modal quality, which he calls *similitudines vocum*, explaining it by recurrent intervallic patterns and assigning every note of his scale except b♭ to one, two, or three modes. He does not ask whether some larger principle controls the recurrences.

**Guido's *modi vocum* and the affinities**

- *Modi vocum* names the idea that the intervallic configuration in the vicinity of a note makes it a certain kind, which is equivalent to its having a certain quality. The first *modus vocum* is a whole tone below the starting note and T–S–T–T above it, the configuration around A and D. These intervallic modes are not the church modes, though they are clearly related to them.
- Octave equivalence reduces the number of truly distinct notes to seven, and in fact there are only four qualities: A and D share one, B and E another, C and F another, and G stands alone, without affinity to any other note.
- The shared *modi vocum* of those three pairs occupy the two identically structured six-note segments Γ–E and C–a, substantively though not functionally identical to the hard and natural hexachords. Guido then adds three further affinities at the fifth above D, E, and F, namely a, ♮, and c, whose six-note module is G–e.
- G has no such affinity because its *modus vocum* is not replicated at the lower fourth or upper fifth. Guido found G problematic and handles it differently in the *Epistola*, and in *Micrologus* chapter 8 grants it partial affinities with both D and C.
- The point of the upper-fifth affinities is practical: a, ♮, and c were the most commonly used alternative ending notes, the cofinals, later called *affinales* or *confinales*. They were the usual solution to conflicts between the scale and the implicit pitch content of chant melodies that require low B♭, E♭, or F♯, conflicts that arise because those melodies pre-date the modal and scalar systems later imposed on them. Transposition to the fifth above puts the melody where the gamut, with both b♭ and b♮, supplies the pitches.
- Guido mentions further affinities at the fourth above, some requiring b♭, and disapproves, since b♭ creates a confusion and transformation of the established qualities: in g–a–b♭–c the g takes protus quality, a becomes deuterus, c becomes tetrardus, and b♭ itself counts as tritus. Transposition to the upper fourth, called transformation, found some acceptance and stayed much less common than the upper-fifth cofinals as long as the music in question was plainchant. From the thirteenth century it is increasingly accepted, especially by theorists of the later fifteenth and sixteenth centuries discussing mode in polyphony.
- What Guido had found, in Pesce's term, is a modal nucleus: a scale segment smaller than an octave encircling the final, whose intervallic configuration determines the final's quality and is replicated, except for tetrardus, four steps lower and five steps higher.

**The South German school**

- Berno of Reichenau, Hermannus Contractus, Wilhelm of Hirsau, and others. The scale is Hucbald's two-octave scale with b♭, divided into T–S–T tetrachords given the *Enchiriadis* functional names: *graves* A–B–C–D, *finales* D–E–F–G, *superiores* a–♮–c–d, *excellentes* d–e–f–g. Hermannus later adds the low gamma from the Italian school.
- Two chief contributions: the species of the consonances, above all of the fourth and fifth, as an instrument for defining the modes, and Hermannus's doctrine of the *sedes troporum*, a reconceptualization of Guido's *modi vocum* within his own system.
- Species theory arrives in three stages. The second, in Berno's *Prologus* and its interpolations, is the one that fed later theory: the three species of fourth and the four species of fifth listed in a continuous ascending series, so the first species of fourth T–S–T runs A–B–C–D, the second S–T–T runs B–C–D–E, each recurring at every scalar segment with the right intervallic series.
- Protus takes the first species of fifth and fourth, deuterus the second, tritus the third. Tetrardus takes the fourth species of fifth with the first species of fourth, since there is no fourth species of fourth, at the alternative positions D–G and d–g. The fourth *maneria* is once again the exception.
- The governing principle: every plagal has the same species of fifth and fourth as its authentic, and they differ in that the authentic sets its fourth above its fifth while the plagal sets it below. Species determine *maneria*; their relative position reflects ambitus. The resulting modal octaves are idealized constructs, one or two notes narrower at each end than the practical ranges.
- Modes 1 and 8 both run D to d and are distinguished by the division: Mode 1 has the first species of fifth below the first species of fourth with D as final, Mode 8 the first species of fourth below the fourth species of fifth with G as final. This is the species-theory version of Author 3's solution.
- The third stage belongs to Hermannus's *Musica*, c. 1050, and has little influence beyond his followers.

**Postscript, to c. 1500**

- *Musica mensurabilis* separates from *musica plana*, to which it is subaltern. *Musica plana* holds the general principles of all music, mensural polyphony and plainchant alike, including the notes and scale, staff notation, solmization and the Hand, and the modes, all now relegated to elementary instruction.
- From Jerome (Hieronymus) of Moravia (*Tractatus de musica*, c. 1272–1304) the structural identity between the solmization hexachords and the six-note modules of the *modi vocum* leads theorists to incorporate the hexachord syllables into modal theory, as markers of modal quality and affinity that they had not previously carried. For the first three *maneriae*, *re*, *mi*, and *fa* in the natural and hard hexachords indicate both the finals D, E, F and the cofinals a, ♮, c, while *sol* on G in the natural hexachord is the final for tetrardus.
- Species theory spreads, particularly with regard to polyphony, and helps validate the upper-fourth transpositions with b♭, since those preserve the modal species for all four *maneriae* exactly while the upper-fifth cofinals do not. A highly elaborated version, alongside an expanded set of ambitus categories, is in Marchetto's *Lucidarium* (1317/18), and both aspects pass into Italian treatises such as Tinctoris's *Liber de natura et proprietate tonorum* (1476). By the mid-sixteenth century species theory is commonplace across much of the continent. The growing conviction that the mediated octave species are the true determinants of mode produces dissatisfaction with the upper-fifth cofinals and culminates in Glarean's rejection of them and his promotion of c and a to regular finals of modes of their own in the *Dodecachordon* (Basel, 1547).
- Tinctoris's modal treatise is explicitly concerned with applying modal theory to polyphony as well as plainchant, a project with few precedents and enormous consequences in the following century.
- Flat signatures indicating a systematic preference for b♭ split the gamut by about 1500 into *cantus durus*, also *scala ♮-duralis*, comprising the natural and hard hexachords, and *cantus mollis*, also *scala b-mollaris*, comprising the natural and soft. Some theorists recognize a third scale, *cantus fictus*, at a two-flat signature, using the soft hexachord together with a fictive hexachord whose *ut* is on B♭ and which includes e♭ as *fa*.
- The exception worth holding onto: the tritus modes used B♭ regularly in their untransposed position on F, so for them it is *cantus durus* and not *cantus mollis* that signals transposition, placing the tritus final at the upper-fifth cofinal c, or an octave lower on C. *Cantus fictus* signals transposition a further fourth up or fifth down, so that C, solmized as *re* in the fictive hexachord on B♭, can serve as an irregular final.
- From the late fourteenth century the gamut expands in two directions: new notes below gamma-ut and above ee-la, and interstitial pitches between the notes of the Hand. Those lack proper places and names, and are understood under *musica ficta* or *coniuncta*, both of which involve feigning hexachords with *ut* on notes other than C, G, and F so as to produce the wanted pitch as a member of a fictive *mi*–*fa* semitone. That is the primary meaning of the notational signs, which are the signs for hard b (♮ or ♯) and soft b (♭) placed on the appropriate line or space: they instruct the singer to sing or conceive the note as *mi* or as *fa*, which usually but not always alters its pitch by a semitone.

## fuller, "organum – discantus – contrapunctus in the middle ages" (pp. 477–502) · c. 850 to c. 1480

Three phases named by their governing terms, with an argument against reading any of them as composition rules of general validity.

**The framing case: "Quilibet arectans," early fourteenth century**

Circulated under the authority of Jehan des Murs and best identified by its incipit. Its precepts:

- Strictly note against note.
- Permitted intervals: perfect (unison, fifth, octave) and imperfect (minor third, major third, major sixth), plus octave compounds.
- Natural sequel to any interval is the nearest interval of the opposite category: minor third after unison, octave after major sixth.
- No perfect interval repeated in direct succession; imperfect intervals may chain.
- Contrary motion is the norm.
- Begin and end on a perfect consonance.

Its companion "Cum notum sit omnibus cantoribus" defines *contrapunctus* as placing or making (*ponere vel facere*) a point or note against another, and as the foundation of discant. Fuller's point about the pair: these are treatises on musical production, not on composition, and the dual verbs and dual nouns fold extemporized and notated polyphony together without distinguishing them.

**Three discontinuities across the tradition**

- Idiom: *organum in symphoniae*, *musica mensurabilis*, and fourteenth-century *contrapunctus* are distinct musical languages requiring distinct instructions.
- Perspective: informal teaching manuals and bare precept lists sit alongside scholarly treatises. "Quilibet arectans" and Jacques of Liège's *Speculum musicae* are roughly contemporary and share almost nothing.
- Precept against practice: theorists' rules and notated repertory diverge, partly from allegiance to rationalizable axioms and partly because older ways of singing persisted. Elias Salomo describes discant in parallel fifths, fourths, and octaves as current in the late thirteenth century; Jacques of Liège opens a chapter on discant intervals with an example in parallel fourths and fifths.

**Organum, c. 850 to c. 1030**

- *Musica* and *Scolica enchiriadis* introduce multi-voice singing under the *symphoniae*: three simple (fourth, fifth, octave) and three compound. Organum proper is singing at the fourth and fifth, with octave doublings of either voice.
- The maximum illustrated texture is four lines spanning two octaves; up to six simultaneous lines are allowed, by men's and boys' voices or voices with instruments. Examples are short, mostly from the Office, since the practice is easily extemporized over an entire chant.
- Daseian notation keeps parallel fifths perfect. Organum at the fourth cannot parallel consistently: *Musica enchiriadis* blames the tritone between the deuterus of a tetrachord and the tritus four notes below, so the organal voice may not descend below tetrardus, sustains that pitch, and produces seconds and thirds against the cantus before converging at the unison. *Scolica* gives a different reason, that the organal voice cannot replicate the trope or mode of the principal and must follow its own natural law.
- Guido's *Micrologus*, c. 1026–28, chapters 18 and 19, describes strict (*durus*) and flexible (*mollis*) diaphony, matching the two *Enchiriadis* types. Flexible organum admits four intervals only, ranked from the fourth down to the minor third; the organal voice never descends below a tritus pitch (F or C) unless the cantus does; phrase endings converge by *occursus*. Guido gives no reasons, which suits his orientation toward instructing boys.
- The Winchester organa of c. 1000 show a wider range of choices for the organal voice than the theorists allow. Taken with the reworkings of the *Enchiriadis* passages and Guido's insistence that he reports local usage, this suggests the early theory idealizes a more fluid practice.

**Discantus, the 1200s**

- *Musica mensurabilis* is coined in Paris and puts rhythm and its notation at the center. Genres are distinguished by rhythmic quality: discant, *organum in speciali*, *copula*. The split from *musica plana* leaves the eight church modes on the plainchant side, so *modus* in organum treatises means rhythmic mode.
- John of Garland's *De mensurabili musica* defines discant as the sounding together of diverse melodies according to rhythmic mode and mutual correspondence in length through concords. Melodic independence is the stress.
- His concord and discord table, each ranked perfect, medial, imperfect:

| | perfect | medial | imperfect |
|---|---|---|---|
| concords | unison, octave | fifth, fourth | major third, minor third |
| discords | semitone, tritone, major seventh | whole tone, minor sixth | major sixth, minor seventh |

- The Boethian ratio justification is imperfect on its own terms, since the whole tone at 9:8 ought to rank higher than it does. Franco later drops the medial discord category and moves the tone and minor sixth.
- What is remarkable from either side of it: Garland admits every interval within the octave into discant, ranked but not excluded.
- Contextual softening: any discord before a perfect or medial concord counts as medial, and rules yield to *color* or *pulchritudo*. Concords are required at odd-numbered positions in rhythmic modes 1, 2, and 3, which is the first linkage of stable interval quality to the beginning of a durational unit, and *color musicae* may override even that.
- Running alongside is the interval-succession tradition, *Klangschritt-Lehre*: stock responses to each diatonic cantus interval, moving in mainly contrary motion from one stable concord to another, with no reference to mensuration, phrase, or mode. The "Discantus positio vulgaris" gives a complete cycle; the Vatican organum treatise transmits over 340 elaborated upper-voice motions and ends with three complete organa.
- A subgroup teaches how to *quintare*, accompanying a chant in parallel fifths punctuated by octaves. Anonymous IV distinguishes these *plani cantores* from true discantors, and calls them young, which suggests parallel singing was the first polyphonic training boys received.

**Contrapunctus, the 1300s and 1400s**

- Sachs lists some 185 treatises, of which nine survive in four or more copies. "Quilibet arectans" leads with twelve sources. Two strands: a northern Paris-centered one and a southern Italian one, prominent in the early fifteenth century with Prosdocimo de' Beldomandi at Padua and Ugolino of Orvieto.
- The defining move: note-against-note texture is now a structural level beneath an elaborated surface, in the recurring metaphor a foundation to a building. The elaborated surface has no settled name: *cantus fractabilis*, *contrapunctus diminutus*, *flores musicae mensurabilis*, *voces dividere*. Prosdocimo in 1412 distinguishes *contrapunctus communiter sive large* (many notes against one, which he denies is counterpoint) from *propria sive stricte*.
- Imperfect concords become structural components: perfect intervals are stable, imperfect unstable, and the rhetoric has thirds and sixths seeking perfection. Natural sequels: minor third to unison, major third to fifth, major sixth to octave. Chains of imperfect intervals are allowed and in effect delay resolution.
- Because imperfect intervals are now structural, the rule that a *contrapunctus* begins and ends on a perfect concord has to be stated. "Cum notum sit" gives the reason from perception: ending on an imperfect concord would leave the *anima* suspended, with no signal that the song had ended.
- Ficta enters unevenly. "Quilibet arectans" requires minor thirds to be made major unless followed by a unison, and admits only major sixths, without discussing *musica ficta* as such, which was usually handled under *musica plana*. Petrus dictus palma ociosa treats *falsa* inside a counterpoint context and needs it for major-third-to-fifth and major-sixth-to-octave progressions. Marchetto founds the Italian line insisting on the closest possible junction; Prosdocimo and Ugolino follow. Johannes Boen testifies in 1357 to the proliferation of semitone inflections in England and northwestern Europe.
- Elaboration is taught through mensuration. Petrus dictus palma ociosa's *Compendium* of 1336 gives twelve exercises running systematically through the combinations of modus, tempus, and prolation. On dissonance he says only that one should not dwell on it. The Parisian theorist of 1375 reports two schools, one requiring half or more of the pitches above a cantus tone to be consonant, the other requiring the first to be, with a syncopation exception.
- Vernacular systems for producing ordinary polyphony, transmitted informally and often in the vernacular:
  - The Italian *gradus* system, four hexachord zones (*di pari*, *di quarta*, *di quinta*, *di octava*), in which the hexachord limit restricts both intervals and notes. Against a cantus *ut* the singer may sound unison, third, fifth, or sixth; against a cantus *mi*, only the unison or the thirds, since a fifth would exceed the hexachord.
  - English sights: quatreble, treble, mean, countertenor, countir. The singer visualizes an interval on the staff and sounds a different one, so the mean sights a unison and sings a fifth above, the treble an octave, the quatreble a twelfth. Leonel Power's exercises extend the interval-succession patterns over tenors of up to twelve notes.
  - Faburden: the treble parallels the plainsong in fourths above while the faburden voice sights thirds and unisons and sings a fifth lower, producing thirds and fifths below the chant. Guilielmus monachus later describes an Italianized version under the name *faulxbourdon* and transmits several procedures for three and four voices.
- Tinctoris's *Liber de arte contrapuncti* (1477) is the culmination and the break. Familiar topics (interval successions out to the triple diapason, eight general rules, simple against decorated counterpoint, dissonance regulation tied to mensuration), but the orientation shifts to notated music: the Prologue praises Ockeghem, Regis, and Busnois and traces them to Dunstable, Dufay, and Binchois, and Book II cites contrapuntal faults in real pieces by respected composers. His distinction between counterpoint sung *super librum* and composed *res facta* follows Prosdocimo's earlier *vocalis* against *scriptus*.
- The conclusion rejects Riemann's premise that a theory of art grounds the lawfulness of artistic creation. These writings mostly teach elementary production, their conventions hold for delimited strata and locales, and their sphere overlaps only partly with written composition.

## schubert, "counterpoint pedagogy in the renaissance" (pp. 503–33) · c. 1430 to c. 1618

Thesis: the real challenge for a Renaissance composer was not correct voice leading but the elaboration of a *soggetto*, which may be a melody, a duo, or an entire polyphonic composition. Voice leading was learned young for improvisation and came as naturally as grammatical speech.

**The four treatise families**

- Fifteenth century: Ugolino of Orvieto (*Declaratio musicae disciplinae*, c. 1430), Tinctoris (1477), Ramis de Pareia (1482), Gaffurio (1496). Counterpoint in two parts, simple and florid, brief rules with exhaustive example lists.
- Zarlino (1558) and followers: Pontio (1588), Tigrini (1588), Morley (1597), Artusi (1598), Cerreto (1601). Zarlino is the first to describe kinds of *soggetto*, to classify cadences, imitation, and double counterpoint, and to set conditions for repetition.
- The Spanish line: Lusitano (1553), Sancta María (1565), Montaños (1592), culminating in Cerone's *El melopeo* (1613), which absorbs both the Spanish material and Zarlino's.
- Early seventeenth-century Italians: Banchieri (*Cartella*, 1614), Rodio (1609), Brunelli (1610), Chiodino (1610), Angleria (1622), with Campion (c. 1618) as a chordal descendant of *contrapunctus simplex*.

**Simple counterpoint and the two-note unit**

- Ugolino starts from the direction of the tenor and the first vertical interval, and shows what second interval may follow. His rules are in rhymed couplets for memorization: the seventh rule, ascending from a sixth, is that a sixth desires a third if it aims above the note.
- Ramis quotes Ugolino's rhymes and corrects them, allowing a sixth to move to a fifth because from the minor sixth one aims strongly toward the fifth. That is the closest-approach principle, first clearly stated by Marchetto: one note of a perfect consonance should be approached by semitone.
- Tinctoris starts instead from the pair of vertical intervals and is exhaustive, taking nineteen chapters of Book I on the condition that no voice leaps more than a fifth and that similar fifths are barred, and repeating whole series for compound intervals.
- The difference matters for how a singer thought: Ugolino's orientation suits the improviser watching the cantus about to rise, Tinctoris's prioritizes the vertical succession.

**Where counterpoint touches mode**

- Tinctoris: ficta added to correct a diminished fifth causes commixture, because B♭ turns the first-species fourth A to D into a second-species fourth, which belongs to mode 4.
- Aaron objects to a B♭ in the bass signature to correct an imperfect fifth with the tenor F, since the bass would then sing a species foreign to the upper voices.
- Cerreto, as late as 1601, applies closest approach unless the accidental would leave the mode.
- Ramis rewrites a line added to a Dorian cantus firmus because it emphasizes b and a skip from b to e, both Phrygian; his replacement uses reiterations and leaps proper to Dorian, and its Ds all form perfect intervals with the cantus.
- Tinctoris's fifth rule: above no note should a perfection be taken by which *distonatio* can occur. "Perfection" covers both the octave and the cadence, and the rule anticipates all later strictures on which cadences suit which mode.

**Florid counterpoint and the proto-species approach**

- Morley in 1597 still calls the two genres counterpoint and descant.
- Montaños grades added lines against a cantus firmus in breves: breves as the first manner, semibreves the second, minims the third or first diminution, semiminims the second diminution.
- Cerone recommends learning note against note, then two minims to the semibreve, then four semiminims, and teaches "guides," two semibreves in note-against-note texture that establish the positions and are filled in afterward. His guides often contain voice-leading errors that the diminution corrects.
- Dahlhaus objected that species counterpoint on a cantus firmus is hardly reconcilable with Palestrina's pervasively imitative style. Schubert's answer: the objection assumes that the improvised training should resemble the finished composition. Treat the cantus firmus as a chain of two-note fragments and it supplies a vocabulary of consonances underlying every texture. Cerone: as 22 letters make thousands of orations, 22 intervals make all music, and he calls his stock semiminim patterns an *Abecedario*.

**Contraponto fugato**

- Names for the technique: *contraponto con obbligo*, *per perfidia*, *per riditta*, *capriccioso*, *ymitacion*, "maintaining a point." Names for the short motive: *inventioni*, *riditti*, *punti*, *passaggi*, *passi*, *pertinacie*.
- The term descends from Tinctoris's definition of *fuga* as the identity of the parts of a line as to the value, name, form, and sometimes placement of notes and rests, where "name" means solmization syllable and "form" means notation. Repetition within a line therefore counts as *fuga*.
- Tinctoris raises repetition negatively, in his sixth rule: avoid repetitions when singing above a plainchant, especially if the tenor repeats, since the whole combination would then be duplicated.
- Zarlino permits repetition of a motive under three conditions: both it and the cantus firmus change pitch level, producing a sequence; or the motive is accompanied by different vertical intervals; or it is varied rhythmically. Artusi renders the conditions as a flow chart.
- Motives may be freely invented or drawn from the cantus firmus (*contraponto ad imitatione*), and may be transposed, displaced, rhythmically altered, used as ostinati, inverted, retrograded, or set as *fuga d'inganno*, in which the melody is defined by solmization syllables so that hexachordal mutation changes the sounding pitches.
- Montaños calls any repetition *ymitacion* and divides it into *general* (both parts transposed, a harmonic sequence) and *particular* (the motive repeated in different values against different vertical intervals). Cerone's finer distinction, *thema* or *subiecto* against *passo* or *invencione*, is the more useful one.
- Lusitano uses the technique to give form: a *passo* at the beginning, another in the middle, the first again at the end, producing a simple ABA.
- Licenses are granted for the sake of the obligation. Zarlino allows liberties because the style is very difficult; Morley writes that in fugues "the point excuseth it"; Cerreto suspends closest approach under harder conditions; Cerone apologizes for an example on the ground that the obligation excuses it.
- The payoff for analysis: Zarlino's three conditions prove that composers were attentive to the succession of vertical intervals as a repeatable object.

**Canonic duo on a cantus firmus**

- Two added lines must imitate each other strictly at a fixed time and pitch interval and each work against the cantus firmus. Keyboardists and singers could improvise it.
- Lusitano supplies memorizable sequential patterns of cantus-firmus segments (third up and step down, fourth up and third down, and so on), so any cantus firmus can be read as a succession of borrowed segments. His duos move mostly note against note, at one or two minims, at the fourth or fifth.
- Zarlino on the mental operation: the contrapuntist, writing or improvising, must always visualize what the consequent will do, and one should not be surprised at occasional lapses.

**Double counterpoint**

- Inversion at the octave, tenth, and twelfth. The rules are stated as prohibitions: no strong-beat sixths for the twelfth, since they become sevenths; no parallel thirds or sixths for the tenth, since they become octaves and fifths.
- Brunelli inverts both parts melodically and exchanges their positions, yielding the same succession of vertical intervals, then doubles one or both parts in tenths, combining inversion at the tenth and twelfth in a single texture. To make the composite work the original combination must contain no parallel motion, so the elaboration has to be planned before the material is chosen.
- Chiodino's three-voice examples run like a word ladder: two voices move down a third while one of them is replicated at the unison or octave, and the third combination is "the same as the first," a third lower.
- Rodio distinguishes inverted counterpoints "called double," where the parts switch position, from inverted counterpoints "without the tenor being inverted," where the added part is simply transposed by a third, fourth, fifth, or sixth. Angleria uses inversion at the third and sixth to explain parallel lines against a cantus firmus, a typical seventeenth-century texture.

**From cantus firmus to free imitation**

- The missing link is diminution, which most authors discuss for a single line out of context. Montaños supplies the exception: a point in semibreves imitating at the fifth below, then a version with repeated notes and passing notes, then one adding consonant skips, a suspension in diminished values, and a lower neighbor. The rhythmic independence of the final version conceals its note-against-note origin.
- A second link is the *canto figurato* cantus firmus, a melody in mixed values, typically a chant paraphrase or a line from a chanson. Zarlino adds a line to the soprano of a two-voice Willaert motet, imitating fragments of it. Banchieri updates the exercise to the *moderna pratica*, taking melodies from Lassus and Rore sestina settings, and changes technique every few breves: imitation at the sixth, *scherzi* on a tiny motive, imitation by inversion, closest approach, evaded cadences, word painting, and *fuga anteveduta*, where the added voice begins before the given one.
- Writing against a duo goes back to the fifteenth century. Burzio identifies soprano and tenor as the duo to which a third voice is added; Gaffurio has the bass and soprano in parallel tenths as the duo taking a tenor. Zarlino adds a third line to a Josquin duo and still works in imitative fragments, and permits license, since the added third part is hard. Artusi says the singer needs long practice on a cantus firmus, a quick ear, and a good memory.

**Sancta María and paired duos**

*Libro llamado arte de tañer fantasia* (1565) is the fullest account of large-scale technique at mid-century, addressed to organists but independent of medium.

- A *passo* may be presented in a duo, in three parts with the outer voices in sixths or tenths, or in four-voice chords. Parallel sixths are allowed in the course of a phrase and never at its beginning or end.
- Duos are *en fuga* or *sin fuga*. Imitative duos are *travadas* when the second voice enters before the first has finished (at a minim or semibreve) and *sueltas* when the first voice finishes and then accompanies (his example imitates at two breves). In practice the distinction is hard to draw, since melodic functions elide.
- In a *passo sin fuga* both voices begin together with different melodies, and it becomes impossible to say which carries the *passo*, so Sancta María recommends at least a little imitation to clarify it. Cerone prefers the non-imitative duo because the words are more easily understood.
- Four-part openings are built from paired duos, with the second pair entering against one of the three steps of the first pair's cadence: the consonant preparation of the suspension, the dissonance, and its resolution. Chapters are titled by which voice enters, on which step, and with what melodic motion, and give the required interval between the entering note and the cadential goal.
- A modal consequence worth teaching: in his own example the cadences to G are wrong by his criteria, since the opening entries put the piece unambiguously in mode 1 or 2, which should cadence on D and A. The G cadence follows from the requirement that the soprano entry be dissonant with the suspension. Prior decisions box in the modal outcome.

**Four-part sonority**

- Zarlino prefers vertical sonorities containing a third with either a fifth or a sixth, one voice doubled, and gives chord tables that take the tenor-to-soprano interval as given and list options for alto and bass, allowing unisons and crossing. The tables are not exhaustive and the criteria are largely a preference for the unmediated intervals of the *senario*.
- Sancta María starts instead from the soprano-to-bass interval, likening the bass to a foundation, and sorts four-voice sonorities into groups by that interval (octave, tenth, twelfth, thirteenth and compounds), ranking the inner-voice arrangements into four classes. His criteria: only one pitch class doubled, not the soprano's, and sixths above the bass used sparingly. The wider the outer voices, the more open the inner ones. Students are to memorize the class 1 voicing for every outer-voice interval.
- His four-part note-against-note exercises take a soprano scale, an opening position, and a pattern of outer-voice intervals, cycling class 1 chords of the twelfth, thirteenth, and tenth until the cadence.
- Campion's tables are more restrictive: every chord is a 5/3, and by his "most familiar, and infallible rule," when the bass ascends the octave above the first bass note goes to the fifth above the next, the fifth to the third, and the third to the octave, reversed when the bass descends. With Campion one may finally speak of simultaneous composition, and seventeenth-century composers working from dance ostinati and *passacaglie* stand where earlier ones worked from chant tenors.
- Burmeister reverses the operation: compose a short four-voice harmonic passage, extract the voice best suited to begin, place it diagonally down the score in all four voices, and the result is a canon at the unison. Fattorini's hundreds of *accadenze*, reprinted by Diruta, are meant to be unzipped the same way.
- Closing: counterpoint treatises offer almost no guidance on writing a good melody. Bermudo calls counterpoint the father of melody.

## judd, "renaissance modal theory" (pp. 364–406) · 1476 to 1626

A localized study of mid-century Venice rather than a survey, framed by Powers's question "Is Mode Real?"

**The problem, as the sixteenth century stated it**

- Aaron in 1525 claims that no writer of his age has explained how the many different modes are to be recognized, and that the subject was abandoned by celebrated musicians as troublesome rather than through ignorance.
- The difficulty is structural: modal theory is intimately tied to monophony in its origins, and Aaron appears to be the first to articulate the problem of applying it to polyphony.
- "Mode" in modern English conflates *modus*/*tonus*, *modo*/*tuono*, and *tropus*, used sometimes interchangeably and sometimes distinctly.

**Two traditions inside modal theory**

- The ecclesiastical tradition: a scheme of classification by final, ambitus, and beginnings, whose core Wiering labels the *omnis cantus* definition, a mode being the rule by which every song is judged. The final supplies the rule, and this strain stays essentially unchanged.
- Pseudo-classical species theory: seven octave species from Boethius, subordinated to the final by Marchetto, with a mode built from a species of fifth plus a species of fourth. The species are ordered patterns of tones and semitones, usually represented by solmization and abbreviated by their boundary syllables, so the first species of fifth is tone-semitone-tone-tone, or *re–la*. Hexachordal relationships enter modal theory through this door.

**The unresolved tensions Judd names**

- Mode in relation to polyphony as well as chant.
- Mode applied to secular as well as sacred genres.
- Mode as an a priori compositional construct against an a posteriori analytical deduction.
- Classification by species against classification by final; the relevance of the authentic and plagal distinction to polyphony; the mode of a single voice against that of the whole complex; and whether modal determinants are audible at all.

**The treatise typology, and what a musician in the 1540s would have known**

- The *cantorinus*: pocket manuals teaching clergy and choirboys to sing the liturgy, beginning with solmization and the Hand, then mutation, the *diapente* and *diatessaron*, and modal ambitus, then chants and recitation formulas. Reprinted through the seventeenth century, culminating in Banchieri's *Cantorino* of 1622.
- Humanist school textbooks in question-and-answer form, such as Cochlaeus's *Tetrachordum musices*.
- Manuals for performers and composers. Zarlino owned Vanneus's *Recanetum de musica aurea* (1533), which is rarely cited precisely because it innovates nothing, and is therefore the best index of ordinary modal knowledge. Mode appears there under plainsong, in the last chapters of Book I; Book II is mensural theory, Book III counterpoint.
- Vanneus's definition, after Gaffurio, falls in the *omnis cantus* class. His diagram lists the four finals by *vox* and *littera* (D sol re, E la mi, F fa ut, G sol re ut), the four *maneriae* above and below, authentics on top and plagals mirrored beneath. He gives the fourteenth-century psalm-tone mnemonic beginning "Primus cum sexto fa sol la semper habebunt."
- Vanneus's table of modal affects, which composers are told they must know so as to join like with like on pain of being thought an unmusical musician: 1 cheerful and adaptable, 2 woeful and deprecatory, 3 sharp and harsh, 4 given to love and adulation, 5 moderate and jocund, 6 pious and tearful, 7 querulous and mixed, 8 mild and sweet.
- Mode enters his counterpoint book only peripherally, in the chapter on cadences.
- Speculative books for the literati, in which Gaffurio's *De harmonia* (1518) moves the modes out of practical theory into *musica speculativa* with ancient nomenclature, ethos, and cosmic analogy.
- Two Venetian treatises tie mode to polyphony more specifically than any before: Aaron's *Trattato* (1525) and Del Lago's *Breve introduttione* (1540). Both are in Italian, and Del Lago's takes up the modes inside a book on counterpoint with no plainchant section at all.

**Aaron, and why his method is a posteriori**

- His examples are real pieces from Petrucci and Antico prints of 1500 to 1522, which makes the treatise testable, unlike Tinctoris's invented illustrative phrases.
- He is reconciling a given repertory to a given system rather than reporting how music was composed. His priorities run from the mode of the tenor or pre-existing melody through final, species, and *processo*.
- His device for awkward finals is the *differentia*, normally a melodic formula ending a psalm tone, redeployed as a specific pitch that may terminate the tenor in place of the final or cofinal. Judd calls this theoretically dubious; it stirs together modal theory, psalmodic convention, and secular genre, and it caused his later readers lasting trouble.
- His citations of polyphony stop after chapter 7, which leaves the relation of the rest of the treatise to polyphony open.

**Printers, ordering, and modal cycles**

- Around 1540 printers begin ordering partbook publications by tonal type, defined after Hermelink and Powers as a combination of ambitus (low clefs c1 to f4 against the *chiavette* g2 to c4), system (*cantus durus* or *cantus mollis*), and final. Judd's table of Zarlino's 1549 *Musica Quinque Vocum* shows Gardano grouping the nineteen motets by signature and final.
- Such ordering usually reflects pragmatism rather than theory. Some prints, however, advertise modality: Scotto's two books of madrigals for two and three voices (1541) label pieces by mode, though the collections are not in modal order and mostly cover authentics only.
- Rore's *Madrigali a cinque voci* (1542) appears to be the first fully ordered modal collection, and the appearance of an ordered Rore motet print in 1545 suggests the ordering came from the composer.
- Martinengo's 1544 madrigals state on the title page that he composed and arranged them in modal order, which raises the possibility that modal order structured whole cycles of texts, not just individual pieces.
- Willaert's circle is preoccupied with the possibilities. The hard question is telling a posteriori ordering (a publisher's device) from a generative one, and the answer may lie in the kind of print: anthology, single-composer print, or large-scale textual cycle.

**Zarlino's Song of Songs cycle and the fate of one motet**

- An unprecedented eight-mode setting of the Song of Songs itself rather than the usual centonate liturgical texts, one chapter per mode: chapter 1 as four motets in mode 1 on D, chapter 2 as mode 2, chapter 3 as mode 3. He stopped there.
- Because the text order fixes the modal order, modal affect can have played at best a minimal role in the plan. Modal order supplies narrative continuity and asserts the primacy of the eight ecclesiastical modes in freely composed motets.
- *Ego veni in hortum meum*, from the beginning of chapter 5, marks the end of the work. Its tonal type is g2–♭–C,F, the same Rore used in 1542. The opening point of imitation signals mode 5 by bounding melodic lines on C and F and by modified tonal imitation; the *secunda pars* does the same; the ranges of superius, tenor, and quintus are authentic mode 5.
- The built-in flaw: the *diapente* is not the third species proper to tritus, because the cantus mollis signature converts it into the species proper to modes 7 and 8. Theorists had long accepted the substitution to avoid the tritone, and Vanneus's generic mode 5 formula carries a B♭ signature even where no melodic difficulty requires it. That anomaly is one of the pressures behind the dodecachordal reform.
- The motet's afterlife: composed as mode 5; labeled Ionian in the 1549 print, using Glarean's new names; cited as mode 11 in the 1558 *Istitutioni*; and, after Zarlino's renumbering, mode 1 in 1573. Zarlino dismantled and redistributed the cycle across three prints, obscuring its origins.

**Glarean**

- Hints at dissatisfaction in the *Isagoge* (1516) and rejects the eight-mode system only in the *Dodecachordon* (1547), a folio of 470 pages with numerous polyphonic examples in choirbook format, no counterpoint book, and no *theorica* in the usual sense.
- The essence: modes derived not from adding *diapente* and *diatessaron* but from dividing the *diapason*. Six usable octave species, since the B octave has a false fifth, give six authentic and plagal pairs. Palisca has shown that Glarean got his Greek modal theory badly wrong.
- Unlike Aaron, he prints the music he discusses. His examples were largely compiled by 1527 and the treatise finished around 1539, so the repertory was antiquated by publication. He authenticates the new theory with chant, which Judd reads as the gesture of a northern Catholic humanist facing the Reformation.

**Zarlino's own modal writing**

- Part IV of the *Istitutioni* (1558) draws on three strands: traditional modal discourse as mediated by Glarean, the Venetian strand from Aaron with its citations of printed polyphony, and the *musica practica* tradition of the counterpoint book.
- He adopts Glarean's twelve modes without acknowledgment and is openly contemptuous of Vanneus and Aaron. He uses Glarean's classical names in the 1549 print and abandons them in Part IV in favor of numbers.
- Each chapter follows one format: species of the mode, then ethos, then regular initials and cadences (final, third, fifth), then citations of polyphony, then a newly composed didactic duo, then the irregularities. The duos are the original contribution, and they move the discussion from Aaron's classification toward prescription.
- The mode 11 chapter as a specimen: derived from the third species of octave C to c divided harmonically at G; suited to *balli*, so called lascivious by some; instanced by the Missa de Angelis, *Alma redemptoris mater*, and *Regina coeli*, all of which had been unproblematically mode 5; and instanced in polyphony by Josquin's *Stabat mater*, works by Willaert, Jacquet, and Gombert, and Zarlino's own *Ego veni*. All the polyphonic citations share the tonal type g2–♭–C,F, while the duo has C as final.
- The *Dimostrationi* (1571) renumbers the modes from C to a with no gap, correlating the finals with the hexachord syllables and establishing the priority of harmonic over arithmetic division. Ionian becomes mode 1, Hypoionian mode 2. The renumbering is what drives the revisions in Part IV of the 1573 edition.
- The print-culture window: at his death Zarlino owned some 1,141 books. His citations in Part IV run from ancients to moderns, where "ancient" means the *Liber selectarum cantionum* of 1520 and "modern" means Willaert's *Musica nova* of 1559. Those two prints and his own supply most of nearly eighty citations.

**After Zarlino**

- The eight-mode system persists: Dressler (1561), Aiguino (1562, and in 1581 refuting twelve- and fourteen-mode systems), Cerreto (1601 and 1626). The eight-mode cyclic compositions of Palestrina and Lasso are its strongest testimony.
- Dodecachordal theory travels through Hoffmann's *Doctrina de tonis* (1582) in the German Lateinschule tradition, and through Artusi (1586), Tigrini (1588), and Cerone (1613) in Italy. Twelve-mode cycles are most often instrumental, by Merulo, Gabrieli, Luzzaschi, and Frescobaldi, most of them with Venetian connections to Zarlino. Keyboard treatises such as Banchieri's carry the eight- and twelve-mode systems side by side. In England the influence of continental modal theory is minimal.

**The analytical postscript**

- The 1960s and 70s reaction against anachronistic tonal analysis (Besseler, Lowinsky) sent musicologists to sixteenth-century theorists, and mostly to Aaron rather than Tinctoris.
- The results conflict: Perkins builds a study of Josquin's masses on Aaron's principles; Bergquist argues Aaron's classifications are irrelevant to polyphony; Powers shows Aaron's concern extends only to tenors; Krantz works from Meier's codification instead.
- Judd's own hypothetical classification of Josquin's motets on Aaron's criteria showed how uneasily the repertory sits with the theory, and invoking repercussion, other voices, and cadence tones does not ease the fit. Her alternative is three tonal systems described as *ut*, *re*, *mi* tonalities, combining hexachordal position and pitch of the final, melodic character, contrapuntal procedure, and registral convention. She is explicit that the combination is a modern analytical construction.
- Owens has shown the pervasive twentieth-century superimposition of a "neo-modal" system of transposable scales functioning as keys, producing labels like G-Dorian and B♭-Ionian. Scale type in the modern sense is a resultant of earlier models of generating modes, not a defining feature.
- Meier is the strongest case for modality as an inherent property of sixteenth-century polyphony, and was criticized on two fronts: his polemic with Dahlhaus over authentic and plagal in polyphony, both sides arguing from Palestrina's offertory cycle, and a broader charge of uncritical use of sources and readiness to attach expressive interpretation.

## powers, "tonal types and modal categories in renaissance polyphony" (pp. 428–70) · 1525 to 1595

The article the other three orbit.

**The historical setup**

- The modal system that supposedly governed medieval and Renaissance polyphony began as an eighth- and ninth-century Carolingian borrowing from Byzantine chant theory, applied to classifying single-line melodies, and stabilized by the end of the eleventh century.
- Polyphonic theory was preoccupied with ensemble control, with durational relationships and vertical sonorities, and there is only one piece of evidence in medieval technical literature that polyphony was ever thought subject to the church modes, with good indications to the contrary.
- The separateness persisted in treatise design: rules for simultaneities under counterpoint, the modal system in its own section. Zarlino's Books III and IV are the case in point.
- The stimulus for importing modal theory into polyphony was cultural rather than technical: the humanist recovery of the doctrine of ethos, whose effects were attributed to the modes. Since the only modes anyone actually knew were the church modes, which already carried Greek names grafted on in the ninth century, the humanists took them for descendants of the ancient ones.
- Burzio (1487) already tells the composer of chansons to know the modes, lists their affects, and specifies the order of composing the voices with the highest first, which foreshadows a pattern that matures three-quarters of a century later in Finck and Dressler.
- Tinctoris's fifth rule for counterpoint (1477) guards the mode of the given chant, not any mode of the polyphonic complex. In his modal treatise of 1476 he claims the modes govern all music, then assigns a mode to each voice separately and says the mode of the whole is that of the tenor.
- Aaron in 1525 is the first to claim modality as a unifier for polyphony and to test the claim on a large body of real pieces. What he does is assign pieces to categories after the fact, adducing reasons for each choice.

**The central distinction**

- Showing that the eightfold system can be made a set of categories to which any piece can be assigned a posteriori is not showing that mode is an a priori pre-compositional property, as tonality is for every eighteenth-century piece.
- The asymmetry is the evidence: from Mattheson to Schoenberg nobody disputes how many tonalities there are, while the second half of the sixteenth century has two competing general schools of polyphonic modal theory with subvarieties in each.
- A *tonal type* is a class of polyphonic compositions minimally characterized by system, cleffing, and final sonority. Abbreviations: ♮ for *cantus durus* and ♭ for *cantus mollis*; g2 for the *chiavette* and c1 for standard clefs; the final named by its pitch class.
- Etic against emic: a tonal type is identifiable by its three markers and observable apart from any musical or cultural context. A mode is bound into sixteenth-century musical culture, a living doctrine of the church and a construct being experimented with, and requires study on its own terms.
- The formulation: a mode is always a tonal type, but a tonal type is not always a mode.

**How printers and composers actually grouped things**

- Early in the century, Petrucci's anthologies show no discernible musical basis for arrangement.
- Later, two practical criteria dominate: system, and whether the voices as a group use a higher or lower segment of the gamut, marked by high or low clefs.
- Where the final is a third criterion, the collection almost always falls into an intended eight- or twelve-mode pattern with a clear authentic and plagal distinction.
- The high-clef against low-clef contrast, with system and final held constant, represents the authentic against plagal contrast in principle, but there are other ways to make it, and it is the contrast itself that is essential.

**Hermelink and Meier**

- Hermelink's *Dispositiones modorum* classified Palestrina and Lasso objectively by system, cleffing, and final, arriving at twenty tonal types, and rejected traditional modal doctrine on the discrepancy between the multiplicity of types and the paucity of categories. Two different notations both count as Hypodorian, and he asks what the twofold possibility is for, if not for notating different kinds of pieces.
- Powers grants that the two will indeed have different characters, and answers that traditional theory could assign both to mode 2 by treating them as transpositions up a fifth and up a fourth.
- Meier showed the way to an emic understanding and demonstrated that one must distinguish authentic from plagal at least until about 1600, but grounded the claim on internal and partly subjective criteria (imitative fragments, cadential progressions, the primacy of the tenor), which Dahlhaus showed are not consistent enough in the music. Powers's verdict: the critique invalidates Meier's preferred justification, not the distinction.
- Meier had himself called attention to Valerio Bona's explicit description in 1595 of the link between cleffing and modal ordering, and then preferred not to rely on cleffing.
- The logical difficulty in Meier is treating the modes as pre-compositional entities to be composed out, and not distinguishing mode as musical property from mode as category, so that if one instance of a tonal type in a genre correlates with a mode, all instances are said to be in that mode.

**The tabulated collections**

- Rore, five-voice madrigals (1542), the earliest unmistakably modal collection Powers knows: odd and even pieces contrasted by ambitus with final and system constant, modes 1 and 2 transposed, the last three madrigals outside the cycle.
- Susato, chansons for two and three voices (1544): grouping at double remove, since the pieces are reductions of chansons by others, and Meissner's Glarean labels for the four groups are anachronistic for 1544.
- Lasso, Penitential Psalms plus Psalm 148 (composed c. 1560, published 1584): psalms and modes each in their own numerical order, so no expression of the texts through modal affect can have been intended and the message is in the order itself. Modes 3 and 4 are contrasted by lower clefs in three outer voices rather than by the standard pair, which reflects the traditionally reduced practical contrast in the deuterus pair.
- Lasso, *Lagrime di San Pietro* (1595): four madrigals in ♮-c1-D for mode 1, four in ♭-c1-G for mode 2, in each case with the third of the subgroup closing a fifth higher; no authentic and plagal contrast in the deuterus pair; modes 5 and 6 contrasted normally as ♭-g2-F against ♭-c1-F; mode 7 as ♮-g2-G; mode 8 absent, its place taken by a single Latin envoi in ♮-g2-A that no rationalization construes as mode 8.
- Palestrina, *Vergine* cycle (1581), the first thirty-two compositions of the Offertory cycle (1593), and the ottava rima *Figlio immortal* cycle (1594), all ordered by the church modes, the last more conventional in its modal representation than the *Vergine* cycle and, with the Lasso cycles, plausibly read as an affirmation of faith through the prescribed musical system of the church.
- Lasso's 1562 Munich motets, modally ordered on Lechner's testimony, reprinted by Gardano in Venice in November of the same year with the modal order destroyed and the pieces grouped as best he could by tonal type.
- Lasso 1582c, a composite reprint assembled with the author's consent from six earlier collections, which restores modal order and corrects two places where the sources had pieces out of order.

**The three lines of argument for keeping type and category apart**

- One to many in both directions. Most modes can be represented by more than one tonal type: the authentic and plagal contrast of modes 1 and 2 is realized four ways across four collections, by ambitus (Rore 1542), by system and final (Penitential Psalms), by ambitus in reverse (the 1577 duos), and by final alone (the *Vergine* cycle). Conversely one type can stand for more than one mode: ♮-g2-A is mode 3 for Susato in the 1540s and mode 1 for Palestrina in the 1580s. The two A-types have little in common musically: ♮-g2-A incipits orient on *re–la* and cadence on a and d, ♮-c1-A on *mi–la* and cadence on a and e, with e frequent and d unused.
- Modes were not always recognized as such, even at the height of their conscious application, as the Gardano reprint shows.
- Types have different patterns of use in modal and non-modal collections. ♮-g2-A is Palestrina's fourth most frequent type on Hermelink's count and appears in only two modal collections, both anomalously. ♮-g2-C is standard for mode 6 in the Franco-Flemish tradition, claimed for mode 7 in an Italian theoretical line running from Aaron through Aiguino on the strength of a psalm-tone *differentia*, and was a favorite of Palestrina's, the type of the Pope Marcellus Mass, that he never once used in a modal collection.
- Two Hollander motets in Susato's series appear to have been assigned by their opening melodies alone: *Congratulamini* opens with a characteristic mode 6 figure and sits in the mode 6 book, *Beatus athleta* opens *ut–sol* and sits in the mode 7 book though both parts end on a C major triad, and in each case the *secunda pars* incipit contradicts the assignment.
- The generalization: where a type fits the traditional features of a category well it was used often to represent it; where it fits less well, as with the A- and C-tonalities, it was used rarely, ambiguously, or not at all, however common it was in the repertory at large.

**The closing argument**

- Two obstacles to understanding Renaissance tonal structure: confusing emic with etic, and the evolutionist stance of the major modern theories, whether modality gradually changing into tonality (Dahlhaus) or gradually being replaced by it as secular genres spread (Lowinsky).
- Both fail on two historical points: interest in and evidence for modality increased rather than lessened as the century wore on, and the major composers of the second half followed the old eight-mode system when they followed any scheme at all, without taking up Glarean's or Zarlino's twelve.
- Whether modality evolved into tonality is therefore a non-question, since they are of different orders. A particular tonal type can be ancestral to a particular tonality: ♭-g2-G and ♭-c1-G stand behind eighteenth-century G minor, and the very different ♮-g2-A and ♮-c1-A are among the progenitors of A minor. In that descent emic Renaissance modality plays no direct part, however necessary it is to understanding the polyphony itself.

## burnett and nitzberg, chapter 3, "the modal gamut in the sixteenth century" (pp. 39–88) · c. 1500 to 1650

The analytical spine of the unit.

**What the chapter claims**

- The music of Rore through Lasso to Monteverdi fluctuates between a chromatically extended modal system and an emerging key-centered one, often deliberately setting them against each other in the same piece. The usual responses are anachronistic mixtures of modal and key-centered terminology or purely Schenkerian readings that equate the music with eighteenth-century tonality.
- The alternative offered: a theory built on constructs available to sixteenth- and seventeenth-century musicians, namely hexachordal modulation of eleven-pitch-class areas.
- The claim is explicitly forward-looking. Understanding modal hexachordal gamut modulation is said to explicate similar operations in later tonal and even atonal music.

**The system**

- Guido's untransposed gamut contains three hexachords: *durum* on G, *naturale* on C, *molle* on F. Renamed by function, the upper fifth is the dominant hexachord, the lower fifth the subdominant, and the governing one the central hexachord, at any transposition level.
- The central hexachord reordered as fifths supplies all the structurally significant roots and all the essential cadential points of every mode of that gamut. For naturalis: F–C–G–d–a–e.
- Eleven pitch classes per system: the six of the central hexachord, the added flat from the subdominant hexachord, one added natural or sharp from the dominant hexachord, and three sharps needed to form the major sixth at cadences. Quality of third above each degree, in naturalis, runs A, E, B♭/B♮, F♮/F♯, C♮/C♯, G♮/G♯.
- The twelfth pitch is the missing pitch: D♯/E♭ in naturalis, G♯/A♭ in mollis. Spelled as a flat, that is as the minor third above the central hexachord's root, it moves the system down a fifth; spelled as an augmented second, up a fifth.
- Constraints inside a system: B♭ may function contrapuntally as an upper neighbor to A or as part of a fifths cycle to F but cannot be a harmonic goal in naturalis; B♮ is a leading tone and, like the other sharps, is contrapuntally derived and cannot root a triad; a first-inversion diminished triad on B♮ resolving to C is admitted by Monteverdi's time, and a root-position triad on B♮ remains impossible.
- Four criteria determine the governing system: the signature; the absence of the missing pitch; the presence of the missing pitch spelled as a minor third above the central hexachord's root; the presence of the missing pitch spelled as an augmented second above it. Ficta must be written into the music, not supplied editorially, to count.
- Once key-centered tonality emerges at the end of the seventeenth century, the variable quality of each triad becomes fixed, the subdominant hexachord's material settles as pre-dominant harmony and becomes IV, and B♭ and B♮ stop being freely exchanged. The C naturalis system becomes C major, the F mollis system F major.

**The documentary case**

- Willaert in Venice in the 1530s begins the investigation of chromaticism as an expressive resource, driven by Petrarchan and Tasso-esque poetry and by interest in the Greek chromatic genus. Signatures nevertheless stay at durus, one flat, or rarely two throughout the sixteenth century and into the first half of the seventeenth; sharps as signature transpositions appear only in the 1640s.
- Conservative resistance quoted at length: Danckerts, judging the Vicentino and Lusitano debate of 1551, on greenhorn composers busying themselves only with sharpening and flattening notes; Artusi on Monteverdi's *Cruda Amarilli* cadencing more often in C than in its final G, and on mixtures that exceed the bounds of mixture; Galilei in 1591 on the sure identification of mode being derived from the last note in the bass, so that modern practitioners know their modes with the eyes and not the ears, and on modal ethos as tales intended to confuse dunderheads.
- Vicentino, *L'antica musica* (1555), chapter 32: four-voice cadences in every mode, using only C♯, F♯, G♯, and B♭ in cantus durus and only F♯, C♯, B♮, and E♭ in cantus mollis, with no operable cadence on A in modes 5 and 6.
- Banchieri, *L'organo suonarino* (1605) and *Cartella musicale* (1614): eight church modes, five in cantus durus and three in cantus mollis, with only G♯, C♯, and F♯ as leading tones in durus and only C♯, F♯, and B♮ in mollis, and no D♯ in either phrygian cadence.
- Harnisch, *Artis musicae delineatio* (1608): monophonic cadences in twelve modes recognizing final, third, and fifth as structural, consistently avoiding D♯ and A♯, and dropping a root-position cadence on B altogether as not in use.
- Kircher, *Musurgia universalis* (1650), Book 8: four-voice cadences through twelve modes in both cantus, ending each on a major third, with the hypophrygian marked *in hoc tono non valet clausula*.
- Wert's madrigal books 7 (1581), 10 (1591), and 11 (1595) contain no A♭, A♯, or D♯ anywhere; the tones are spelled G♯, B♭, and E♭. Each madrigal unfolds one flat only, B♭ in durus or E♭ in mollis, the remaining chromatics all sharps. Keyboard black keys of the period are labeled B♭, C♯, E♭, F♯, G♯.
- Lowinsky observed in 1946, in a footnote, that all seven Penitential Psalms of Lasso use exactly the same eleven tones, forming two chromatic tetrachords with only the tritone missing, and did not extend the finding beyond that cycle.
- Chafe is engaged at length and rejected. His four-hexachord framework (B♭, F, C, G) yields overlapping systems of twelve pitch classes, which makes them theoretically redundant and untransposable; his allowance of a major triad on B in the untransposed gamut requires a D♯ that the system does not contain; and he abandons hexachordal terminology halfway through his book.
- Berger's account of the limits of flats given the signature is accepted as matching general practice, with the refinement that the subdominant hexachord of the system always contains the greatest number of flats, and that signatures do not always reflect the operative system, since composers were reluctant to write more than two flats.

**The two arrays**

- PCA, the Primary Chromatic Array: the slow ascending chromatic unfolding of the tonic octave across an entire composition, numbered pc 0 to pc 11 from the final. It may omit pitch classes, in which case a second rise typically fills them in.
- PDA, the Primary Diatonic Array: the concurrent diatonic descent to the final, usually in the tenor.
- The justification is contrapuntal: a formal cadence is a 6–8 progression in which the upper voice rises to the final through a leading tone forming a major sixth with the tenor, and the two resolve outward, *ti* to *do* against *re* to *do*.

**The analyses, in the chapter's order**

- Josquin, *Dominus regnavit*: a 1♭ system carried by conflicting signatures (one flat in discantus and tenor, two in altus and bassus). No system modulation, which is typical for the early sixteenth century. The only chromatic relationship is E♭ against E♮, worked motivically through the progression E♭–c–C–F, which yields the dyad conflict. The deeper organization pairs two conjunct tetrachords a fifth apart, F–G–A–B♭ and B♭–C–D–E♭, from the central and subdominant hexachords. Smijers reduces the signatures to one flat and writes in the E♭s, tacitly agreeing on the system; Dahlhaus's counter-claim of a 2♭ system is unsupportable, since no A♭ appears or is implied and E♮s abound.
- La Rue (attributed), *Absalon, fili mi*: two flats in all four voices despite the scribal errors in Royal 8 G VII, so a 2♭ system, transposed lydian mollis on B♭. D♭ at m. 51 on "Non, vivam ultra" shifts to 3♭ with E♭ central; G♭ at m. 66 on "plorans" shifts to 4♭ with A♭ central. The final stays B♭ throughout while the mode turns from transposed lydian to transposed dorian mollis, and the quality of the B♭ triad changes from m. 36, which the chapter proposes as music history's earliest instance of a piece beginning in major and ending in its parallel minor. Each new system introduces its own missing pitch spelled as a flat, tracking David's deepening despair. The final system is uncontested, with no B♮ to revert it and no C♭ to continue.
- Rore, *Da le belle contrade d'oriente*: classic three-part mid-century madrigal form, diatonic outer sections in 1♭, chromatic middle. G♯ at m. 36 on "Che sarà qui di me" lifts to naturalis for five bars unfolding eleven pitch classes; the C minor triad at m. 41 on "Ahi crud'amor" drops back to 1♭; A♭ at m. 46 on "dubbose" to 2♭; D♭ at m. 48 on "dolcezze" to 3♭; F♯ at m. 53 on "l'estremo" back to 2♭; C♯ at m. 56 on "pianto" restores 1♭ and the narrator's voice. The systems are assigned to speakers. The PCA begins on F in the alto at m. 22 and completes at the final cadence, omitting pc 10 because Rore keeps the close diatonic. The PDA descends in the tenor, F to E♮ at m. 40, D approached by an E♭ upper neighbor at mm. 41 to 42, C sustained from m. 52, and B♭–A–G from m. 80 into the cadence.
- Lasso, *Prophetiae Sibyllarum*, prologue: naturalis for two bars, then D♯ in the soprano at m. 3 on "Chro" of "Chromatico" lifts to G durus, allowing G♯ and A♮; B♭ at m. 7 returns to naturalis and E♭ at m. 8 continues to mollis for "modulata tenore"; G♯ at m. 10 restores naturalis; D♯ in the alto at m. 14 lifts to durus again; B♭ at m. 19 and E♭ in tenor and bass drop to mollis; G♯ in the alto at m. 21 restores naturalis to the end. The final F♮ to F♯ ascent completes the octave in the last bar. Cadences on G predominate, and the internal scheme is not controlled by the diatony of G mixolydian.
- Lasso, *Sibylla Persica* and *Sibylla Libyca*: both G mixolydian in cantus durus. In *Persica* the PCA opens G–G♯–A in the first three bars, and D♯ at m. 6 on "residebit" enters as the third of a B major triad applied to E. In *Libyca* the PCA runs 0 to 7 twice before completing, F acts as a large-scale lower neighbor to G worked out as a system relationship, and E♭, the allowable flat of the 1♭ system, is reinterpreted as D♯ under a B major triad at m. 26. The recurring G–G♯–A trichord is read as an anticipation of the later developmental working-out of a chromatic trichord across a whole piece.
- Marenzio, *Solo e pensoso*: an accelerated PCA, a complete chromatic ascent in the soprano within fifteen bars, which the chapter says is found in no other composer of the period. Marenzio keeps the modal structure and reverts to diatonic mixolydian for long stretches, where Lasso and Rore care less about modal definition and more about the background system. D♯ at m. 9 on "deserti" lifts to durus; B♭ at m. 17 on "passi" restores naturalis; E♭ at m. 21 on "lenti" drops to mollis, which then holds until m. 65. The four sections balance naturalis, mollis, naturalis, mollis, and the madrigal ends on G with the system still mollis.
- Monteverdi, *Eccomi pronta ai baci* (Book 7, 1619): naturalis throughout, only ten pitch classes, no flats at all, not even the allowable B♭. D mixolydian, so the sharp side of the hexachord. The opening period is D with A as dominant, then the same phrase on G with its dominant, and the whole first period is D–G–a with the a kept minor, so that the fifths above and below D have equal status, which is unlike common practice. A 5–6 sequence from m. 14 leads to a D dorian cadence at m. 19, and the piece's controlling issue is the F♮/F♯ dyad conflict, D dorian against D mixolydian, mapped onto the two lovers and onto Ergasto's name at m. 11. The climax at mm. 52 to 55 juxtaposes the two directly, with F♮ supported by a minor cadential six-four. The close runs a harmonic ostinato on A–D–G.
- Monteverdi, *Ecco vicine o bella Tigre* (Book 7): the same major and minor working, now G mixolydian against G dorian, giving a B♭/B♮ conflict that is composed out across systems, G with B♭ inside naturalis and G with B♮ inside G durus. It opens against expectation with B♭ over a G pedal. Two incomplete PCA rises from 0 to 7, the first omitting G♯ and the second filling it. D♯ at m. 37 on "ma potess'io sequir solingo errante" shifts to G durus and opens E as the next fifth; B♭ at m. 59 reverts to naturalis; D♯ at m. 64 on "Amante" lifts again; B♭ at m. 76 gives the last juxtaposition of the conflict. The piece ends on octave Gs, which the chapter reads as implying B♮ through the overtone series and so resolving the conflict.
- The closing claim: these procedures are neither peculiar to Monteverdi nor to Italy. Schütz shows the same concerns, with a greater interest in large-scale prolongation of structurally significant areas, and the chapter states outright that limitations of space prevent it from exploring those works.

## barnett, "tonal organization in seventeenth-century music theory" (pp. 407–55) · c. 1596 to 1713

**The framing**

- The century is read as confused because it is overshadowed by Renaissance modal theory before it and harmonic tonality after it, and because its treatises appear to mix precepts of major and minor freely with modal teaching and terminology. Theorists of the period contribute to the impression: Barnett opens with Abbatini's downcast poem asking where the beautiful age has gone.
- The resolution: treatises address different purposes, five of them, serving the church singer, training keyboardists to extemporize over a bass, educating the well-rounded musician in counterpoint and modal theory, instructing the rational mind in tuning, and entertaining the curious with speculation and myth.
- The thesis: theorists began speaking in terms of major and minor when their purpose changed from handing down an inherited tradition to creating a practical construct fitting the music around them. Taken as an undifferentiated body, the writings suggest an evolution from modes to keys. No such evolution occurred; one set of concerns replaced another, at different times and to differing degrees.

**Solmization**

- The gamut and the three hexachords are unchanged; mutation rules differ in *cantus durus* (la to re at each A, sol to re at each D ascending; mi to la at each E, re to la at each A descending) and in *cantus mollis* (la to re at each D, sol to re at each G ascending; mi to la at each A, re to la at each D descending). Three hexachords solmizate two systems.
- Reform attempts that went nowhere: Banchieri (1614) adds a seventh syllable in two forms, *ba* from B-fa for b♭ and *bi* from B-mi for b♮, which removes the need for mutation and ties each syllable to one pitch class. Bocedization, attributed to Waelrant and expounded by Calvisius, uses *bo ce di ga lo ma ni* with *pa* for the flat seventh, needs no mutation, solmizates ascent and descent identically, and can be transposed entire from durus to mollis.
- The reform that eventually took hold, also attributed to Waelrant, was adding *si*, and even that gained acceptance slowly on the continent.
- What Guidonian solmization was made to do instead was cope with signatures beyond one flat. Cerreto (1601) illustrates mutation in two flats and two sharps, for singing the *chiavi accidentali*, effectively shifting the whole pattern of syllables against the letters by a whole step. Zarlino had taught transposition forty years earlier so that organists could match the singers' chosen pitch. Penna (1672) covers signatures up to three sharps and three flats, with *do* for *ut*, and tabulates sixty-three combinations of nine clefs and seven signatures.
- The point of that wealth of combinations is that every signature reduces to durus or mollis by an imagined clef substitution. No transposition actually occurs; the singer imagines one to erase the accidentals. The rule outlives Guidonian solmization: late seventeenth-century French theorists, Rousseau and Loulié among them, still teach naturalizing transposed *tons*.

**Modes and the fugal answer**

- Solmization reform provoked no lasting polemic. Competing modal theories did, and theorists in the later half of the century preface their remarks with caveats: Bononcini on the diversity of opinion as to the number of modes and their names, Berardi on the modes being named differently and turned upside down from low to high.
- Eight against twelve. Cerreto devotes a chapter to arguing that there cannot be more than eight, since modes 9 to 12 draw on species already found in the original eight and are therefore *modi commisti*. Bononcini includes a chapter arguing the contrary, and follows Zarlino's twelve modes on six finals, tabulated by species of fifth and fourth.
- Zarlino's four-voice rule of 1558 had the greatest impact: the mode of the tenor determines the mode of the piece, and the bass fills the collateral ambitus because it lies a fourth or fifth below, with soprano matching tenor and alto matching bass.
- Seventeenth-century theorists read that parsing as a rule for imitation, in which a subject's fifths are answered by fourths and the reverse, so that subject and answer between them outline the modal octave. Diruta states it in 1609 for the first mode as *re la* and *re sol* between D, A, and d. Scacchi attacks real answers on these grounds in the 1640s, in the polemic with Siefert whose titles are *Cribrum musicum ad triticum Siferticum* and *Anticribratio musica ad avenam Schachianum*, at bottom a dispute over whether northern Europe would accept modern Italian style. Berardi's *Miscellanea musicale* (1689) supplies four-voice fugues in each of the twelve modes on the pattern.
- A further consequence: rising motion is linked to authentic modes and falling to plagal, which Zarlino implies by his diagrams and Diruta states explicitly and ties to affect. Bononcini calls the authentic lively and the plagal sad, and notes that the rule is optional.
- The terminology flips. For Zarlino *fuga* is intervallically exact imitation at perfect intervals and *imitatione* is inexact imitation at perfect and imperfect intervals. Tevo in 1706 reverses them: fugue occurs at the unison, fourth, fifth, and octave with the rule of following the notes of the mode; imitation occurs at the second, third, sixth, and seventh, where the mode need not be observed. What is now called the tonal fugue was the model of a modal fugue.

**Tuono: mode, psalm tone, church key**

- *Tuono* covers both the eight or twelve modes and the eight psalm tones. The confusion is compounded by a third system based not on finals and ambitus but on the psalm tones themselves.
- The rationale for the church keys is practical. Untransposed, the psalm tones span an eleventh and their reciting tones cover a major sixth. Transposing some of them limits the total range to just over an octave and the reciting tones to a perfect fourth, and Banchieri's set of transpositions needs only signatures of one flat, so that everything is durus or mollis and any tone can follow any other smoothly.
- Barnett tabulates the finals and signatures across seven sources from 1598 to 1706, spanning slightly more than the century. Tone 1 is d with no signature throughout; tone 2 is g with one flat; tone 5 is C; tone 8 is G. The variable one is tone 7, minor on d with one flat in Banchieri and Penna and major on D with two sharps in Nivers, Prinner, and Brossard.
- Titelouze's *Le Magnificat* (1626) explains the change. He treats tone 7 after the principal degrees of its antiphons, finishing on Ut so that the choir can find its intonation, and transposes it a fourth lower for the choir's convenience, which requires the F double sharp he does not write into the signature. The final stays D and the third turns major.
- Banchieri's method for *alternatim* practice, which is what generated the church keys as a compositional resource: for each psalm tone he gives points of imitation, principal degrees, and cadences, and supplies a duo in two voices as a ready-made verset, taking the signature from the transposition and the final from the last note of the tone's principal *differentia*. Some are irregular: tone 5 takes a C final rather than the E the *differentia* implies, and tone 6 carries a flat signature without being transposed, since it needs B♭ to avoid a melodic tritone.
- The conceptual distinction, insisted on by earlier theorists and collapsed by later ones: Zarlino's "stable" *modi* are the psalm tones and canticles with their fixed melodies, and his "varied" *modi* are the modes of antiphons, responsories, introits, and graduals, classifiable by final and ambitus. Maillart's *Les tons* (1610), 380 pages, argues that modes are twelve *harmoniae genera*, categories under which all species fall, and psalm tones are themselves species, specific chants invented for singing particular things.
- Two strategies emerge: reconcile the church keys with modal theory as transposed and untransposed modes (Banchieri, Bononcini), or ignore the distinction (Penna). Tevo lists all three systems without reconciling them, the eight traditional *tuoni*, Glarean's twelve, and the *tuoni delli moderni*, and closes by citing musicians who recognize only two modes, that with the greater third and that with the lesser.
- The church keys become an organizing formula outside psalmody: sonata and dance collections order their finals and signatures by them, and their internal cadence points show the same affinity. Mattheson's twenty-four keys of 1713 come in three groups of eight beginning with the church keys, the second group being whole-step transpositions of the first, so the church keys sit at the center of the system rather than being superseded by it.

**Transposition and the incomplete signature**

- Tonalities themselves are transposed, not only pieces. Nivers distinguishes *voix haultes* from *voix basses* and tabulates the church keys natural and transposed at four levels for different kinds of choir.
- Degli Antonii's versets of 1687 print natural and transposed *tuoni*, either reusing one of the eight church keys at the new level or drawing on transposed tonalities.
- The payoff for reading scores: the so-called incomplete key signatures of the period, c with two flats, A with two sharps, d with none, g with one, e with none, G with none, are properly understood as church keys or their transpositions rather than as defective major and minor signatures.
- Gasparini's *L'armonico pratico al cimbalo* (1708) gives twenty-one keys under "tutti i toni," the last five called enharmonic and chromatic, and orders them in major and minor pairs on alphabetically ascending finals rather than from the church keys.

**French theory**

- Caus (1615) and Parran (1639) adapt Zarlino's twelve modes, including his revised numbering from C, so French modes 1 and 2 are the C pair.
- Caus introduces *note dominante* as a secondary cadence point, borrowing a term that since the late Middle Ages had meant the reciting tone of a psalm tone. In his examples it sits at the top of the fifth in authentic modes and at the top of the fourth in plagal, which is the final itself.
- Parran fixes the *dominante* a fifth above the final and adds the *mediante*, matching Zarlino's *chorda mezzana*. His formulation: comparing *ut mi sol*, *ut* is the final, *mi* the mediant, *sol* the dominant.
- Barnett tabulates the same three degrees across Calvisius (1592), Burmeister (1601), Lippius (1612), and Parran, where the terms *mediante* and *dominante* are the French contribution.
- Parran's examples do not match his text: *mediante* sometimes means any cadence in the middle of a piece and sometimes the third above the final, and in modes 9 and 10 he cadences on E rather than B, probably because cadences on B were discouraged for lack of a natural fifth. Calvisius and Harnisch recommend A or C instead of B in the E and G modes.
- The shift to practical aims removes the modes from French writing. Nivers (1667) still postulates an equivalence between the twelve modes and the eight *tons de l'eglise*. Rousseau (1678), a bass violist, drops the modes, treats the eight *tons* plus transpositions as the whole set of tonalities in use, and names each by its *note finale* plus a third *majeure* or *mineure*. Millet in 1666 had already reduced all singing to two kinds, the cadence of *b. mol* or minor third, born on the degrees called *re*, and the cadence of *b. quarré* or major third, born on *ut*, which prefigures *moll* and *dur*.

**English theory**

- Far from Catholic Europe and its modal traditions, English theorists give the modes almost no attention. Morley (1597) calls them the ancient *modi* and the Eight Tunes and then lists the eight psalm tones as examples, conflating the two. Campion treats *moode*, *key*, and *tone* as synonyms and, after naming the authentic and plagal division, moves to the major and minor triad and never returns. Simpson (1667) mentions the modes out of a sense of duty, not for any great use, and quotes Morley's admission that there is no general rule for keeping the key.
- Solmization differs: the Guidonian gamut and the same letters and syllables, but taught over three signatures rather than two, adding a system of two flats, and reduced by the later seventeenth century to four syllables, *mi fa sol la*, which do not vary with the direction of the melody. Barnett tabulates Morley, Campion, Butler, Playford, and Simpson side by side. Playford's mnemonic: if you would sing true without all blame, call all eights by the same name.
- What English theory does treat carefully is key, understood as a concluding bass note plus its proper cadences, with the injunction to keep the key. Morley calls going out of the key one of the greatest faults that may be committed, and allows internal cadences on the fourth and fifth. Campion allows beginning on the fifth degree and closes on the first five degrees, with the first and then the fifth preeminent.
- Keys are named by the concluding bass note and the quality of the third: Campion's "key of G with B flat" and "key of G with B sharpe," from which the later English usage of sharp keys for major and flat keys for minor descends. Simpson lists fourteen major and minor tonalities on G, A, B♭, C, D, E, and F; Playford's thirteenth edition (1697) lists sixteen, adding B♭ major, E♭ major, and F♯ minor and dropping B♭ minor, and calls A minor and C major the natural keys.
- Butler is the exception, naming tonalities by solmization syllable rather than letter: six "tones" or "airs," *ut*, *re*, *fa*, *sol*, *la*, *pha*, each an octave species, each realizable at three signature levels, so the *ut* tone is G to G with no flats, C to C with one, F to F with two. He calls the *pha* tone, major with a raised fourth, rare, and the *la* tone rarer.
- Essentially free of Catholic modal theory and psalmodic practice, English musicians reached a two-tonality system by the mid-seventeenth century, earlier than all but the most practice-oriented continental writers.

**Thorough bass, the rule of the octave, and the circle**

- Agazzari (1607) gives three reasons for figured bass: the *cantar recitativo* needs no more than a bass, the notation is easy to execute, and it spares the accompanist a library as great as a doctor of law's. He notes that imitative and fugal music is ill served by it, which locates the practice with the *seconda prattica*.
- Three fundamentals of the practice shape the later conception of tonal space: the major or minor triad as the basic harmonizing sonority; generalized scale harmonizations for when figures are missing; and the ability to harmonize at any pitch level.
- The early rules are not yet scale-degree rules. Bianciardi (1607), on a single broadsheet, prescribes the 5/3 above every bass note except those lacking a perfect fifth, B in durus and E in mollis, which take a sixth. Penna (1672) recasts it as a solmization rule: bass notes read as *mi* take a sixth, so E and B in durus, A and E in mollis.
- The full *regola* appears in Bruschi (1711): degrees 1, 4, and 5 take root-position triads, degrees 2, 3, 6, and 7 take sixth chords, with the third over degree 5 always major and the third over degree 4 matching the mode. Bruschi's stated purpose is that the harmonization establishes the *tuono*, and he demonstrates it on a modulating passage. An anonymous Italian manuscript of the end of the century says the same. Locke's *Melothesia* (1673) has a nearly identical scheme in England well before either.
- The circle of fifths is first drawn by Heinichen in 1711, who credits Kuhnau and, behind him, Kircher. The circular arrangement of harmonies is older: Amat's guitar treatise of about 1596 arranges strummed chords around a circle, major on one half and minor on the other, and Penna (1672) works four types of cadence around a *circolo, ò ruota delle cadenze*, noting that the cadences may be formed with either third. Neither claims to be relating keys. Werckmeister connects the closed circle to temperament, and the idea of distinct keys arranged on a circle is not articulated before Heinichen.

**Cadence**

- Theorists earlier in the century concentrate on which degrees may carry cadences in which mode; from mid-century they attend to cadence types. Loulié likens cadences to punctuation without saying which cadence answers to which mark.
- Contrapuntal terminology gives simple cadences (note against note, consonances only) and *composte* or *diminute* cadences (with dissonance and rhythmic figuration), and perfection defined by the resulting consonance, where only the octave and unison count as perfect.
- The continuo player's terminology gives cadences by bass motion, as in Penna's *cadenze del primo ordine* on a bass falling a fifth or rising a fourth, or by the intervals above the bass, as in Berardi's cadence of the seventh and tied sixth and cadence of the fourth resolved to the third. Berardi's *cadenza all'antica* adds a bass to a two-voice progression and produces what would now be called IV–V–I with a perfect authentic cadence.
- Distance from a modern reductive view: Berardi counts two nearly identical cadences as distinct types because the intervals above the bass differ, and La Voye-Mignot groups three cadences as *parfaites* because they converge on an octave, one of which is a Phrygian half cadence in modern terms.
- La Voye-Mignot's three types, *parfaite*, *rompuë*, and *attendante*, are graded by finality and notated in shorter values as they become less conclusive, which gives a clear hierarchy. Masson (1699) reorders the priorities by conjunct against disjunct motion, and still places cadences on the final, mediant, or dominant, a prescription originating with Zarlino, of the two modes recognized in 1699, major and minor.

**The closing specimen**

An anonymous treatise, probably early eighteenth century, states that the student already knows the eight church modes on which the psalms, antiphons, hymns, and introits are based, that modern music has modes distinct from those, and that because these have been altered and mixed the author sets down only two: one authentic with the major third, one plagal with the minor. The conception is clear and the inherited vocabulary nearly obscures it, which is Barnett's case for the period in a single passage.

## where the readings collide

**Burnett against Powers**

- Burnett and Nitzberg treat the three-hexachord system as a real pre-compositional harmonic background that composers thought in and worked out across whole pieces, and they read the theorists' avoidance of D♯ in durus and G♯ in mollis as evidence that the eleven-pitch-class area was a live constraint.
- Powers argues that modality is not an a priori property of sixteenth-century polyphony, that the modal assignments visible in the sources are classifications after the fact, and that the observable facts about a piece are its system, cleffing, and final and nothing more.
- The two are compatible if the levels are kept apart: Powers describes what marks a piece from outside, Burnett claims what governs its chromatic behavior from inside. They do disagree on whether the hexachordal system is the operative background, and a student who reads them as making the same kind of claim will produce a muddle.
- Judd sides with Powers. Barnett's non-evolution thesis cuts against Burnett's framing of the sixteenth century as the origin of eleven-pitch-class tonality, though Barnett's evidence is seventeenth-century theory rather than sixteenth-century practice.

**Cantus durus and cantus mollis, said three ways**

- Cohen: two scales into which the gamut divides by about 1500, defined by hexachord content, with *cantus fictus* at two flats.
- Powers: one of three markers of a tonal type, notated ♮ and ♭, purely a signature fact.
- Burnett: the signature that identifies the initial system, with the system itself extending through transpositions the signature does not show. At p. 52 he equates cantus durus with his own naturalis system, against his naturalis, mollis, durus taxonomy, in which durus is the G system reached by D♯. The directed questions use the taxonomy and carry no gloss.
- Barnett: the two scale systems that all seventeenth-century signatures reduce to for a singer, by imagined clef substitution.

Four compatible uses, one term. Worth putting on the board in Week 2.

**Convergence at Paper 1**

Powers's table of *Lagrime di San Pietro* gives madrigals 1 to 4 as ♮-c1-D representing mode 1, and 5 to 8 as ♭-c1-G representing mode 2. Paper 1 takes no. I, from the first block: naturalis in Burnett's terms, cantus durus in Powers's. Two frameworks, one observation, and different claims about what the observation means. The Monteverdi and the Schütz supply the mollis side of the comparison.

**Fuller, Schubert, and the analytical questions**

Both refuse the assumption that treatise precepts are composition rules. Fuller makes the point against Riemann, Schubert against the expectation that improvised training should resemble finished pieces. That refusal is the counterweight to Burnett, who reads Vicentino, Banchieri, Harnisch, and Kircher as evidence of what composers held in mind. Students who take the theorists at face value in one direction should be asked to reconcile it with the other.
