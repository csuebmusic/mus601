# unit 1: reading synthesis

Instructor-facing. What each assigned reading argues, what in it is teachable, and where the readings meet the unit's repertoire.

## burnett and nitzberg, chapter 3, "the modal gamut in the sixteenth century" (pp. 39–88)

The analytical spine of the unit.

Guido's gamut, by the sixteenth century, functions as a harmonic system of three hexachords: subdominant, central, dominant. The central hexachord, reordered as fifths, supplies every structurally available root and every cadential goal in the system, independent of mode. Naturalis reordered is F–C–G–d–a–e. The system comprises eleven pitch classes: the six of the central hexachord, the flat from the subdominant hexachord, one natural or sharp from the dominant hexachord, and three sharps forming the major sixth at cadences. The twelfth is the missing pitch, D♯/E♭ in naturalis and G♯/A♭ in mollis. Its appearance spelled as a flat transposes the system down a fifth; spelled as an augmented second, up a fifth. That transposition is what Burnett and Nitzberg call modulation.

Four criteria determine the governing system: the signature, the absence of the missing pitch, the presence of the missing pitch spelled as a minor third above the central hexachord's root, and the presence of the missing pitch spelled as an augmented second above that root.

Theoretical support: Vicentino's four-voice cadence tables in *L'antica musica* (1555), Banchieri's *L'organo suonarino* (1605) and *Cartella musicale* (1614), Harnisch's *Artis musicae delineatio* (1608), and Kircher's "Systema universale" in *Musurgia universalis* (1650). All avoid D♯ in cantus durus and G♯ in cantus mollis. Kircher and Harnisch both state that no good cadence can be made in the hypophrygian mode, which is the D♯ prohibition stated as a rule of taste. Lowinsky observed in 1946, in a footnote, that each of Lasso's Penitential Psalms unfolds exactly eleven tones; the chapter takes up the observation and generalizes it.

Chafe's four-hexachord framework is engaged and rejected: it yields twelve pitch classes per system, which makes the system untransposable and forces Chafe to abandon hexachordal terminology midway through his book.

Two linear constructs. The PCA (Primary Chromatic Array) is the ascending chromatic filling of the tonic octave across a whole composition. The PDA (Primary Diatonic Array) is the concurrent diatonic descent to the final in the tenor. Both derive from the 6–8 cadential progression of Renaissance counterpoint.

Analyses in the chapter, by work:

| work | system behavior |
|---|---|
| Josquin, *Dominus regnavit* | 1♭ throughout, no system modulation, E♭/E♮ dyad conflict, conflicting signatures resolved to a single 1♭ system |

## cohen, "notes, scales, and modes in the earlier middle ages" (pp. 307–63)

The prehistory of every term the unit uses.

Two traditions fuse in the Carolingian period: the cantus tradition (eight *toni* adapted from the Byzantine oktoechos, classificatory, matching antiphons to psalm tones) and the harmonics tradition (note, interval, and scale, from Boethius and other late Roman transmitters). Aurelian's *Musica disciplina* represents the pre-theoretical state: melodies described in qualitative, grammatically derived language, with no final, no interval, and no background scale.

Hucbald and the *Enchiriadis* treatises introduce the final as a specific note within a scale, and with it mode as an intervallic pattern relative to that final. The *Enchiriadis* system is periodic at the fifth rather than the octave, which makes every note one of four modal qualities (protus, deuterus, tritus, tetrardus) and produces the treatises' idiosyncratic Daseian notation.

*Alia musica* imports Boethius's octave species and the ethnic Greek names. Its author equates Boethius's transpositional *tonoi* with the church modes and reverses the ordering of the species while keeping the name-to-number pairing. That misreading is the origin of the modal labels Dorian through Hypomixolydian.

Pseudo-Odo's *Dialogus* gives the letter notation still in use, the two forms of b, and eight modal "forms." Guido gives the hexachord, solmization, mutation, the Hand, and staff notation, and the doctrine of *modi vocum* and affinities: the modal quality of D, E, and F recurs a fifth above at a, ♮, and c, which is the origin of the cofinals. G has no such affinity, which is why tetrardus is the recurring anomaly.

The postscript is the bridge to Judd and Powers. By about 1500 the gamut has split into *cantus durus* (no signature, natural and hard hexachords) and *cantus mollis* (one flat, natural and soft), with *cantus fictus* at two flats.

## judd, "renaissance modal theory" (pp. 364–406)

A case study of mid-century Venice rather than a survey, framed by Powers's question "Is Mode Real?"

The structural problem: modal theory originates in monophony, and Aaron in 1525 is the first to state the difficulty of applying it to polyphony. Judd separates the ecclesiastical tradition (final, ambitus, beginnings) from pseudo-classical species theory (Marchetto: species of fifth plus species of fourth, solmized re–la and mi–mi).

The narrative spine is a single motet. Zarlino composed an eight-mode *Canticum canticorum* cycle in the 1540s, one chapter of the Song of Songs per mode, and abandoned it after chapter 3. *Ego veni in hortum meum* was composed as a representation of mode 5. It is labeled Ionian in the 1549 print, cited as mode 11 in the 1558 *Istitutioni*, and becomes mode 1 after the 1571 renumbering in the *Dimostrationi*. One motet, five classifications, depending on whether an eight- or twelve-mode system is invoked and which numbering applies.

Also in the chapter: Vanneus's *Recanetum* (1533) as the ordinary state of modal knowledge in the 1530s and 40s, with his table of modal affects; the treatise typology (cantorinus, Lateinschule textbook, composer's manual, humanist speculativa, patron's book); the print-culture argument that the modal theory of Aaron, Glarean, and Zarlino is unimaginable without printed repertory to cite; Glarean's derivation of twelve modes from the division of the octave rather than the addition of fifth and fourth; and the Meier–Dahlhaus–Powers dispute over whether the authentic-plagal distinction is real in polyphony. Judd's own position: Aaron's criteria fit the Josquin repertory badly, and she proposes three "ut, re, mi" tonalities instead.

## powers, "tonal types and modal categories in renaissance polyphony" (pp. 428–70)

The article the other three orbit.

A *tonal type* is the observable triple of system (cantus durus ♮ or cantus mollis ♭), ambitus (high clefs or chiavette, notated g2, against low clefs, notated c1), and the pitch class of the final sonority. A *mode* is a category internal to sixteenth-century musical culture. Tonal types frequently represent modes. They are never identical with them. The formulation: a mode is always a tonal type, but a tonal type is not always a mode. Powers frames the distinction as etic against emic.

Evidence is tabulated from modally ordered collections: Rore's five-voice madrigals (1542), Susato's two- and three-voice chansons (1544) and his *Liber ecclesiasticarum cantionum* series (from 1553), Lasso's Penitential Psalms, Lasso's Munich motets of 1562, Lasso's duos (1577), Lasso's *Lagrime di San Pietro* (1595), Lasso's Graz *Cantiones sacrae* (1594), Palestrina's *Vergine* cycle (1581), and Palestrina's spiritual madrigals (1594).

The one-to-many correspondences carry the argument. Mode 1 is represented by at least three distinct tonal types across those collections, and the authentic-plagal contrast of modes 1 and 2 is realized four different ways (by ambitus, by system and final, by ambitus in reverse, by final alone). The type ♮-g2-A stands for mode 3 in Susato in the 1540s and for mode 1 in Palestrina in the 1580s. Palestrina used ♮-g2-C constantly, including for the Pope Marcellus Mass, and never once in a modal collection. Gardano reprinted Lasso's modally ordered 1562 Munich collection in Venice the same year and destroyed the modal order, evidently not recognizing it.

The closing argument: any evolutionary account of modality becoming tonality is a non-question, because the two are different orders of thing. A sixteenth-century tonal type can be ancestral to an eighteenth-century key; ♭-g2-G and ♭-c1-G stand behind G minor, ♮-g2-A and ♮-c1-A behind A minor. Emic modality plays no part in that descent.

## barnett, "tonal organization in seventeenth-century music theory" (pp. 407–55)

Continues the unit past its end date and holds the same anti-evolutionary line as Powers on different grounds: theorists spoke in modes or in keys according to whether their aim was transmitting an inherited tradition or describing the music in front of them. One set of concerns replaced another.

Solmization: hexachordal mutation in durus and mollis, the seventeenth-century reform attempts (Banchieri's added *ba* and *bi*, Calvisius's bocedization), and the practice by which singers reduced any signature to durus or mollis by imagining a clef substitution rather than performing a transposition.

Modal theory and fugal answer: Zarlino's rule that the tenor carries the mode, and that the bass fills the collateral ambitus, becomes Diruta's and Scacchi's rule that the subject's fifth is answered by the fourth so that subject and answer together span the modal octave. What is now called the tonal answer was the modally correct answer. Tevo by 1706 has reversed Zarlino's definitions of *fuga* and *imitatione*.

The church keys carry the most weight. Eight tonalities arise from psalm tone transpositions that keep the reciting tones within a fourth. Barnett tabulates them across Banchieri (1614), Nivers (1667), Penna (1672), Prinner (1677), Brossard (1703), and Martín y Coll (1706), against an anonymous 1598 set. Modes are abstract categories; psalm tones are actual melodies. Zarlino's distinction between "stable" and "varied" modi and Maillart's *Les tons* (1610) both insist on the difference, and later theorists collapse it, since *tuono* names both. Mattheson's twenty-four keys of 1713 are built outward from the eight church keys as a core, with the second group of eight derived by whole-step transposition.

Add the figured-bass strand: scale harmonizations from Bianciardi and Penna to Bruschi's *regola* and the rule of the octave, and the circle of fifths, first drawn by Heinichen in 1711 though anticipated in Amat's guitar treatise of about 1596 and in Penna's *circolo delle cadenze*. Seventeenth-century tonal space is the convergence of separate strands, not a metamorphosis.

## fuller, "organum – discantus – contrapunctus in the middle ages" (pp. 477–502)

Three phases distinguished by their governing terms.

*Organum* in the *Enchiriadis* treatises is singing in symphoniae, controlled by the fourth and fifth, with the tritone prohibition forcing the organal voice to sustain a tetrardus pitch and converge at unison. Guido's *Micrologus* describes the same two practices as strict (durus) and flexible (mollis) diaphony, with the occursus at phrase ends.

*Discantus* under musica mensurabilis brings rhythm and its notation to the center. John of Garland admits every interval within the octave, ranked in a hierarchy of perfect, medial, and imperfect concord and discord, and requires concords at the beginnings of perfections. Alongside the learned treatises runs the practical interval-succession tradition (Klangschritt-Lehre), stock discant responses to each cantus interval.

*Contrapunctus* from the 1330s is note-against-note by definition and is conceived as the foundation beneath an elaborated surface. Imperfect concords (major and minor third, major sixth) enter as structural components, which requires new constraints: begin and end on a perfect concord, and inflect imperfect intervals toward the closest perfect sequel. Marchetto founds the Italian line on that closest-approach principle; Prosdocimo and Ugolino carry it forward.

Vernacular practices: the Italian *gradus* system, which is hexachord-zone counterpoint, the English sights (quatreble, treble, mean, countertenor, countir) and faburden, Guilielmus monachus transmitting English practice to Italy, and Tinctoris's distinction between counterpoint sung *super librum* and *res facta*. Fuller closes by refusing Riemann's premise that these are universally valid composition rules; they are elementary production training, local and stratified.

## schubert, "counterpoint pedagogy in the renaissance" (pp. 503–33)

The most immediately usable for seminar exercises.

Thesis: the challenge for a Renaissance composer was not correct voice leading but the elaboration of a *soggetto*, which may be a melody, a duo, or an entire polyphonic composition. Two dozen treatises in four families: the fifteenth century (Ugolino, Tinctoris, Ramis, Gaffurio), Zarlino and his followers (Pontio, Tigrini, Morley, Artusi, Cerreto), the Spanish line (Lusitano, Sancta María, Montaños, Cerone), and the early seventeenth-century Italians (Banchieri, Angleria, Rodio, Brunelli, Chiodino, Campion).

Simple counterpoint: Ugolino begins from the tenor's direction and the first vertical interval; Tinctoris begins from the pair of vertical intervals. Ramis corrects Ugolino by the closest-approach principle. Modal consequences of counterpoint appear scattered: Tinctoris on commixture caused by ficta, Tinctoris's fifth rule against perfections producing *distonatio*, Ramis rewriting a line that emphasizes Phrygian degrees over a Dorian cantus firmus.

Florid counterpoint and the proto-species presentations of Montaños and Cerone, who teach diminution by first setting "guides" in note-against-note texture and filling them afterward.

*Contraponto fugato*: repetition of a short motive against a cantus firmus, licensed by Zarlino under three conditions (sequence, different vertical intervals against it, rhythmic variation). Lusitano uses it to give form to a cantus-firmus piece, producing simple ABA structures.

Invertible counterpoint at the octave, tenth, and twelfth, with the constraints stated as prohibitions (no strong-beat sixths for the twelfth, no parallel thirds or sixths for the tenth), and Chiodino's successive transpositional stages.

Sancta María's paired duos are the fullest account of four-part imitative openings: a *passo* presented in a duo, with or without fuga, tightly overlapped (travada) or loose (suelta), and the second duo entering against one of the three steps of the first duo's cadential suspension. Montaños supplies "commonplaces," memorizable openings, many using invertible counterpoint without comment.

Four-part sonority: Zarlino's chord tables, Sancta María's classes ranked by the distance between the outer voices, and Campion's rule producing a chain of 5/3 sonorities. Bermudo's line that counterpoint is the father of melody closes the chapter.

## where the readings collide

Burnett and Nitzberg treat the three-hexachord system as a real pre-compositional harmonic background that composers thought in and worked out across whole pieces. Powers argues that modality is not a pre-compositional universal in sixteenth-century polyphony, and Judd follows him. Barnett's non-evolution thesis cuts against the framing of the sixteenth century as the origin of eleven-pitch-class tonality.

The two positions can be held together by keeping the levels apart: Powers describes what marks a piece from outside, Burnett claims what governs its chromatic behavior from inside. They disagree about whether hexachordal system is the operative background, not about the observable facts.

The strongest convergence falls on Paper 1. Powers's table of *Lagrime di San Pietro* gives madrigals 1 to 4 as ♮-c1-D and 5 to 8 as ♭-c1-G. Paper 1 pairs numbers I and V, one from each block: naturalis against mollis in Burnett's terms, cantus durus against cantus mollis in Powers's. Two frameworks, one observation, different claims about what it means.

## open items

The directed questions ask students to place each piece in naturalis, mollis, or durus. That is Burnett's system-level taxonomy, in which durus is the G system reached by D♯. In Powers, Judd, and Barnett, cantus durus and cantus mollis are the only two signature systems and naturalis is not a term. Students reading Powers first will read the question as offering three signature categories where only two exist. Decision needed: gloss the term on the unit page, or restate the question in Burnett's terms.

Paper 1's three works have no analytical model in the assigned reading. Burnett analyzes no Schütz, no *Lagrime*, and nothing from Book 8. Powers supplies the *Lagrime* tonal-type table and nothing on the other two. Decision needed: leave the gap as scaffolding removal, or add a handout applying the three-hexachord method to *Fili mi, Absalon* and *Perché te'n fuggi*.
