# unit 1: the Italian madrigal

Instructor-facing. What the project's readings give on the madrigal, and a working sheet for "Da le belle contrade d'oriente."

## what the readings cover

The project files carry the madrigal as a theoretical problem, not as a genre history. Burnett and Nitzberg give the system analysis and the chromatic practice of the 1540s onward; Judd gives the modal and tonal-type question as it bears on secular polyphony; Powers gives tonal types from Rore's first book. Nothing in the files narrates the genre itself: no Verdelot, Arcadelt, or Willaert, no account of the poetry, the print culture, or the shift from the first generation to the second. If you want that in the seminar it has to come from elsewhere, and I can find scholarship for it.

## the madrigal in the theoretical readings

- **the chromatic turn of the 1540s.** Composers found cadencing only on the structurally significant pitch classes of the pure mode too constricting for the emotions of Petrarchan and Tasso-esque poetry. The expanded chromatic vocabulary that answered it outgrew diatonic modality, and what results is what Burnett and Nitzberg call extended modality: more chromatic pitch classes arriving faster than before, with the three-hexachord system taking on a harmonic dimension of its own and displacing an ever more weakly defined modality. Burnett and Nitzberg ch. 3.
- **transposition of the system becomes structural.** Moving the system up or down a fifth inside a piece was uncommon before the 1540s, *Absalon* being one of the exceptions, and becomes ordinary with the northern Italian madrigalists. Burnett and Nitzberg ch. 3.
- **mode and the secular repertory.** Whether modal theory applies to secular genres as well as sacred, and whether mode is an a priori compositional construct or an a posteriori analytical deduction, are two of the tensions Judd names in sixteenth-century modal thought. Judd 364–65.
- **ordering by mode.** The first seventeen madrigals of Rore's first book (1542) follow the order of the eight modes. Powers's tonal types are built on that book together with the Susato anthologies and works by Lasso and Palestrina, and the ordered Gardano print of Rore's motets in 1545 suggests the arrangement came from the composer. Judd 379, with Table 12.2 after Powers, "Tonal Types," 444.
- **the seconda prattica claim.** Giulio Cesare Monteverdi's "Dichiaratione," appended to Claudio's *Scherzi musicali* (1607), names Rore (1516–1565) as the originator of the seconda prattica and names madrigals of his that approximate the new practice. "Da le belle contrade," from the Fifth Book of 1566, is among them. Burnett and Nitzberg ch. 3.

## "Da le belle contrade d'oriente"

Edition in the files: Rore, *Opera omnia*, ed. Bernhard Meier, vol. 5. Also in Davison and Apel, *Historical Anthology of Music*, vol. 1, no. 131, pp. 142–43. The text is an Italian sonnet by an unknown poet; the translation Burnett and Nitzberg use is from Atlas, *Renaissance Music* (1998), 635, and Atlas analyzes the piece there.

Set in an F mode in cantus mollis, a 1♭ three-hexachord system. Three sections: the outer two diatonic and within the 1♭ system, the middle chromatic and shifting systems repeatedly. Both outer sections unfold eleven pitch classes omitting G♯ and A♭, the missing pitch of a 1♭ system.

The flat and sharp signs are indistinguishable in the extracted text of Figure 3.6, so the spellings above are reconstructed from the four criteria: the minor third above the central root moves the system down a fifth, the augmented second moves it up. Check them against your copy.

The narrative: the chromatic middle section starts at m. 25 with the change from narrator to female lover, but the system holds until m. 36, at "Che sarà qui di me," where the G♯ shifts the 1♭ system up a fifth into naturalis. Those five bars, mm. 36–40, unfold eleven pitch classes omitting only E♭/D♯. The C minor triad at m. 41 on "Ahi crud'amor" drops the system back to one flat and starts a steady descent through increasingly flat hexachords: A♭ at m. 46 on "dubbiose," D♭ as the root of its own major triad at m. 48 on "dolcezze." Rore also distinguishes the two lovers by giving their speeches different harmonic areas; the turn to mollis at m. 41 is Venus's sorrow.

### the two arrays

The chromatic ascent, which Burnett and Nitzberg call the Primary Chromatic Array, unfolds the tonic octave upward across the whole piece, often coinciding with the system shifts.

| pc | pitch | where |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | F | alto, m. 22 |
| 1 | F♯ | alto, m. 25 |
| 2 | G | m. 25 |
| 3 | G♯ | soprano, m. 36, over an E major triad, shifting 1♭ to naturalis |
| 4 | A | m. 37 |
| 5 | B♭ | m. 38 |
| 6 | B♮ | tenor, last beat of m. 51, raised third of an applied dominant to C |
| 7 | C | m. 52 |
| 8 | C♯ | alto, m. 56, returning 2♭ to 1♭ |
| 9 | D | soon after |
| 10 | E♭ | omitted, the last section being kept diatonic |
| 11 | E♮ | the cadence at m. 73 |
| 0 | F | the cadence at m. 73, repeated as an authentic cadence in the last two bars |

Against it the tenor carries a diatonic descent to the final, the Primary Diatonic Array. The tenor starts on the fifth degree and reaches F in m. 2. The first part is diatonic with many cadences on F, so nothing descends further. In m. 38 the tenor's F is shifted up an octave and descends in m. 40 to E♮. D is approached by an upper neighbor E♭ in mm. 41–42 and arrives in m. 43. The descent from D to C is confirmed by a subordinate cadence on a C major triad in m. 52, and C is held at a higher structural level until mm. 79–80, where the tenor descends B♭–A–G. The G forms a major sixth with the soprano's E♮ and both resolve to the F octave: ti–do above, re–do below.

The cadence model behind that is the 6–8 progression: the upper voice ascends to the final through a leading tone forming a major sixth with the tenor, and the two resolve outward in stepwise motion to the octave. Sixteenth-century counterpoint here is the interaction of two structural voices, usually soprano and tenor, though alto and bass or bass and soprano can carry it.
