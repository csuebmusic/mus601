description

Examine the evolution of tonality from the Renaissance to Romantic eras. Develop research proficiency and analytical skills through comparative analysis of selected works. Explore the rich history of music styles and their impact.

learning outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course students will be able to:

  1. demonstrate proficiency in musicological research methodologies, including the ability to critically evaluate scholarly sources, formulate research questions, and utilize appropriate bibliographic tools.
  2. develop effective communication skills through written analysis. They will be able to articulate complex musical concepts, tonal structures, and historical contexts coherently and concisely in a scholarly format adhering to proper citation styles.
  3. showcase a deep understanding of theoretical concepts learned throughout the course. This includes the ability to identify and analyze tonal structures, forms, and stylistic elements in musical compositions from the Renaissance to the Romantic era.
  4. develop critical thinking skills in interpreting and evaluating musical works. They will demonstrate the ability to connect historical and theoretical knowledge to their analyses, offering insightful perspectives on the birth and death of tonality within the broader context of music history.

Each paper asks you to get to the heart of a piece and say something true about how it works: find what organizes the music, and show it. A short frame to place the works is fine, a paragraph at most, and everything after that is analysis, grounded in the score and in what you hear.

the method

Bring the analytical apparatus of the seminar to the score and follow the evidence.

  • Read harmony as a system. Place each passage in the hexachord framework, track the system and its modulations, and name the pitch that forces each change and where it lands.
  • Read form as a tonal process. Segment it (ritornello function, sonata action) and treat the structural events (the medial caesura, the EEC and ESC, a refrain, a mode switch) as things that happen for a reason rather than labels to affix.
  • Follow the specific pitches and measures that carry the argument. A dyad conflict, a missing pitch: cite it by measure and show what it does.
  • On the comparative papers, set the two designs side by side. The difference between them is the point.

the standard, and what goes beyond it

A paper at the standard answers the directed questions with an argument rather than a description. It states what organizes the music, ties every claim to the measures that show it, and puts its sources where they bear on that claim. On a comparative paper it names the difference between the works rather than describing each in turn.

A paper beyond the standard reaches something the questions did not hand it: a claim about how the music works that was not obvious before the paper, and evidence that makes it hold. It knows which one or two things matter most in the movement and spends its space there.

Three habits keep a paper below the standard: the label with no consequence, naming a chord and moving on; narration, walking through the piece measure by measure without an argument to organize the walk; and the claim the score does not support.

sources

Every paper takes an authoritative edition of each work you analyze, plus secondary sources. The table above gives the number of each.

  • The edition. An authoritative edition names its editor and states which sources it was prepared from. Analyze from the score packet handed out in seminar, and separately find an authoritative edition of each work on Alexander Street, a score database you reach through the University Library.
  • The secondary sources. Scholarship on the works or their composers: books, articles in scholarly journals, dissertations, signed entries in reference works.
  • The research tools. These arrive one at a time and accumulate. Paper 1 uses RILM to find what has been written and JSTOR to read it. Paper 2 adds Oxford Music Online. Paper 3 adds ProQuest. On Papers 4 and 5 you choose among them.
  • The citations. Chicago style, notes-bibliography system: a numbered footnote wherever you draw on a source, and a bibliography at the end listing everything the paper cites. Editions and secondary sources both appear in both places.

Source work sets out each tool, how to search it, and the citation forms.

the specifications

  • 6 to 8 pages, double-spaced, 12-point serif font, one-inch margins, counting musical examples, footnotes, and bibliography.
  • The directed questions for that paper addressed.
  • An authoritative edition of each work analyzed, found on Alexander Street and cited.
  • The paper's secondary sources, found through the databases this course introduces.
  • Every source cited where it bears on the analysis, in Chicago notes-bibliography, with numbered footnotes and a bibliography at the end.
  • Musical examples and analytical graphs, numbered and referred to by measure.
  • A measure number for every passage discussed.

use of AI

Generative AI (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, and similar tools) is permitted in this seminar for two narrow purposes: checking grammar and mechanics in writing you have already drafted, and checking your citations and bibliography against Chicago style. Everything else is off limits. The tools may not generate analysis, ideas, arguments, or prose. The analytical thinking and the writing are yours.

Every use of AI, including the two permitted ones, must be disclosed. Undisclosed use, or use beyond grammar and citation-format checking, will be treated as a violation of the CSUEB Academic Dishonesty Policy and may be subject to disciplinary action. This is the seminar's Generative AI use policy under CSUEB policy 25-26 CIC 1. For CSU guidance on the ethical and responsible use of AI, see the CSU AI resource site.

These tools misformat Chicago citations and invent sources that do not exist. You are responsible for every citation in your paper. Treat AI output as a prompt to recheck your work, and verify each citation against the source in your hands and against Chicago, notes-bibliography.

disclosing AI use

Disclose the permitted uses with a short acknowledgment rather than a formal source citation. Place it as an unnumbered note at the foot of the last page, before the bibliography. Name the tool and its developer, the date, and what you used it for:

AI acknowledgment: I used ChatGPT (OpenAI) on October 14, 2026, to check grammar and mechanics and to verify Chicago citation formatting. All analysis, argument, and prose are my own.

Chicago cites AI-generated text in a note, with the prompt first, then the model, its developer, the date, and a link to the exact output where one exists:

1. Response to “your exact prompt,” ChatGPT-4o, OpenAI, October 14, 2026, https://chatgpt.com/share/…

Chicago treats an AI exchange as a personal communication. It stays out of the bibliography unless an archived, publicly accessible link to the exact output exists; where one does, the entry is arranged under the developer (Chicago Manual of Style, 18th ed., 14.112).

directed questions

wk 09 · Oct 13

paper 2 due

Compare the first movements of the two concertos below. How do both composers use ritornello form in these movements? In particular, how do they harmonically organize the tonality of A major into the re-ordered fifths of its hexachord?

  • Antonio Vivaldi: Concerto in A major for viola d'amore, strings, and B.C., RV 396
  • J. S. Bach: Concerto in A major for harpsichord and strings, BWV 1055

directed questions

  • Segment each ritornello theme by function (Vordersatz, Fortspinnung, Epilog). Where does each ritornello return, in what key, and in what form?
  • Which pitches carry the A-major system changes, and where does each composer leave and re-enter the home system?
  • What organizes each movement: the tutti/solo alternation, or the presence and absence of ritornello material? Test both against the solos.
  • What is the essential difference in how the two composers use the ritornello?

Comparison analysis, first movements only:

  • W. A. Mozart: Symphony no. 41 in C, “Jupiter,” K.551 (1788)

directed questions

wk 14 · Nov 17

paper 4 due

Beethoven, Violin Sonata no. 9 in A, “Kreutzer,” Op. 47 (1802–03), first movement.

directed questions

  • Take the major/minor mode switch as the organizing gesture. Which dyad conflicts carry it, and where is each first heard?
  • Follow the system battle (3# against 0, and the flat side). Where do the dyad conflicts also act as system modulations?
  • Why a minor v in the second harmonic area? What would a stable major V or bIII cost the movement?
  • Name the single idea the movement serves, and show how the coda settles the conflicts it opened.

Schubert, String Quartet no. 14 in D minor, “Death and the Maiden,” D. 810 (1824), first movement.

directed questions
